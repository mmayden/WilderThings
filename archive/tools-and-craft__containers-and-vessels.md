# Improvised Containers and Vessels

> How to make waterproof containers, cooking vessels, and storage bags from bark, clay, hide, and plant materials when you have no manufactured gear.

## At a Glance
- Birch bark containers can be folded in minutes and hold water immediately.
- Hot-rock boiling lets you cook in any container that holds water — even a hole in the ground.
- Clay pots require drying and firing but provide durable, heat-resistant vessels for long-term camps.
- Animal stomachs and bladders make effective water bags with no processing beyond cleaning.
- Waterproofing with pine pitch or tree resin seals seams and extends container life.

---

## Bark Containers

### Birch Bark

Birch bark is the premier natural container material — flexible, waterproof, and easy to work.

1. Select a living or recently dead white birch (paper birch) with smooth, intact bark.
2. Score a rectangle on the trunk with a knife. Make the vertical cuts first, then connect them at top and bottom.
3. Peel the bark gently away from the trunk. Work slowly to keep the sheet intact.
4. The inner (light-colored) side becomes the outside of your container — it is more waterproof.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Harvesting bark from a living tree can kill it. In a true survival situation this is acceptable. In practice, use bark from fallen or dead trees when available.

**Folded Box Container:**

1. Lay the bark sheet flat, inner side up.
2. Score fold lines to form a rectangular base with four sides.
3. Fold the sides up and pinch the corners into pleats.
4. Secure corners with wooden pegs, thorns, or cordage lashing.
5. Seal seams with heated pine pitch (see Waterproofing below).

**Quick Cone Cup:**

1. Roll a square piece of bark into a cone shape.
2. Secure the overlap with a thorn or wooden peg.
3. Holds water for immediate drinking. Not durable but takes under a minute.

### Elm and Tulip Poplar Bark

Where birch is unavailable, elm and tulip poplar inner bark form serviceable containers.

1. Harvest inner bark in large sheets during spring when sap flows (bark peels most easily).
2. Shape while fresh and flexible — these barks stiffen as they dry.
3. Sew seams with cordage or root lashings rather than folding.

## Woven Baskets

Baskets require more time but produce versatile, durable containers.

### Simple Plaited Basket

1. Gather long, flexible strips — cattail leaves, willow bark, or split cedar bark work well.
2. Lay 6-8 strips parallel as the warp (base strips).
3. Weave cross strips (weft) over and under alternately.
4. Build up the base as a flat mat, then bend the warp strips upward and continue weaving to form sides.
5. Finish the rim by folding warp ends over and tucking them into the weave.

!!! tip
    Soak dry materials in water for 30 minutes before weaving. Pliable fibers weave tighter and resist cracking.

### Coiled Basket

1. Bundle a core of grass, pine needles, or thin roots into a rope approximately 0.5 inches (1.3 cm) thick.
2. Coil the bundle into a flat spiral for the base.
3. Stitch each coil to the previous one using a bone needle and thin cordage or split root.
4. Build sides by stacking coils vertically, stitching each row.

## Clay Pots

Clay pots withstand direct fire and are essential for long-term camp cooking.

### Finding Clay

- Look along riverbanks, stream cuts, and eroded hillsides.
- Test by rolling a small piece between your palms into a pencil-thin coil. If it bends without cracking, the clay is workable.
- Remove stones, roots, and debris. Knead thoroughly until uniform.

### Pinch Method (Small Vessels)

1. Form a ball of clay the size of your fist.
2. Press your thumb into the center, leaving 0.5 inches (1.3 cm) of wall thickness at the base.
3. Pinch and rotate, thinning the walls evenly to approximately 0.25-0.5 inches (0.6-1.3 cm).
4. Shape the rim by smoothing with wet fingers.

### Coil Method (Larger Vessels)

1. Roll clay into ropes approximately 0.75 inches (2 cm) thick.
2. Form a flat base disk by coiling a rope into a spiral.
3. Stack additional coils on the base, pressing each coil firmly into the one below.
4. Smooth the interior and exterior with wet fingers or a flat stick to seal the coils together.
5. Build walls gradually — if they slump, let the lower section firm up before adding more.

### Drying and Firing

1. Dry the pot slowly in shade for 2-3 days. Rapid drying causes cracking.
2. Once fully dry, place the pot near a small fire for 1-2 hours to drive out remaining moisture.
3. Gradually move the pot closer to the fire over 30 minutes.
4. Build a fire around and over the pot. Maintain high heat for 1-2 hours.
5. Let the pot cool slowly in the ash. Do not quench with water.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A pot that is not fully dry will crack or explode when fired. Be patient with the drying stage. If in doubt, wait another day.

## Animal-Based Containers

### Stomach and Bladder Water Bags

1. Remove the stomach or bladder from a harvested animal.
2. Turn it inside out and scrape clean of all contents and membrane.
3. Rinse thoroughly with clean water.
4. Tie off one end with cordage. Fill through the other end.
5. A deer stomach holds approximately 1-2 gallons (4-8 liters).

### Hide Bags

1. Scrape a section of fresh hide clean of fat and flesh.
2. Cut into a circular or rectangular shape.
3. Punch holes around the perimeter with a bone awl.
4. Thread cordage through the holes and draw it closed like a drawstring bag.
5. Use for carrying water (short-term), gathering food, or storing supplies.

## Bamboo and Gourd Vessels

=== "Tropical/Subtropical Regions"
    **Bamboo:** Cut a section of bamboo just below a node (the solid joint). The node forms a natural bottom. Cut the top open above the next node for a lidded container, or leave open for a cup. Bamboo holds water, withstands moderate heat, and lasts weeks.

=== "Temperate Regions"
    **Gourds:** Dry mature gourds for several weeks until the seeds rattle inside. Cut an opening, remove seeds and pith, and clean the interior. Dried gourd walls are hard and waterproof. Use for water storage, bowls, and scoops.

## Hot-Rock Boiling

When your container cannot go on direct flame (bark, hide, woven basket, or ground depression), use hot rocks to boil water.

1. Heat fist-sized stones in a fire for 30-45 minutes until they glow or are extremely hot.
2. Use wooden fire tongs or two sticks as chopsticks to transfer the rocks.
3. Drop hot rocks into the water-filled container.
4. The water reaches boiling temperature within 1-3 minutes depending on volume and rock temperature.
5. Remove spent rocks and add fresh hot ones to maintain the boil.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never use river rocks or stones from wet areas — trapped moisture expands when heated and can cause the rock to explode violently. Use dry rocks from high ground.

!!! note
    Even a hole dug in clay soil and lined with a hide or bark sheet becomes a boiling vessel with this method.

## Waterproofing with Pitch and Resin

Pine pitch seals seams, repairs cracks, and waterproofs porous containers.

### Making Pine Pitch Glue

1. Collect hardened resin blobs from pine, spruce, or fir trees (look for sap oozing from wounds in the bark).
2. Melt the resin in a small container near the fire — not over direct flame, as it ignites easily.
3. Mix melted resin with crushed charcoal (roughly 3 parts resin to 1 part charcoal) to add strength and prevent brittleness.
4. Apply warm pitch to seams with a small stick.
5. The mixture hardens as it cools and is waterproof.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Pine resin is highly flammable when liquid. Keep it away from open flame during melting. Heat gently near coals.

## Common Mistakes
- Using bark from the wrong side (inner bark faces outward for birch containers — it is the waterproof layer).
- Firing clay pots too fast (cracks and explosions from trapped moisture).
- Using river rocks for hot-rock boiling (explosion risk from trapped water).
- Not sealing bark container seams (leaks render the container useless for water storage).
- Making clay walls too thin (less than 0.25 inches / 0.6 cm) — they crack during firing.
- Forgetting to clean animal stomachs thoroughly (bacteria contaminate stored water).
- Weaving with dry, brittle fibers (soak materials before weaving for flexibility and tight construction).
