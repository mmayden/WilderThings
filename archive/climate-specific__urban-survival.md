# Urban Survival and Disaster Response

> How to survive in a city after a major disaster when infrastructure fails and normal services are unavailable.

## At a Glance

- The first 72 hours after a disaster are the most dangerous and the least supported. Expect no outside help for at least 3 days.
- Shelter-in-place is usually safer than evacuation unless your building is structurally compromised, on fire, or in a flood zone.
- Know how to shut off gas, water, and electricity in your building before you need to.
- Communication infrastructure fails first. Have a pre-arranged meeting point and contact plan with your household.
- Sanitation without plumbing becomes a health crisis within 48 hours. Plan for waste disposal immediately.

## Building Collapse Response

### If You Are Trapped

1. Cover your mouth and nose with fabric to filter dust.
2. Do not shout continuously — it exhausts you and you inhale dust. Tap on pipes or walls in groups of three. Use a whistle if available.
3. Do not light a match or lighter. Gas leaks are common after structural collapse.
4. Do not move heavy debris above you. Shifting rubble can cause secondary collapse.
5. If you can move, work toward light or fresh air. Move small debris carefully without disturbing load-bearing elements.

### If You Are Searching

- Call out and listen for tapping every 30 seconds. Silence the area during listening periods.
- Mark searched areas with a standard X: slash when entering, complete the X when finished. Note date, team ID, hazards found, and number of victims on the four quadrants.
- Do not enter structures with visible lean, cracked foundations, or sagging floors without shoring.
- Gas smell = evacuate the area. Do not use electric switches or open flames.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Aftershocks follow earthquakes for days or weeks. A damaged building that is standing now can collapse in an aftershock. Limit time inside damaged structures.

## Utility Shutoffs

Learn these locations in your building before any disaster occurs.

### Gas

- Locate the main shutoff valve at the gas meter (usually outside the building).
- Use a wrench or the gas shutoff tool to turn the valve 90 degrees (quarter turn) so the handle is perpendicular to the pipe.
- Shut off gas if you smell rotten eggs, hear hissing, or see visible damage to gas lines.
- Once shut off, do not turn gas back on yourself. Only the utility company should restore gas service.

### Water

- Locate the main shutoff valve (typically near the water meter or where the supply line enters the building).
- Turn clockwise to close.
- Shut off water if pipes are broken to prevent flooding and preserve the water already in your system.
- Your hot water heater contains 30-80 gallons (114-300 liters) of drinkable water. Drain from the bottom valve.

### Electricity

- Locate the main breaker in your electrical panel.
- Switch to OFF if you suspect wiring damage, see sparks, or smell burning insulation.
- Never touch electrical panels while standing in water.
- If power lines are down outside, stay 35 ft (10 m) away from any downed line or anything it contacts.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Downed power lines can energize the ground around them. Assume all downed lines are live. Shuffle away with small steps keeping feet together — do not stride or run.

## Urban Water Sources

When municipal water fails, secure water immediately. You need 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day minimum.

=== "Inside Your Building"
    Hot water heater (30-80 gallons / 114-300 liters). Toilet tanks (not bowls) — water is clean if no chemical treatment tablets have been added. Ice in freezers. Canned food liquids.

=== "Outside Sources"
    Rainwater collection from rooftops and tarps. Water heaters in adjacent buildings.
    Streams and ponds (purify before drinking). **Swimming pools and hot tubs — for
    washing and flushing, not for drinking.**

=== "Purification"
    Boil for 1 minute. Or add plain unscented household bleach per gallon (3.8 L) — 8
    drops if the label says 6%, 6 drops if it says 8.25% — and wait 30 minutes; double
    both if the water is cloudy or very cold. Or use a portable filter rated for bacteria
    and protozoa. Bleach does not kill cryptosporidium; boiling and filtration do. Full
    detail in [Water Purification](../water/purification.md#household-bleach-sodium-hypochlorite).

!!! warning "CAUTION: pool water is for washing, not drinking"
    A swimming pool looks like the largest water source in the neighbourhood and it is
    the wrong one to drink. The problem is not germs — it is the cyanuric acid, algaecides
    and stabilisers already dissolved in it, and boiling, bleaching, or filtering removes
    none of them. Chemical treatment makes it worse by adding more.

    Boiling actually concentrates them, since the water leaves and the chemicals stay.
    Only distillation separates them, and that is slow enough to be irrelevant at pool
    volumes.

    Hot tubs are worse again: far higher chemical loads in far less water. Use pool water
    for flushing toilets, washing, and firefighting, which frees your drinkable water for
    drinking. That is a real and substantial gain — it is just not a drinking source.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not drink water from radiators, boilers, or industrial systems. They contain antifreeze, rust inhibitors, and other toxic chemicals.

## Shelter-in-Place vs. Evacuation

### Shelter-in-Place When

- Your building is structurally sound.
- Authorities advise sheltering.
- Hazardous materials are released outside (seal windows and doors, turn off HVAC).
- Roads are impassable or dangerous.
- You have adequate supplies for 72+ hours.

### Evacuate When

- Your building is structurally damaged (visible cracks in foundation, leaning walls, sagging floors).
- Fire is approaching or in the building.
- Flooding is rising toward your level.
- Authorities order mandatory evacuation.
- Hazardous materials are released inside or immediately adjacent.

!!! tip
    Keep a go-bag packed at all times: 3 days of water and food, medications, copies of ID and insurance documents, cash (ATMs fail without power), first aid kit, flashlight, radio, and phone charger.

## Communication When Infrastructure Fails

Cell towers fail or become overloaded within minutes of a major disaster. Plan ahead.

- **Pre-arranged meeting point:** Establish two locations — one near your home, one outside your neighborhood — where household members gather if separated.
- **Out-of-area contact:** Designate a friend or relative in another city as a message relay. Local calls fail; long-distance calls often work.
- **Text messages:** SMS uses less bandwidth than voice calls and often gets through when calls cannot.
- **Battery radio:** AM/FM radio receives emergency broadcasts when all other communication fails. NOAA Weather Radio (162.4-162.55 MHz) provides official emergency information.
- **Written messages:** Leave notes at your home and meeting points stating your status, direction of travel, and date/time.

## Urban Fire Safety

Post-disaster fires are common. Gas leaks, downed power lines, and damaged chimneys ignite fires that no fire department responds to.

- Extinguish small fires immediately if you can do so safely. Use a fire extinguisher (PASS method: Pull pin, Aim low, Squeeze handle, Sweep side to side).
- Close doors behind you when evacuating a burning building. Closed doors slow fire spread dramatically.
- Stay low in smoke — breathable air is closest to the floor. Crawl on hands and knees.
- Feel doors before opening. If the door or handle is hot, do not open it — fire is on the other side.
- Designate two exits from every room. Practice using them.

## Self-Defense Considerations

!!! note
    Post-disaster environments can create security threats as normal social structures are disrupted. Most people help each other. Some do not.

- Travel in groups. Solo individuals are more vulnerable.
- Avoid displaying supplies, food, or water visibly when moving through areas.
- Maintain situational awareness. Avoid confrontation when possible — walk away from threats.
- Secure your shelter. Lock doors, barricade ground-floor windows, establish watch shifts at night.
- Know your neighbors before a disaster. Communities that know each other cooperate more effectively and recover faster.

## Scavenging Safely

- Enter only structures that are visually stable. Look for cracked foundations, leaning walls, and sagging rooflines before entering.
- Wear sturdy shoes (not sandals), gloves, and eye protection. Broken glass and nails are everywhere.
- Move in pairs. One person enters while the other watches from outside.
- Do not hoard. Take what you need. Leave supplies for others.
- Prioritize water, food, first aid supplies, batteries, and tools.
- Avoid pharmacies and hospitals — these are high-conflict areas after disasters.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER: taking property is a legal matter"
    Taking goods from a damaged or abandoned building is theft in ordinary
    circumstances, and remains so in most circumstances after a disaster. Most legal
    systems recognise a defence of necessity when life is genuinely at risk and no
    alternative exists — the same principle that applies to taking game out of season.

    In practice that means: take what is needed to keep people alive and nothing beyond
    it, leave what others will need, note what you took and from where, and report it to
    the authorities afterwards. Emergency services and aid distribution exist and reaching
    them is almost always the better option. The further you are from immediate danger to
    life, the less this defence holds.

## Group Organization

### Immediate Priorities

1. Account for all group members. Assess injuries.
2. Assign roles: water procurement, security/watch, medical care, communication, food preparation.
3. Inventory all supplies across the group. Pool and ration collectively.
4. Establish a decision-making process. One leader with group input prevents paralysis and conflict.
5. Set a daily schedule: work periods, rest periods, watch rotations.

### Sustaining the Group

- Share information openly. Rumors and uncertainty cause panic.
- Maintain hygiene and sanitation standards. Disease from poor sanitation kills more disaster survivors than the disaster itself.
- Assign meaningful tasks to everyone, including children. Idle people spiral into despair.
- Check on elderly, disabled, and isolated neighbors regularly.

## Sanitation Without Plumbing

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Human waste becomes a disease vector within hours. Cholera, dysentery, hepatitis, and parasitic infections spread through fecal contamination of water and food.

### Emergency Toilet

1. Line a bucket or toilet bowl with a heavy-duty garbage bag.
2. After each use, add a handful of cat litter, sawdust, wood ash, or dirt to absorb moisture and reduce odor.
3. Seal the bag when two-thirds full. Store sealed bags outside and away from water sources.
4. Bury waste 200 ft (60 m) from any water source and 6 inches (15 cm) deep if no waste collection is available.

### Hygiene

- Wash hands with soap and water (or hand sanitizer) after every toilet use and before handling food. This single practice prevents the majority of post-disaster illness.
- Keep cooking and eating areas separate from waste areas.
- Dispose of all garbage in sealed bags away from living areas.
- If water is scarce, prioritize hand washing over bathing.

## Urban Navigation When Landmarks Are Destroyed

- Street signs may be down. Count blocks and use compass directions (north/south/east/west) instead of street names.
- Carry a paper map of your city. Electronic maps require power and cell signal.
- Major infrastructure — highways, rail lines, rivers, bridges — persists through most disasters. Navigate by these features.
- Mark your route with chalk, spray paint, or tape so you can retrace it.
- Avoid shortcuts through damaged areas. Stick to main roads that are more likely cleared.

!!! tip
    Before a disaster, identify three routes from your home to your workplace, your children's school, and your designated meeting point. When one route is blocked, use an alternate without hesitation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Not shutting off gas.** A single gas leak can destroy an entire building. If you smell gas, shut off the main valve and leave.
- **Drinking untreated water.** Municipal water safety cannot be assumed after infrastructure damage. Treat all water.
- **Sheltering in a damaged building.** Aftershocks and secondary collapse kill people who stay in structurally compromised buildings. Evaluate and leave if in doubt.
- **Waiting for help.** Professional rescue may take 72+ hours. Self-rescue and neighbor-to-neighbor assistance save the most lives in the first 3 days.
- **No communication plan.** Cell networks fail. Without a pre-arranged meeting point and out-of-area contact, families cannot reunite.
- **Ignoring sanitation.** Disease outbreaks from poor sanitation cause more casualties than many disasters themselves. Manage waste from day one.
- **Going solo.** Groups are safer, more efficient, and more resilient than individuals. Cooperate with neighbors.
