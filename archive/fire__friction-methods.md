# Friction Fire Methods

> Generate fire using muscle power and wood-on-wood friction — the oldest and most universally available ignition technique.

## At a Glance

- Bow drill is the most reliable friction method; expect 2-4 weeks of practice to achieve consistent results.
- Practise this before you need it. A first attempt during an emergency will most likely fail and will cost hours, calories, water, and hand skin.
- Hand drill requires less equipment but far more endurance and technique.
- Wood selection is the single most important factor — wrong wood means no fire regardless of skill.
- All friction methods require wood moisture content below 15%; 8-12% is ideal.
- Friction fire produces a coal, not a flame. Transfer the coal to a tinder bundle and blow it to flame.

!!! warning "CAUTION: this is a skill to learn before you need it, not during"
    The numbers in this guide are honest and worth reading as a decision, not as
    encouragement. Bow drill takes 15-30 hours of practice to become reliable; hand drill
    takes 30-60. Someone attempting either for the first time in an emergency will most
    likely fail, and will spend hours of daylight, a large number of calories, a
    meaningful amount of water, and the skin on their hands finding that out.

    **If you have a lighter, matches, or a ferro rod, use them** — see
    [Spark and Lens Methods](spark-methods.md). Carry two. A ferro rod weighs almost
    nothing, works wet, and lasts for thousands of strikes.

    If you have no ignition source and have never made friction fire, weigh those hours
    honestly against shelter and insulation, which need no practice and no equipment.
    Fire is not always the priority it feels like — see
    [Shelter Principles](../shelter/shelter-principles.md).

    None of this argues against the skill. It argues for learning it at home, where
    failing costs an afternoon.

## Bow Drill

**Difficulty:** Moderate
**Time to master:** 15-30 hours of practice (2-4 weeks)
**Best conditions:** Dry climate, humidity below 60%, dead standing softwood available
**Wood moisture requirement:** Below 15%

### Components

The bow drill system has five components. Each must be correct for the system to work.

#### Fireboard (Hearthboard)

- Flat piece of dry, dead softwood.
- Dimensions: 12-18 in (30-45 cm) long, 2-4 in (5-10 cm) wide, 1/2-3/4 in (1.2-2 cm) thick.
- Flatten the top and bottom surfaces. The board must not rock.
- Carve a shallow starter depression 1/4 in (6 mm) deep on the top surface, one spindle-width from the edge.
- After burning in the spindle (see technique), cut a notch: a wedge-shaped cut from the edge of the board to the center of the depression, roughly 1/8 of the circle (45-degree pie slice). The notch must reach just past the center of the depression — this is where the hot dust collects to form a coal.

**Wood selection for fireboard:**
| Wood | Rating | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| Cottonwood | Excellent | The gold standard; soft, consistent grain |
| Willow | Excellent | Widely available; use dead, dry branches |
| Cedar (western red) | Excellent | Aromatic; easy to identify |
| Basswood (linden) | Excellent | Very soft; burns in quickly |
| Aspen / poplar | Very good | Similar to cottonwood |
| Clematis (vine) | Good | Common in temperate forests; use dried dead vine |
| Yucca (dried stalk) | Good | Desert environments |
| Sycamore | Fair | Harder; requires more effort |

#### Spindle

- Straight, dry, dead softwood stick.
- Length: 8-12 in (20-30 cm). Diameter: 1/2-3/4 in (1.2-2 cm) — approximately thumb thickness.
- Round cross-section. Carve or abrade off any bark.
- Bottom end: rounded to a blunt point (not sharp — you want friction, not drilling).
- Top end: tapered to a narrower point (reduces friction against the bearing block).
- Use the same wood species as the fireboard, or a matched pair (see below).

**Matched pairs (spindle / fireboard):**
- Cottonwood / cottonwood (best)
- Willow / willow
- Cedar / cedar
- Willow / cottonwood
- Cottonwood / willow

!!! tip
    Do not mix hardwood and softwood. A hardwood spindle on a softwood board drills a hole. A softwood spindle on a hardwood board glazes and polishes without producing dust.

#### Bearing Block (Handhold / Socket)

- Holds the top of the spindle in place under downward pressure.
- Material: hardwood, bone, antler, smooth stone with a natural depression, shell, or a knot of hardwood.
- The bearing block must create low friction against the spindle top. Lubricate the socket with tree resin, earwax, animal fat, crushed green leaves, or nose oil.
- Carve or find a depression that fits the tapered top of the spindle.
- Size: fits comfortably in one hand.

#### Bow

- A sturdy, slightly curved branch. Gentle curve — not a tight arc.
- Length: approximately arm's length, 24-30 in (60-75 cm).
- Diameter: 3/4-1 in (2-2.5 cm) — strong enough to resist bending under string tension.
- Slight flex is acceptable; excessive flex wastes energy.
- Notch or drill holes at each end to anchor the string.

#### String (Cordage)

The string is the most critical and often most difficult component to source in a true survival scenario.

**Ideal string materials:**
- Paracord (best — always carry some)
- Bankline / tarred nylon twine
- Rawhide lace (leather bootlace)
- Natural cordage: twisted dogbane, stinging nettle, inner bark of basswood, yucca fiber (must be strong and resist abrasion)
- Shoelaces

**String requirements:**
- Strong enough to resist snapping under tension and friction.
- Rough enough to grip the spindle without slipping.
- Length: slightly longer than the bow, tied with enough slack to wrap once around the spindle.

Tie the string to one end of the bow with a fixed knot. At the other end, use an adjustable knot (timber hitch or bowline with a toggle) so you can tension the string as it stretches.

### Technique: Step-by-Step

1. **Prepare all components.** Fireboard, spindle, bearing block, bow with strung cordage, tinder bundle, kindling, and fuel wood — all ready before you start.
2. **Wrap the spindle.** Twist the string once around the spindle. The spindle sits on the outside of the string (away from the bow). The string should be taut enough to grip the spindle when the bow is held horizontally.
3. **Position the fireboard.** Place the fireboard on a flat, stable surface. Put a leaf, thin piece of bark, or flat chip underneath the notch area — this catches the coal and allows you to transport it to the tinder bundle.
4. **Kneel.** Place your left foot (if right-handed) firmly on the fireboard, close to the depression but not covering the notch. Your arch pins the board flat.
5. **Set the spindle.** Place the rounded bottom of the spindle in the fireboard depression. Place the bearing block on top of the spindle.
6. **Lock your wrist.** Wrap your left hand around the bearing block and brace your left wrist or forearm against your left shin. This creates a rigid column: shoulder, arm, wrist, shin, foot, board — all locked. The spindle must remain perfectly vertical.
7. **Begin sawing.** With your right hand, draw the bow back and forth in smooth, full-length strokes. Use the entire length of the string. Start with moderate speed and light downward pressure.
8. **Burn in.** The first 30-60 seconds seats the spindle into the board, creating a round socket. Stop. Carve the notch now (if not already cut).
9. **Resume drilling.** Apply moderate speed and moderate downward pressure. Produce brown dust. The dust collects in the notch.
10. **Increase tempo.** When you see consistent smoke rising from the notch (not just the socket), increase speed and pressure. Drive hard for 10-15 final strokes.
11. **Stop and observe.** Carefully lift the spindle. Look at the dust pile in the notch. If it continues to smoke on its own after you stop drilling, you have a coal. A coal glows faintly and produces a thin, steady wisp of smoke.
12. **Transfer the coal.** Gently tap the fireboard to release the coal onto the leaf or bark chip. Carefully transfer the coal into the center of your prepared tinder bundle.
13. **Blow to flame.** Hold the tinder bundle loosely. Blow gently and steadily at the base of the coal. Increase force as the smoke intensifies. The bundle will ignite — this moment happens suddenly. Be ready to place it into your fire lay.

!!! tip
    Smoke from the socket (where the spindle meets the board) is not the same as smoke from the notch (where the dust collects). Socket smoke means friction is working. Notch smoke means a coal is forming. Do not stop at socket smoke — push through to notch smoke.

### Troubleshooting

#### Too Much Smoke but No Coal

- **Notch too shallow or too narrow.** The notch must reach past center of the socket. Widen it to a full 1/8-of-circle wedge.
- **Wood too hard.** Switch to softer species.
- **Insufficient downward pressure.** Press harder during the final burst.
- **Dust is being blown away.** Shield the notch from wind.

#### Spindle Won't Stay in the Socket

- **Socket not deep enough.** Burn it in longer before cutting the notch.
- **Spindle bottom too pointed.** Blunt it — you want a rounded nub, not a spike.
- **Bearing block shifting.** Lock your wrist against your shin. Eliminate all wobble.
- **Fireboard rocking.** Flatten the bottom of the board. Place it on level ground.

#### String Slipping on the Spindle

- **String too loose.** Tighten at the adjustable end.
- **Spindle too smooth.** Score light grooves around the spindle with a knife or rough rock.
- **String too slick.** Rub the string with tree resin or pine pitch. Use a rougher cordage material.
- **Too much downward pressure.** Reduce pressure slightly — excessive load increases slippage.

#### Spindle Squeaking

- **Wood is glazed.** Rough up both the spindle tip and socket with a rough stone or sand.
- **Too little pressure.** Increase downward force.
- **Wrong wood pairing.** Ensure both woods are similar hardness.

## Hand Drill

**Difficulty:** Hard
**Time to master:** 30-60 hours of practice (4-8 weeks)
**Best conditions:** Hot, dry climate; humidity below 40%; thin, straight softwood stalks available
**Wood moisture requirement:** Below 12%

### Construction

#### Fireboard

Same specifications as bow drill fireboard but can be thinner — 3/8-1/2 in (1-1.2 cm) thick. The same wood species apply. The notch is identical.

#### Spindle

- Straight, dry, dead stalk or stick.
- Length: 18-24 in (45-60 cm) — longer than a bow drill spindle for leverage.
- Diameter: 3/8-1/2 in (1-1.2 cm) — thinner than a bow drill spindle.
- Must be as straight as possible. Remove bumps and nodes.
- The bottom end is blunted. The top can be left natural.

**Best hand drill spindle materials:**
- Mullein stalk (excellent — pithy center)
- Cattail stalk
- Elderberry (remove pith)
- Yucca flower stalk
- Horseweed / marestail stalk
- Clematis vine (dried)

The fireboard and spindle do not need to be the same species for hand drill, but both must be soft, dry, and dead.

### Technique

1. **Position identically to bow drill.** Kneel, foot on board, tinder and coal-catcher beneath the notch.
2. **Place spindle in socket.** Hold the spindle between both palms, hands near the top.
3. **Apply downward pressure and spin.** Press palms together with the spindle between them. Rub hands back and forth rapidly, as if rolling the spindle. Hands naturally migrate downward as you press and spin.
4. **Float back to the top.** When hands reach the bottom of the spindle, quickly reposition them to the top without losing spindle contact with the board. This is the hardest part of the technique. Some practitioners use a "fly-back" motion; others simply move fast.
5. **Maintain continuous pressure and speed.** Do not stop. The dust must accumulate heat in the notch. Any pause lets the dust cool.
6. **Push through to coal.** When smoke pours from the notch, give 10-15 more seconds of maximum effort.
7. **Transfer coal to tinder bundle** exactly as with bow drill.

### Endurance Tips

- **Spit on your palms** or rub them with fine sand for better grip (but not so much sand that it tears skin).
- **Use a thumb loop.** Tie a short cord loop around the top of the spindle. Hook your thumbs through the loop to prevent hands from sliding down.
- **Use a mouth hold.** Place the top of the spindle against a socket held in your teeth,
  with a piece of flat hardwood between the spindle and your teeth. This adds downward
  pressure and stops the spindle walking out.

    !!! warning "CAUTION"
        Never take the spindle directly against your teeth — the load fractures enamel,
        and a spindle that slips travels toward your palate and throat. Use a carved
        wooden bearing piece thick enough that the spindle cannot punch through it, and
        stop the moment it starts to wear thin. A broken tooth or a puncture to the roof
        of the mouth is a poor trade for a fire you could have made another way.
- **Alternate with a partner.** One person holds the board and feeds the coal; the other drills. Switch before exhaustion.
- **Build calluses.** Practice frequently in short sessions. Blisters make the hand drill
  impossible — and in the field they are an open wound on the hands you need for
  everything else. Leave blisters intact rather than draining them, and see
  [Wound Infection](../medical/wound-infection.md).
- **Work fast, not long.** A coal from hand drill should come in 30-90 seconds of full-effort drilling if conditions are right. If nothing happens in 3 minutes, stop and reassess materials.

## Fire Plow

**Difficulty:** Hard
**Time to master:** 10-20 hours of practice
**Best conditions:** Tropical and subtropical environments; soft, dry wood (hibiscus, sotol)
**Wood moisture requirement:** Below 15%

### Construction

#### Fireboard (Plow Board)

- Flat piece of dry softwood, 18-24 in (45-60 cm) long, 3-4 in (7-10 cm) wide, 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) thick.
- Carve a groove down the center of the flat face, approximately 6-8 in (15-20 cm) long, 1/2 in (1.2 cm) wide, 1/4 in (6 mm) deep.

**Preferred woods:** Hibiscus, sotol, willow, cottonwood, clematis.

#### Plow Stick

- A stick of the same or slightly harder wood, 12-18 in (30-45 cm) long, 1-1.5 in (2.5-4 cm) diameter.
- Carve one end to a blunt chisel or rounded point that fits the groove.

### Technique

1. Brace the fireboard at an angle — prop one end on a log or rock, or hold it between your knees on the ground.
2. Grip the plow stick with both hands.
3. Push the plow stick back and forth along the groove with heavy downward pressure and rapid, short strokes.
4. Concentrate effort at the far end of the groove — this is where dust accumulates.
5. Hot dust piles up at the end of the groove. Continue until it smokes freely.
6. Tip the coal out onto a leaf. Transfer to tinder bundle.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Fire plow requires enormous sustained effort. It is significantly more exhausting than bow drill. Reserve energy and hydration before attempting.

## Fire Saw (Bamboo Method)

**Difficulty:** Moderate to Hard
**Time to master:** 5-15 hours of practice
**Best conditions:** Tropical environments where bamboo grows; dry season
**Wood moisture requirement:** Below 15%

### Construction

#### Base Piece

- Split a section of dry, dead bamboo lengthwise into a half-round trough.
- Length: 18-24 in (45-60 cm).
- Place it on the ground, hollow side up.
- Score a thin slit across the outside (convex surface) at the midpoint, perpendicular to the length. Do not cut all the way through — just weaken the wall.

#### Saw Piece

- A second piece of dry bamboo, split to have a sharp edge along one side.
- Length: 18-24 in (45-60 cm).
- Alternatively, use a thin strip of bamboo, a piece of rattan, or any material with a hard, abrasion-resistant edge.

#### Tinder

- Place a small bundle of dry tinder (coconut fiber, dried grass, bamboo shavings) inside the trough directly beneath the scored slit.

### Technique

1. Pin the base piece to the ground with your foot or knees, hollow side up. Ensure the tinder is beneath the scored slit.
2. Place the sharp edge of the saw piece across the scored slit on the outside of the base.
3. Saw vigorously back and forth across the slit. Apply firm downward pressure.
4. Hot dust falls through the slit onto the tinder inside the trough.
5. Continue until the tinder begins to smoke, then gently blow to flame.

**Variations:**
- **Bamboo fire thong:** Use a strip of dry rattan or bamboo pulled back and forth through a notch cut in a bamboo base. The friction heats the notch and ignites tinder placed beneath it.
- **Two-piece split:** Split a bamboo section partway, wedge tinder into the split, and saw across the split with a second piece.

## Wood Selection: Universal Principles

Regardless of which friction method you choose, the following principles apply:

### The Thumbnail Test

Press your thumbnail into the wood surface. If it leaves a clear dent, the wood is soft enough for friction fire. If it barely marks or does not dent, the wood is too hard.

### Moisture Assessment

- **Snap test:** Break a small piece. A clean snap with a sharp sound means dry. A bend, flex, or stringy break means too wet.
- **Sound test:** Strike two pieces together. Dry wood produces a sharp crack or clack. Wet wood produces a dull thud.
- **Weight test:** Dry wood is noticeably lighter than wet wood of the same species and size.
- **Visual test:** Dry wood is often grayish or lighter in color than green wood. No green layer under the bark.

### Where to Find Dry Wood

- Dead standing trees and branches (not touching the ground).
- The underside of leaning dead trees.
- Dead branches still attached to living trees.
- Inside dead, fallen logs (split them open).
- Driftwood above the high-water line (often very dry, but may be hardwood).

### Woods to Avoid

- Green / living wood — too much moisture, will not produce a coal.
- Resinous wood (pine, spruce) — resin gums up the contact surfaces and glazes rather than producing dust. Exception: some experienced practitioners use certain pines, but this is not recommended for beginners.
- Hardwoods (oak, maple, hickory) — require extreme effort and rarely produce a coal via friction.
- Rotten / punky wood — crumbles instead of producing coherent hot dust.

## Common Mistakes

- Selecting wood from the ground instead of dead standing sources — ground wood absorbs moisture.
- Cutting the fireboard notch too small — dust has no room to accumulate and form a coal.
- Using a dull or overly sharp spindle tip — too sharp drills a hole; too flat squeaks and glazes.
- Failing to lock the wrist during bow drill — the spindle wobbles, wastes energy, and wears unevenly.
- Using short, fast bow strokes instead of long, full-length strokes — short strokes concentrate wear in the middle of the string and reduce efficiency.
- Neglecting to prepare the tinder bundle in advance — the coal dies while you scramble.
- Stopping too soon — the dust pile must smoke on its own for at least 5 seconds before you have a viable coal.
- Practicing with commercial lumber — kiln-dried construction lumber can work for learning, but it does not replicate field conditions.
- **Attempting friction fire first in a real emergency.** It is the slowest, least reliable ignition method available to an untrained person. Exhaust every other option, and weigh the hours against shelter.
- **Tearing your hands open.** Hand drill blisters cost you every other task in camp. Stop before the skin breaks; swap methods or partners.
