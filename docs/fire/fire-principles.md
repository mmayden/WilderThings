# Fire Principles

> Master the fundamentals of fire creation, management, and safety to survive any situation.

## At a Glance

- Fire requires three elements simultaneously: heat, fuel, and oxygen (the fire triangle).
- Build fire progressively: tinder catches spark, kindling builds flame, fuel wood sustains it.
- Choose your fire lay based on purpose: warmth, cooking, signaling, or stealth.
- Prepare the site before striking the first spark — clear ground, block wind, plan containment.
- Always extinguish fully and practice leave-no-trace when possible.

## The Fire Triangle

Every fire depends on three elements existing together in sufficient quantity:

### Heat

Heat provides the ignition energy. Sources include friction, sparks, solar concentration, electrical arc, and chemical reaction. Remove heat and fire dies — this is how water extinguishes flame. The ignition temperature for most wood is 400-500 F (200-260 C). Maintain heat by sheltering flame from wind during the critical early stages.

### Fuel

Fuel is any combustible material. In survival fire-making, fuel progresses through three stages (see below). Fuel must be dry enough to ignite — wood above 25% moisture content resists ignition. Dead, standing wood is almost always drier than wood on the ground. Strip wet outer bark to reach dry inner wood.

### Oxygen

Air contains roughly 21% oxygen. Fire consumes oxygen rapidly. A smothered fire dies. Ensure airflow reaches the base of the fire at all times. Blow gently on coals to increase oxygen supply. Pack fuel loosely enough for air to circulate between pieces.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    In enclosed spaces (caves, snow shelters, vehicles), fire depletes oxygen and produces carbon monoxide. Ensure ventilation or risk asphyxiation.

## Fuel Stages: Tinder, Kindling, Fuel Wood

### Tinder

Tinder is any material that ignites from a spark or small flame. It burns hot and fast. Gather at least two handfuls before attempting ignition.

**Natural tinder:**
- Dry grass (dead, golden-colored)
- Birch bark (contains flammable oils even when damp)
- Cattail fluff
- Cedar bark (shredded fine)
- Pine resin / fatwood shavings
- Dried moss
- Seed down (thistle, milkweed, dandelion)
- Bird nests (abandoned)
- Fungus: cramp ball (*Daldinia concentrica*), true tinder fungus (*Fomes fomentarius*)

**Prepared / manufactured tinder:**
- Char cloth
- Cotton balls with petroleum jelly
- Dryer lint
- Steel wool (fine grade, 0000)
- Commercial fire starters
- Waxed jute twine

Prepare a tinder bundle by forming a loose bird-nest shape approximately 4 in (10 cm) across. Place the finest, most combustible material in the center where the coal or spark will land.

### Kindling

Kindling bridges the gap between tinder and fuel wood. Use dry sticks and twigs from pencil-lead thickness up to pencil thickness — roughly 1/8 in to 3/8 in (3-10 mm) diameter. Gather at least two large handfuls. Dead twigs that snap cleanly are dry enough. Twigs that bend without breaking are too green or wet.

**Good kindling sources:**
- Dead twigs still attached to trees (off the ground, drier)
- Split wood shavings / feather sticks
- Small pieces of fatwood
- Dry pine needles in bundles
- Thin bark strips

### Fuel Wood

Fuel wood sustains the fire. Graduate sizes from thumb-thickness to wrist-thickness to arm-thickness. Start with pieces 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) diameter and work up. Split wood ignites faster than round wood because it exposes dry interior grain.

**Fuel wood by burn characteristics:**
- **Fast / hot burn:** Pine, spruce, fir (softwoods) — good for starting, poor for coals.
- **Slow / steady burn:** Oak, hickory, maple, ash (hardwoods) — excellent coals, long duration.
- **Smoky / resinous:** Green wood, damp wood, pine with heavy sap — avoid except for signaling.

Gather three times more fuel wood than you think you need. A night fire in cold conditions consumes a pile roughly 4 ft long x 2 ft high x 2 ft wide (1.2 x 0.6 x 0.6 m) of mixed wood.

## Fire Lay Types

### Teepee (Tipi)

Lean kindling sticks together to form a cone shape over the tinder bundle. Leave a gap on the windward side for lighting and airflow. As kindling ignites, add progressively larger sticks maintaining the cone shape.

**When to use:** General purpose. Best for quick ignition and fast heat. Collapses inward as it burns, feeding itself. Ideal for boiling water or lighting larger structures.

**Drawbacks:** Burns fast, requires frequent feeding. Collapses unpredictably — poor for cooking with a pot or grill.

### Log Cabin (Crib)

Stack pairs of sticks in alternating perpendicular layers, like building a small cabin. Place tinder bundle in the center at the base. Each layer uses progressively larger wood.

**When to use:** Cooking — provides a stable, flat top surface. Produces a uniform coal bed. Good when you need sustained, even heat. Also effective as the outer structure around an inner teepee for signal fires.

**Drawbacks:** Requires more wood and more preparation time. Slower to ignite than a teepee.

### Lean-To

Drive a long stick into the ground at a 30-degree angle, pointing into the wind. Lean kindling against the windward side of this stick. Place tinder underneath at the base.

**When to use:** Windy conditions. The lean-to structure shields the tinder and flame from wind. Works well when you have only one ignition attempt.

**Drawbacks:** One-directional heat output. Burns unevenly.

### Star Fire (Radial Fire)

Arrange 4-6 logs like spokes of a wheel with the ends meeting in the center. Light a small teepee or tinder bundle at the hub. Push logs inward as the ends burn.

**When to use:** Long-duration fire with minimal attention. Excellent for overnight warmth. Uses large logs efficiently — no splitting or breaking required. Common in dry grassland and desert environments.

**Drawbacks:** Low flame height. Slow to establish. Requires periodic pushing of logs inward.

### Dakota Hole (Dakota Fire Pit)

Dig two holes roughly 12 in (30 cm) apart. The fire hole is 12 in (30 cm) diameter and 12 in (30 cm) deep. The air hole is 6 in (15 cm) diameter, angled to connect to the bottom of the fire hole. Build the fire inside the fire hole. The air tunnel creates a draft that feeds oxygen from below.

**When to use:** Tactical / low-visibility situations. Wind protection. Efficient fuel consumption (burns 50-70% less wood than open fire). Excellent for cooking — set a pot directly over the hole. In dry, sandy, or loamy soil the digging is fast.

**Drawbacks:** Requires digging tools or significant hand effort. Does not work in rocky, frozen, or waterlogged ground. Difficult to extinguish quickly. Radiates almost no warmth sideways — poor for body heating.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never build a Dakota hole in peaty or organic soil. Subsurface root fires can smolder for days or weeks and spread underground.

## Site Preparation

### Clearing Ground

Clear a circle at least 10 ft (3 m) in diameter down to bare mineral soil if possible. Remove dry leaves, pine needles, grass, and duff. Scrape with a stick, boot, or improvised tool. If you cannot reach mineral soil, build the fire on a platform of green logs or rocks.

!!! danger "DANGER: never burn poison ivy, oak, or sumac"
    Burning these releases **urushiol in the smoke**. Inhaling it inflames the airway
    and lung lining, and unlike a skin rash there is nothing you can do about it in the
    field. It is a medical emergency and it has killed people.

    You are most exposed exactly when you are least likely to be thinking about it —
    clearing brush for a fire site, or grabbing an armful of dead vine-covered wood.
    **Learn to recognize these plants without leaves**, since bare winter vines are
    the usual culprit: poison ivy vines are hairy and cling to trunks like rope.

    Also avoid burning oleander (toxic smoke), and any painted, treated, or salvaged
    lumber — pressure-treated wood releases arsenic and chromium compounds.

    See [Plant Poisoning](../medical/plant-poisoning.md) for identification.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never build fire on wet or river-smoothed rocks. Trapped moisture expands when heated and can cause rocks to explode, sending fragments at lethal velocity.

### Wind Considerations

- Determine prevailing wind direction before selecting your site.
- Position your shelter upwind of fire for warmth reflection, not downwind (smoke and spark hazard).
- Use natural windbreaks: rock faces, fallen logs, dense brush lines.
- In high wind, dig a trench or use the Dakota hole method.
- Wind above 15 mph (24 km/h) makes open fire dangerous — sparks carry far.

### Fire Reflectors

Build a reflector wall on the far side of the fire from your shelter. Stack green logs 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) long in a low wall, 2-3 ft (0.6-1 m) high. Angle the reflector to bounce heat toward your sleeping area. A rock face or earth bank serves the same function. The combination of fire + reflector + shelter behind you creates a heat trap.

### Surface Selection

| Surface | Suitability | Notes |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| Bare mineral soil | Best | No subsurface spread risk |
| Sand | Excellent | Easy cleanup |
| Gravel | Excellent | Good drainage |
| Flat rock | Good | Avoid wet / porous rock |
| Snow (on platform) | Acceptable | Build on green-log platform |
| Organic duff | Dangerous | Must clear to mineral soil |
| Peat | Never | Subsurface fire risk |

## The Progression: Spark to Sustainable Fire

Follow this sequence without skipping steps:

1. **Prepare the site.** Clear ground, arrange windbreak, position reflector.
2. **Gather all materials before ignition.** Tinder, kindling (sorted by size), fuel wood (sorted by size). Place everything within arm's reach.
3. **Build the tinder bundle.** Loose, airy, bird-nest shape. Finest material in the center.
4. **Arrange initial kindling.** Set up your chosen fire lay around (but not on top of) the tinder, or have kindling ready to add immediately.
5. **Ignite the tinder.** Use your chosen method. Place ember or spark into the center of the tinder bundle.
6. **Nurture the ember.** Blow gently and steadily at the base. Hold the bundle loosely — do not compress it. Oxygen is critical here.
7. **Transfer to the fire lay.** Place the flaming tinder bundle at the base of your prepared kindling structure.
8. **Add kindling progressively.** Start with the thinnest sticks. Add each size only after the previous size is burning well. Do not smother the flame.
9. **Graduate to fuel wood.** Once kindling is fully engaged, add wrist-thickness fuel wood. Wait for full combustion before adding arm-thickness logs.
10. **Maintain.** Feed the fire on a schedule. Do not let it burn down to bare coals until you have added enough fuel to sustain it.

!!! tip
    The moment most fires fail is between steps 7 and 8. Adding too much kindling too fast smothers the flame. Add one or two sticks at a time and wait for each to catch.

## Why Fire Fails

### Wet Materials

The single most common cause. Even wood that looks dry can hold moisture inside. Test by snapping — a clean snap with an audible crack means dry. A bend or stringy break means wet. Always source dead standing wood over ground wood.

### Smothering

Packing fuel too tightly blocks airflow. Leave gaps between sticks. Think "loosely stacked" not "packed solid." Fire needs to breathe.

### Skipping Fuel Stages

Jumping from tinder to large fuel wood. The tinder cannot ignite thick logs. Always bridge with kindling. Spend more time on kindling than you think necessary.

### Insufficient Tinder

One small pinch of grass is not enough. Gather at least two large handfuls. If your first attempt fails, you need a backup bundle ready.

### Wind Mismanagement

Too much wind blows flame away from fuel. Too little wind (or building in a depression) starves fire of oxygen. Moderate, steady airflow at the base is ideal.

### Giving Up Too Soon

Friction fire takes 10-45 minutes of sustained effort. Modern methods (ferro rod, lighter) should produce flame in under 2 minutes if materials are dry. If you have been trying for more than 5 minutes with a modern ignition source, stop. Reassess your tinder and kindling — the problem is materials, not method.

### Poor Site Selection

Building on damp ground, in a wind tunnel, or on organic soil. Spend 5 minutes selecting and preparing the site. It saves 30 minutes of failed attempts.

## Uses of Fire

### Warmth

Radiant heat from fire maintains core body temperature in cold conditions. Position yourself 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) from a sustained fire. Use a reflector wall to maximize heat capture. A long fire (built parallel to your body) warms more evenly than a single point source.

### Cooking

Coals cook food more evenly than open flame. Allow a hot fire to burn down to a coal bed before cooking. Use a grill of green sticks or flat rocks as a cooking surface. Boiling water requires a sustained, hot flame — use a teepee lay to get to boil quickly.

### Water Purification

Bring water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute at sea level. At elevations above 6,500 ft (2,000 m), boil for 3 minutes. Use a metal container, or heat dry rocks and drop them into water held in something that cannot go on a fire — see [If You Have Nothing to Boil In](../water/purification.md#if-you-have-nothing-to-boil-in). Fire is the most reliable purification method when no chemical treatment or filter is available.

### Signaling

Build a signal fire in an open, visible location — hilltop, clearing, shoreline. Prepare three fires in a triangle (international distress signal), spaced 100 ft (30 m) apart. For daytime signaling, produce smoke by adding green branches, wet leaves, or damp grass to a hot fire. For nighttime signaling, build a large, bright blaze. Keep signal fires ready to light at a moment's notice.

### Psychological Comfort

Fire provides light, warmth, and a sense of security. It reduces panic and improves decision-making. In a group survival situation, fire serves as a morale anchor and gathering point. Do not underestimate this benefit — will to survive is the most critical survival factor.

### Predator Deterrence

Most predators avoid fire. Maintain a fire through the night in areas with large predator risk. Keep burning branches available to wave if an animal approaches. Smoke deters insects, particularly mosquitoes — build a smoky fire upwind of your camp during peak insect hours.

## Common Mistakes

- Building the fire too large too fast — wasting fuel and losing control.
- Failing to gather enough fuel before dark — results in a cold night.
- Building directly on snow without a platform — the fire melts down into a pit and becomes unreachable.
- Ignoring wind direction — smoke fills the shelter, sparks ignite nearby materials.
- Using only one size of kindling — the progression must be gradual.
- Leaving fire unattended — wind shifts cause spread.
- Placing wet rocks around the fire ring — explosion hazard.
- Burning brush without checking it for poison ivy, oak, or sumac. Urushiol in smoke causes severe airway injury and is a medical emergency.
- Not having a backup ignition method — always carry two.

## Fire Safety and Extinguishing

### Containment

Ring the fire with rocks (dry, non-porous) or scrape a bare-earth perimeter at least 2 ft (0.6 m) wide. Never build fire under overhanging branches. Keep the fire only as large as you need — a small, well-managed fire is safer and more fuel-efficient than a bonfire.

### Extinguishing

1. Stop adding fuel and let the fire burn down.
2. Spread coals out flat with a stick — do not leave them piled.
3. Drown with water. Pour slowly, stir the ash, pour again. Repeat until no hissing or steam remains.
4. If no water is available, smother with mineral soil (not organic duff). Cover completely and pack down.
5. Feel the ash and surrounding soil with the back of your hand. If warm, repeat the process.
6. Wait 10 minutes and check again.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Buried coals can reignite up to 24 hours later. If you must leave, ensure the fire is dead cold.

### Leave No Trace

In non-emergency situations, practice leave-no-trace fire principles:
- Use existing fire rings when available.
- Build mound fires on mineral soil carried to a flat rock.
- Scatter cold ashes widely after full extinguishment.
- Return displaced soil and rocks to their original positions.
- Remove all unburned trash and food scraps.
