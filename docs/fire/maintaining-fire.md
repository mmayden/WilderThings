# Maintaining Fire

> How to manage fuel, bank coals, carry fire, and sustain flame through the night and across distances.

## At a Glance

- A fire without a fuel plan dies. Gather three times more wood than you think you need before dark.
- Bank coals under ash to preserve fire overnight without tending.
- Carrying fire between camps saves hours of reignition effort — use punk wood, slow match, or a coal carrier.
- Hardwood burns slower and produces lasting coals. Softwood burns fast and hot but leaves poor coals.
- Manage ash buildup. A thick ash layer smothers coals and blocks airflow.

## Fuel Management

### Fuel Stages and Burn Rates

Different wood types serve different purposes. Match your fuel to your need.

| Wood Type | Burn Rate | Coal Quality | Best Use |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Pine, spruce, fir (softwood) | Fast, hot | Poor — burns to powder | Quick heat, boiling water, starting fires |
| Oak, hickory, maple (hardwood) | Slow, steady | Excellent — long-lasting coals | Overnight fire, cooking, banking |
| Birch | Medium-fast | Moderate | Good all-purpose, bark for tinder |
| Ash | Medium | Good | Burns well even slightly green |
| Willow, poplar | Fast | Poor | Emergency fuel only |

!!! tip
    In temperate hardwood forests, prioritize oak and hickory for overnight fires. One armload of split oak outlasts three armloads of pine.

### How Much Fuel to Gather

Underestimating fuel is the most common fire maintenance failure. Use these benchmarks:

- **Overnight fire (8 hours, moderate cold):** A woodpile 4 feet long, 2 feet high, and 2 feet wide (1.2 x 0.6 x 0.6 m) of mixed split wood.
- **Cooking fire (2 hours):** An armload of wrist-thick hardwood, plus kindling.
- **Signal fire (ready state):** Three prepared fire lays plus a reserve pile of dry fuel and green-branch smoke material at each.

Gather all fuel before dark. Searching for wood in darkness is slow, dangerous, and produces poor-quality wet or green wood.

### Fuel Sizing

Feed fuel in a steady progression. Jumping from small kindling to large logs smothers the fire.

1. Once kindling is fully engaged, add thumb-thick sticks, 1-2 inches (2.5-5 cm) diameter.
2. After 5-10 minutes, add wrist-thick pieces, 3-4 inches (7.5-10 cm) diameter.
3. For sustained burning, add arm-thick logs, 5-8 inches (13-20 cm) diameter. Split these in half — split wood ignites faster and burns more completely.

!!! note
    Never add a single large log to a small fire. The log absorbs heat faster than the fire produces it, and the fire dies. Always have an intermediate-size bridge.

## Banking Coals for Overnight

Banking is the technique of burying coals under ash to slow combustion and preserve live coals for hours without fuel addition. A properly banked fire survives 6-10 hours.

### Procedure

1. Stop adding fuel 30-45 minutes before you intend to bank. Allow the fire to burn down to a deep coal bed. The coal bed should be at least 3 inches (7.5 cm) thick.
2. Push all coals into a compact mound in the center of the fire pit.
3. Cover the mound with a 2-3 inch (5-7.5 cm) layer of fine ash. Pack it gently. Do not use mineral soil unless ash is insufficient — soil insulates but also contaminates the coal bed.
4. Place 2-3 large, dense hardwood logs (6-8 inches / 15-20 cm diameter) directly on top of the ash mound. The coals slowly consume these from below overnight.

### Revival in the Morning

1. Remove the hardwood logs (they may be partially burned through).
2. Scrape away the ash to expose the coal bed. Live coals glow red when exposed to air.
3. Blow gently on the coals. They should brighten within seconds.
4. Place fine tinder (shaved bark, dry grass, feather stick) directly on the coals.
5. Continue blowing. The tinder catches flame within 1-3 minutes.
6. Add kindling and rebuild the fire using standard progression.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    If no glow appears after scraping and blowing, the coals may be dead. Verify by holding your hand 4 inches (10 cm) above the coal bed. If you feel heat, live coals remain deeper. Continue scraping. If no heat at all, start a new fire.

## Carrying Fire

Moving a live coal or ember between locations saves the effort of starting a new fire from scratch. Traditional and indigenous peoples carried fire routinely across long distances.

### Punk Wood

Punk wood is soft, spongy, partially decayed wood that smolders slowly without flaming. It is the most common fire-carrying material.

1. Find a piece of thoroughly dry punk wood from a standing dead tree. It should be soft enough to crumble between your fingers but not wet or powdery.
2. Touch the punk wood to a live coal. Blow gently until it catches and begins to glow and smolder.
3. Wrap the smoldering punk loosely in green leaves or bark to slow the burn and protect it from wind. Leave enough airflow to sustain the smolder.
4. Carry it in your hand or in a bark trough. Check every 30 minutes to ensure it is still smoldering.
5. At your destination, place the glowing punk wood into a tinder bundle and blow it to flame.

!!! tip
    Good punk wood smolders for 2-6 hours depending on piece size and density. Carry a piece the size of your fist for a half-day's travel.

### Slow Match

A slow match is a length of cordage or natural fiber that burns slowly along its length, maintaining a coal at the burning tip.

1. Twist dry plant fibers (cattail, inner bark of cedar or basswood, dried grass) into a loose rope 1/4-1/2 inch (6-13 mm) thick and 3-6 feet (1-1.8 m) long.
2. Light one end from your fire. The slow match should smolder at the tip, advancing slowly along its length.
3. Carry the match with the burning end up. Shield from wind.
4. A 3-foot (1 m) slow match burns for 1-3 hours depending on material density.

### Coal Carrier

Build a simple carrier for transporting coals over longer distances.

1. Line a section of green bark (birch or elm) or a tin can with mineral soil or sand, 1-2 inches (2.5-5 cm) deep.
2. Place 3-4 live coals on the soil bed.
3. Cover with a layer of dry punk wood or tinder fungus to insulate.
4. Add another thin layer of soil.
5. Wrap loosely in green bark. Leave small air holes.
6. This carrier preserves coals for 6-12 hours.

!!! note
    The Pacific Northwest peoples used clam shells lined with damp sand and cedar bark to carry coals in canoes. Adapt the principle to available materials.

## Relighting from Coals

Even a seemingly dead fire may contain viable coals buried under ash. Before starting a new fire from scratch, always check the old fire pit.

1. Scrape away ash to the bottom of the fire pit.
2. Feel for heat with the back of your hand held 2-4 inches (5-10 cm) above the surface.
3. If you find a live coal, no matter how small, place a wad of fine, dry tinder directly on it.
4. Blow steadily from the side. Protect the coal from wind.
5. A single pea-sized coal can relight a fire in under 2 minutes with good tinder.

## Drying Wet Wood Using Existing Fire

An established fire is a wood dryer. Use it to pre-dry the next batch of fuel.

### Methods

- **Lean sticks against flanking rocks.** Place wet or damp split wood upright, leaning against rocks beside the fire. The radiant heat dries the wood face. Rotate pieces periodically.
- **Ring the fire pit.** Lay a ring of fuel wood 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) from the fire's edge. The radiant heat drives moisture out over 30-60 minutes.
- **Overhead drying rack.** Build a simple rack from green sticks above the fire (at least 3 feet / 1 m above flames). Lay small-diameter wet wood across it. Heat rises and dries the wood.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not place wet wood directly on or in the fire to "dry it." This drops the fire temperature, produces excessive smoke, and may extinguish a small fire entirely. Dry wood near the fire, then add it once surface moisture is gone.

## Ash Management

Ash accumulates quickly and causes problems if ignored.

- **Too much ash smothers coals.** Rake ash away from the active coal bed periodically. Push it to the perimeter of the fire pit.
- **Use ash for banking.** Fine ash is the ideal insulating material for overnight coal preservation.
- **Use ash for cooking.** Wrap food in leaves and bury it in hot ash for slow baking.
- **Use ash for sanitation.** Wood ash mixed with water creates a crude lye solution for cleaning.

Maintain a clear coal bed at the center of the fire with ash pushed to the sides. This ensures oxygen reaches the coals from below.

## Fire Watch for Groups

In a group survival situation, assign fire watch shifts to maintain the fire through the night.

### Schedule

- Divide the night into 2-hour shifts. Shorter shifts keep the watcher alert. Longer shifts cause drowsiness and neglect.
- The fire watcher's duties: add fuel every 15-20 minutes, monitor wind changes, watch for sparks threatening shelters, and maintain the fuel supply.
- The first and last watches are hardest. Assign your most reliable people to these slots.

### Fuel Staging

Pre-stage each watch's fuel supply in separate piles within arm's reach of the fire. Each pile should contain:

- 6-8 wrist-thick pieces for regular feeding
- 2-3 arm-thick pieces for sustained burns during the watch
- A handful of kindling in case the fire needs revival

This prevents the watcher from leaving the fire unattended to search for wood.

## When to Maintain vs. When to Let Die

Not every fire needs to last all night. Weigh the cost of maintaining against the effort of restarting.

**Maintain the fire when:**

- Nighttime temperature drops below 50°F (10°C) and you lack adequate insulated shelter.
- Predator activity is likely (large cats, bears, wolves in area).
- You have injured or hypothermic group members who need continuous warmth.
- You plan to carry fire to the next camp in the morning.

**Let the fire die when:**

- Temperature is mild and shelter is adequate.
- Fuel is scarce and tomorrow's fire start is easy (dry conditions, reliable ignition source).
- You are moving camp at dawn and do not need to carry fire.
- You are in a fire-risk area (dry grassland, drought conditions) and cannot tend it safely.

## Fire in Different Weather

### Wind

- In sustained wind above 15 mph (24 km/h), build a windbreak or use a trench/Dakota fire.
- Reposition fuel additions to the windward side so the fire draws across the fuel.
- Secure lightweight fuel so it does not blow away before igniting.

### Rain

- See [Fire in Wet Conditions](fire-in-wet-conditions.md) for detailed procedures.
- Keep a reserve of dry kindling and tinder under cover at all times.
- Feed the fire more frequently. A small fire in rain loses heat faster than in dry conditions.

### Extreme Cold

- Fire consumes fuel faster in extreme cold because more heat energy escapes to the environment.
- Increase fuel reserves by 50% below 0°F (-18°C).
- Position a reflector wall to direct heat toward your shelter. Without a reflector, most heat energy radiates uselessly into open air.

### Extreme Heat

- In desert conditions, fire is used primarily for cooking, signaling, and nighttime warmth (desert nights drop sharply).
- Keep fires small. Large fires waste scarce fuel and add unwanted heat during the day.
- Fire risk is highest in arid environments. Clear a wide perimeter and never leave fire unattended.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Never burn poison ivy, oak, or sumac. Urushiol carries in the smoke and inflames
    the airway and lungs — a medical emergency with no field treatment. Check gathered
    brush and vine-covered deadwood before it goes on the fire. See
    [Fire Principles](fire-principles.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Not gathering enough fuel before dark.** Searching for wood at night produces poor fuel and wastes energy. Stockpile during daylight.
- **Feeding the fire with oversized logs.** Large logs smother a small fire. Maintain the size progression even when adding to an established fire.
- **Ignoring ash buildup.** Thick ash blankets coals and blocks airflow. Rake coals clear regularly.
- **Placing wet wood directly on the fire.** This drops temperature and produces smoke. Pre-dry all fuel near the fire first.
- **Failing to bank before sleeping.** Without banking, the fire burns out in 1-2 hours. You wake cold and must restart from scratch.
- **Carrying fire without checking the ember.** Check smoldering punk wood or slow match every 30 minutes. A dead ember means starting over at your destination.
- **No fire watcher in groups.** An untended overnight fire either dies or spreads. Assign shifts.
