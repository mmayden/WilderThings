# Fire Types

> Purpose-built fire configurations for warmth, cooking, signaling, stealth, and sustained overnight burning.

## At a Glance

- Match fire type to purpose — a warming fire, cooking fire, and signal fire are built differently.
- The long fire and upside-down fire are the best designs for overnight warmth without constant tending.
- The keyhole fire combines cooking and warmth in a single structure.
- Trench and Dakota fires resist wind and conceal the flame.
- Signal fires require advance preparation — build them before you need them.

## Long Fire

The long fire burns parallel to your body and radiates heat evenly along your full length. It is the best fire for sleeping in cold conditions.

### Construction

1. Select two green logs 6-8 inches (15-20 cm) in diameter and 6-8 feet (1.8-2.4 m) long — roughly your body length plus 1 foot (30 cm) on each end.
2. Lay them parallel on the ground, 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) apart.
3. Drive short green stakes into the ground at each end to prevent the logs from rolling apart as they burn.
4. Build a standard fire between the logs using tinder and kindling.
5. Once established, lay fuel wood across the gap between the two logs. The green side logs act as supports and fire walls.

### Use

Lie parallel to the fire, 2-3 feet (0.6-1 m) away. Place a reflector wall or your shelter on the opposite side to trap heat. The long fire radiates warmth along your entire body instead of heating one spot.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    You are sleeping beside an open fire, so treat sparks as certain rather than
    possible. Sleep **upwind** of it, clear anything loose and flammable — dry grass
    bedding, a poncho edge, a pack strap — out of the spark zone, and expect synthetic
    insulation and shell fabric to melt rather than smoulder, which means a hole before
    you smell anything. Check your bedding when you turn over.

!!! tip
    Angle the two side logs so one end is slightly closer together. Light the narrow end. The fire burns slowly toward the wide end over several hours, extending burn time without tending.

## Keyhole Fire

The keyhole fire provides both a cooking area and a warming fire in one structure. The shape resembles a keyhole viewed from above.

### Construction

1. Build a standard campfire ring in a circle 24-30 inches (60-75 cm) in diameter using rocks.
2. Extend a narrow channel from one side of the circle, 18 inches (45 cm) long and 10-12 inches (25-30 cm) wide, lined with rocks on both sides.
3. Build and maintain the main fire in the circular section.
4. Rake hot coals from the main fire into the narrow channel as needed.
5. Cook over the coal channel. The narrow walls support a grill or pot. The coal bed provides even, controllable heat.

### Use

The circular section provides warmth and light. The channel provides a separate, stable cooking surface. Replenish the channel with fresh coals from the main fire. This design separates flame management from cooking — a major advantage.

## Upside-Down Fire (Top-Down Fire)

The upside-down fire inverts the normal fire lay. Large wood goes on the bottom, smallest on top. It is self-feeding, long-burning, and requires minimal tending.

### Construction

1. Lay 3-4 of the largest available split logs side by side on the ground as the base layer.
2. Stack a second layer of slightly smaller split logs perpendicular to the first.
3. Add a third layer of wrist-thick sticks perpendicular to the second.
4. Add a fourth layer of pencil-thick kindling.
5. Place feather sticks or fine tinder on the very top.
6. Light from the top.

### Use

The fire burns downward. As each layer collapses, it falls onto the layer below, which has been pre-dried and pre-heated by the fire above. The result is 2-4 hours of burn time from a single load with no tending.

!!! note
    The upside-down fire takes 15-20 minutes to fully establish because it must burn through the kindling layer before reaching full output. Plan for this delay.

## Swedish Torch (Canadian Candle)

A self-contained fire built from a single log. Ideal for cooking on uneven terrain or in snow.

### Construction (Split Method)

1. Select a dry log 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) long and 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) in diameter. It must stand upright on a flat, sawn end.
2. Split the log into quarters using a knife and baton, or an axe. Do not split completely — leave the bottom 3-4 inches (7.5-10 cm) intact to hold the log together.
3. Stuff tinder and small kindling into the splits at the center.
4. Light the tinder. The fire burns outward from the center, consuming the log from the inside.

### Construction (Cross-Cut Method)

1. Stand a log upright. Cut two perpendicular saw kerfs (cross pattern) from the top, extending two-thirds of the way down.
2. Stuff tinder into the top of the cross cuts. Light it.
3. The cuts act as chimneys, drawing air upward through the log.

### Use

The flat top of the burning log supports a pot or pan directly. The fire burns for 1-3 hours depending on log diameter and wood density. One log equals one stove.

!!! tip
    In deep snow, a Swedish torch stands above the snow surface on its own base. No platform needed.

## Banking a Fire for Overnight

Banking preserves coals through the night so you can revive the fire quickly at dawn instead of starting from scratch.

### Method

1. Allow the fire to burn down to a thick bed of coals. Do not add new fuel for the last 30-45 minutes before banking.
2. Push all coals into a compact mound at the center of the fire pit.
3. Cover the coal mound with a 2-3 inch (5-7.5 cm) layer of ash. If you have insufficient ash, use dry mineral soil (not organic duff).
4. Place 2-3 large, dense hardwood logs directly on top of the ash-covered mound.
5. The ash insulates the coals, reducing oxygen and slowing combustion. The hardwood logs provide fuel that the coals slowly consume overnight.

### Revival

1. In the morning, scrape away the ash layer to expose the live coals beneath.
2. Place fine tinder and kindling directly on the coals.
3. Blow gently to supply oxygen. The coals should glow and ignite the tinder within minutes.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Banking works best with hardwood coals (oak, hickory, maple). Softwood coals (pine, spruce) burn through too quickly and may not survive the night.

## Signal Fire

A signal fire alerts rescuers to your location. Build it before you need it, in the most visible location available.

### Construction

1. Select an open, elevated site: hilltop, clearing, shoreline, or ridgeline. Avoid forest canopy.
2. Build three fire lays in a triangle pattern, spaced 100 feet (30 m) apart. Three fires in a triangle is the international distress signal.
3. Construct each fire as a large log cabin or teepee lay, ready to light.
4. Protect each fire lay from rain with a bark or bough cover that you can remove quickly.
5. Stockpile green branches, wet leaves, or damp grass beside each fire for smoke production.

### Daytime Signaling

Light the fires and add green or wet material to produce thick, white smoke. Smoke is visible for miles against a clear sky. Dark smoke (from rubber, oil, or plastic if available) stands out against overcast skies. Rubber and plastic give off genuinely toxic fumes — dioxins and hydrogen chloride among them. Light it, then get upwind and stay out of the plume; do not stand over it feeding the fire.

### Nighttime Signaling

Bright flame is visible for miles at night. Feed dry fuel aggressively to build maximum flame height. Do not add green material — you want light, not smoke.

!!! note
    Maintain signal fire materials in ready-to-light condition. An aircraft passes your location in seconds. Scrambling to build a fire after spotting a plane is too late.

## Smoky Fire for Insect Control

Mosquitoes, black flies, and other biting insects avoid smoke. A dedicated smoky fire placed upwind of your camp reduces insect pressure significantly.

### Method

1. Build a small, hot base fire.
2. Once a coal bed is established, pile on green leaves, green grass, damp moss, or punky (rotten) wood.
3. The fire smolders and produces dense smoke without much flame.
4. Position the fire 6-10 feet (1.8-3 m) upwind of your sleeping or working area.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Prolonged smoke inhalation damages lungs. Stay upwind of the smoky fire and let the smoke drift across your camp area, not into your face. Sleep above the smoke layer if possible.

## Trench Fire

A trench fire burns below ground level, protected from wind. It is effective in open, exposed terrain (prairies, deserts, ridgelines).

### Construction

1. Dig a trench 12 inches (30 cm) wide, 12 inches (30 cm) deep, and 36 inches (90 cm) long. Orient the long axis into the prevailing wind.
2. Widen the windward end slightly to create a funnel that channels air to the fire.
3. Build the fire in the center of the trench.
4. Lay green sticks across the trench as a grill for cooking.

### Use

The trench walls block crosswind that would scatter a surface fire. The shape creates a natural draft. The fire is nearly invisible from a distance — useful when concealment matters. Cooking over the trench is stable because the walls support a grill or pot at ground level.

## Cooking Fires vs. Warming Fires

Different goals require different fires. Building the wrong type wastes fuel and time.

| Feature | Cooking Fire | Warming Fire |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Heat source | Coal bed | Active flame |
| Flame height | Low / none | Medium to high |
| Best lays | Keyhole, trench, Dakota | Long fire, teepee, log cabin |
| Fuel type | Hardwood for lasting coals | Mixed for sustained flame |
| Distance from user | Arm's reach | 2-3 ft / 0.6-1 m |
| Feed frequency | Add coals as needed | Continuous small additions |

!!! tip
    Let a hot fire burn down for 30-45 minutes to produce a coal bed before cooking. Cooking over flame chars the outside and leaves the inside raw. Coals provide even, radiant heat.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using one fire type for everything.** A teepee is not a cooking fire. A coal bed is not a signal fire. Match the design to the purpose.
- **Building the signal fire under tree cover.** Canopy disperses smoke and hides flame. Signal fires require open sky above.
- **Banking with softwood coals.** Softwood coals burn through fast and die before morning. Use hardwood.
- **Making the trench too wide.** A wide trench loses the chimney effect. Keep it 12 inches (30 cm) wide.
- **Neglecting the long fire's green log supports.** Without green side logs, a long fire spreads sideways instead of burning linearly.
- **Lighting the upside-down fire from the bottom.** Light from the top only. Bottom-lighting collapses the structure before upper layers dry.
- **Forgetting wind direction for smoky fires.** A smoky fire downwind of your camp fills your shelter with smoke. Always place it upwind.

## Quick Reference

| Fire Type | Best For | Burn Time | Tending |
|-----------|----------|-----------|---------|
| Long fire | Sleeping warmth | 4-8 hours | Low |
| Keyhole | Cooking + warmth | Continuous | Moderate |
| Upside-down | Unattended warmth | 2-4 hours | Minimal |
