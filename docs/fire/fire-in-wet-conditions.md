# Fire in Wet Conditions

> How to find dry materials, build, and maintain fire during rain, snow, and high-humidity conditions.

## At a Glance

- Standing dead wood is your primary fuel source in wet conditions — never collect wood from the ground.
- Split all wood to expose dry interior grain before use.
- Birch bark and fatwood ignite even when wet — learn to identify both.
- Build a platform and overhead shelter for the fire before attempting ignition.
- Feather sticks are the single most important wet-weather fire skill.
- If you are already shivering hard and fumbling, get insulated before you try to light anything.

!!! danger "DANGER: cold takes the skills this guide requires, in the order it needs them"
    Everything below — batoning, feather sticks thin enough to be translucent, striking a
    ferro rod — is fine motor work. Mild hypothermia destroys fine motor coordination
    early: the standard field sign is that a person can no longer work a zipper or tie a
    knot. Someone at that point cannot make a feather stick, and each failed attempt
    costs more heat.

    **If you are shivering hard and fumbling, stop and get insulated first.** Dry
    layers, out of the wind, off the ground, into shelter or a debris pile. Shelter is
    the faster and more certain answer, and it needs no dexterity. Build the fire once
    you have stopped losing ground — not as a race against how cold you are getting.

    This is a sequencing point, not an argument against fire. Fire in the wet is worth
    the effort. It is just the second thing, not the first. See
    [Stages of Hypothermia](../medical/hypothermia-and-heatstroke.md#stages-of-hypothermia)
    and [Shelter Principles](../shelter/shelter-principles.md).

## Finding Dry Materials in Wet Weather

Rain soaks exposed surfaces but rarely penetrates deep into standing dead wood, dense bark, or sheltered spaces. Every material you need exists in a wet forest — the skill is knowing where to find it.

### Standing Dead Wood

Standing dead trees and dead branches still attached to living trees are the most reliable fuel source in rain. Ground wood absorbs moisture like a sponge. Standing dead wood sheds rain and dries via air circulation.

1. Look for dead trees that have lost their bark. Bare, gray wood exposed to wind dries fastest.
2. Snap-test branches. A clean, sharp snap means dry interior. A bend or stringy break means too much moisture.
3. Harvest branches from the underside of leaning dead trees — these are the most sheltered.
4. Dead lower branches on living conifers (spruce, pine, fir) are often dry. Snap off branches close to the trunk where the canopy sheds rain.

### The Split-Wood Technique

Even wood that feels damp on the surface is often dry inside. Split every piece of fuel before use.

1. Baton a knife or sharp rock through rounds of dead wood to split them in half. Keep
   both hands clear of the blade path and the split line — see
   [Knife Use and Care](../tools-and-craft/knife-use-and-care.md#batoning-technique).
2. Split halves into quarters. Expose maximum dry interior surface.
3. Use the split face as the ignition surface. Shave tinder and kindling from the dry inner grain.
4. A piece of wrist-thick wood that feels wet on the outside produces bone-dry shavings from the center.

!!! tip
    If you lack a knife, wedge a sharp rock into the end of a short log and strike it against a larger log or rock. Crude splitting still exposes dry wood.

## Wet-Weather Tinder Sources

### Birch Bark

Birch bark contains betulin, a flammable oil that resists moisture. It ignites even when soaked on the surface. Peel thin, papery layers from dead birch trees (do not strip bark from living trees — it kills them). Shred into thin strips. The thinner the strips, the faster the ignition.

!!! note
    Birch bark is available across the Northern Hemisphere: paper birch (North America), silver birch (Europe), and Himalayan birch (Asia). Prioritize it as your first-choice wet-weather tinder.

### Fatwood (Resin-Rich Wood)

Fatwood is the resin-saturated heartwood found in dead conifer stumps and root systems. It smells strongly of turpentine when cut or scraped.

**How to find it:**

1. Locate a dead pine, spruce, or fir stump. The older and more decayed, the better — the sapwood rots away, concentrating resin in the heartwood.
2. Kick or break away soft, rotten outer wood.
3. The hard, dense, amber-colored core is fatwood. It feels heavier and more waxy than normal wood.
4. Shave fine curls from fatwood for tinder. Use small sticks of it as kindling.

Fatwood burns hot and long even in rain. It is the most reliable natural fire-starting material in coniferous forests worldwide.

### Other Wet-Weather Tinders

- **Old man's beard lichen** (*Usnea* spp.) — hangs from branches in damp forests. Shake out excess water, fluff it, and stuff inside a dry pocket for 15-30 minutes. It catches spark from a ferro rod.
- **Dry pine needles** — find them in dense clusters under thick canopy or inside hollow trees.
- **Cedar bark** — shred the inner bark into fine fibers. Even damp cedar bark ignites with sustained flame.
- **Resin globules** — scrape dried sap from conifer bark. It is pure fuel.
- **Cramp ball fungus** (*Daldinia concentrica*) — the black, round fungus on ash trees. The dry interior catches and holds a spark.

## Feather Sticks

Feather sticks are the most important wet-weather fire-building technique. They convert a single stick of split dry wood into both tinder and kindling.

### How to Make a Feather Stick

1. Select a straight piece of split dry wood, thumb-thickness or larger, 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) long.
2. Brace the stick upright against a stable surface or hold it in your non-dominant hand.
3. Place your knife blade near the top of the stick at a shallow angle (15-20 degrees).
4. Push the blade downward in a controlled shaving motion, away from your body and away
   from the hand holding the stick. Curl thin shavings that remain attached to the stick.
5. Rotate the stick and repeat. Build up layers of curled shavings along the length.
6. A good feather stick has 20-30 thin curls hanging from it, creating a mass of fine, dry surface area.
7. Make 3-5 feather sticks. Use the finest-curled one as the ignition point. Stack the others around it as kindling.

!!! tip
    Shave curls thin enough to be translucent. Thick shavings resist ignition. The thinner the curl, the faster it catches.

## Building a Fire in the Rain

### Step 1: Shelter the Fire Site

Before anything else, create overhead protection for the spot where you will build the fire.

- Lean a slab of bark, a flat rock, or a dense bough shelter over the fire site at a steep angle to shed rain.
- Stretch a tarp, poncho, or space blanket above the site if available. Keep it at least 4 feet (1.2 m) above the fire to avoid melting or ignition.
- Use your own body as a windbreak and rain shield during the critical ignition phase.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Remove the overhead shelter or raise it significantly once the fire is established. A burning shelter above a fire is an emergency you do not need.

### Step 2: Build a Platform

Never build a fire directly on wet ground. Moisture wicks upward and kills the fire from below.

1. Lay a platform of green or wet logs side by side, 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) in diameter, covering an area at least 18 inches (45 cm) square.
2. Place a layer of dry bark on top of the log platform.

!!! danger "DANGER: do not use wet or river rocks for the platform"
    Stones are an option for a fire base only if they are genuinely dry. In rain, most
    are not. Water trapped in porous or river-smoothed rock turns to steam when heated
    and bursts the rock, throwing fragments at face height and lethal speed. Dry bark or
    split green logs are the safer platform in wet weather, and always available where
    dry rock is not. See
    [Fire Principles](fire-principles.md) for the full rule.
3. Build your fire on top of this raised, insulated surface.

### Step 3: Use the Upside-Down Method

The upside-down (top-down) fire lay works exceptionally well in wet conditions because it keeps the ignition point elevated above moisture.

1. Lay 3-4 wrist-thick split logs on the platform as the base layer.
2. Stack a second layer of slightly smaller split wood perpendicular to the first.
3. Continue stacking 2-3 more layers, each with smaller wood, in alternating directions.
4. Place feather sticks and fine tinder on the very top.
5. Light from the top. The fire burns downward, drying each layer before it reaches it.

### Step 4: Shield During Ignition

Crouch over the fire site. Use your body, a jacket, or a pack to block wind and rain from the tinder. Light the feather sticks. Once flame is established on the top layer, gradually reduce shielding as the fire grows.

## Fire in Snow

### Snow Platform Method

Deep snow requires extra preparation. Fire melts snow and sinks, eventually drowning in meltwater.

1. Stamp down a wide area of snow, at least 5 feet (1.5 m) in diameter.
2. Lay a platform of green logs across the stamped surface. Use the thickest available logs — 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) diameter. Pack them tightly.
3. Build a second perpendicular layer of green logs on top.
4. Build the fire on top of this double-layer platform.

!!! note
    In extreme cold (-20°F / -30°C and below), standing dead wood is often drier than in mild wet conditions. Extreme cold freeze-dries wood. The challenge is ignition in wind, not material moisture.

### Harvesting Fuel in Snow

- Dead standing trees above the snowline remain accessible. Snap off dead branches by pushing sideways, not pulling down (pulling showers snow onto you).
- Conifer lower branches buried under snow are often dead and dry. Dig down to the trunk and snap off branches close to the bole.
- Avoid collecting snow-covered ground wood. Even if you shake off the snow, the wood has been absorbing moisture for weeks or months.

## Maintaining Fire in Ongoing Rain

Once the fire is burning, keep it alive with active management.

- **Feed continuously.** A small fire in rain dies fast without frequent fuel addition. Keep split wood within arm's reach.
- **Dry your next batch.** Stack the next round of fuel near (not on) the fire to pre-dry it. Lean split wood against rocks flanking the fire, dry faces toward the heat.
- **Maintain airflow.** Rain flattens ash onto coals, smothering them. Rake coals periodically to restore airflow.
- **Keep the fire hot.** A large, hot fire resists rain better than a small one. Once established, build it up to a self-sustaining size. A bed of coals 12 inches (30 cm) across shrugs off light rain.
- **Shield from driving rain.** Position a reflector wall of green logs or stacked stones on the windward side. This blocks horizontally driven rain.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Hissing and steam from the fire are normal in rain. If the fire produces thick white steam with diminishing flame, add more dry split wood immediately — it is losing the battle.

## Common Mistakes

- **Collecting wood from the ground.** Ground wood in rain is saturated. Always harvest standing dead wood.
- **Failing to split wood.** Surface moisture defeats ignition. Split every piece to expose dry interior.
- **Building directly on wet earth.** Moisture wicks up and kills the fire from below. Always use a platform.
- **Making feather sticks too thick.** Thick shavings resist ignition. Shave curls thin enough to be translucent.
- **Skipping overhead shelter.** Rain falling directly on tinder during ignition wastes matches and attempts.
- **Impatience.** Wet-weather fire takes three times longer to establish than dry-weather fire. Accept this and work methodically.
- **Pushing on while hypothermic.** If your hands have stopped working, more attempts will not fix that. Insulate first, then build the fire.
- **Grabbing whatever rock is handy for the platform.** Wet rock explodes. In rain, assume every rock is wet.
- **Using only tinder and no kindling bridge.** Tinder flame alone cannot ignite damp fuel wood. The feather stick and kindling stage is critical.
- **Neglecting to pre-dry fuel.** Stack your next fuel load near the fire. Feeding cold, damp wood kills a struggling fire.

## Quick Reference

|------|--------|
| Primary fuel source | Standing dead wood, split open |
