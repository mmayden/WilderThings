# Home Emergency Preparedness

> Prepare your home to sustain your household for 14 days without external utilities, supply chains, or emergency services.

## At a Glance

- Store 1 gallon (3.8 L) of water per person per day for 14 days minimum.
- Maintain a 14-day supply of shelf-stable food and rotate it on a schedule.
- Plan for power, heating, cooling, sanitation, and communication without the grid.
- Every household member must know the plan — written, practiced, and posted.
- Start with 72 hours of readiness and build to 14 days over time.

---

## Water Storage

Water is the first priority. Without it, survival is limited to 3 days.

### How Much

- **1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day** for drinking and basic hygiene.
- **14-day target:** 14 gallons (53 L) per person.
- A household of 4 needs 56 gallons (212 L) minimum.
- Add 0.5 gallon (1.9 L) per day per pet.

### Storage Methods

| Method | Capacity | Shelf Life | Cost |
|--------|----------|------------|------|
| Store-bought bottled water | Varies | 1-2 years (check label) | Low |
| WaterBricks / stackable containers | 3.5 gal (13 L) each | 5 years with treatment | Medium |
| 55-gallon (208 L) drum | 55 gal | 5 years with treatment | Medium |
| Bathtub liner (WaterBOB) | 100 gal (378 L) | Fill at disaster onset, use within days | Low |

### Treatment for Long-Term Storage

Add 1/8 teaspoon of unscented household bleach (8.25% sodium hypochlorite) per gallon (3.8 L). Replace stored water every 12 months or treat with fresh bleach.

!!! tip
    Fill a WaterBOB or clean bathtub when a storm or disruption is forecast. This adds 100 gallons (378 L) of clean water instantly.

See [Water Storage](../water/water-storage.md) for detailed guidance.

## Food Storage

### Principles

- Store foods your household already eats. Emergencies are not the time for unfamiliar meals.
- Rotate stock using first-in, first-out (FIFO).
- Calculate calories: 2,000 per adult per day, 1,500 per child per day.

### Shelf-Stable Categories

| Food Type | Shelf Life | Notes |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| Canned goods (meat, vegetables, fruit) | 2-5 years | Rotate annually |
| Dried pasta, rice, beans | 1-2 years (8-30 years if sealed with O2 absorbers) | Require water and heat to prepare |
| Freeze-dried meals | 25-30 years | Expensive but lightweight |
| Peanut butter | 1-2 years | Calorie-dense, no prep needed |
| Honey | Indefinite | Energy source, wound care |
| Hardtack, pilot bread | 5+ years | Dense calories, long shelf life |

### Cooking Without Power

- Camp stove with 14 days of fuel.
- Charcoal or propane grill (outdoor use only).
- Rocket stove (uses small sticks, burns efficiently).
- Solar oven in sunny climates.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never use charcoal grills, propane heaters, or camp stoves indoors. Carbon monoxide is odorless and lethal.

## Power Alternatives

### Generator

- Portable gasoline generator: 3,000-7,000 watts covers refrigerator, lights, phone charging, and a medical device.
- Store fuel safely: 10-20 gallons (38-76 L) in approved containers, away from the house.
- Run the generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet (6 m) from windows and doors.
- Exercise the generator monthly. Stale fuel and neglected engines fail on day one.

### Solar

- Portable solar panels (100-200 watts) charge battery banks and small devices.
- A home solar system with battery backup (Tesla Powerwall, EcoFlow Delta Pro) can run essential circuits for days.

### Batteries

- Stock disposable batteries (AA, AAA, D, 9V) for flashlights, radios, and clocks.
- Rechargeable battery banks (20,000+ mAh) for phones and tablets.
- Hand-crank radio and flashlight as a last-resort power source.

## Heating Without Grid

=== "Wood Heat"
    - Wood stove or fireplace with a clean, inspected chimney.
    - Store 1 cord of seasoned hardwood per month of heating season.
    - Keep fire extinguisher and CO detector near the stove.

=== "Propane / Kerosene"
    - Indoor-rated propane heater (e.g., Mr. Buddy).
    - Ventilation is mandatory — crack a window.
    - Store fuel per manufacturer limits.
    - CO detector with battery backup.

=== "Passive Methods"
    - Insulate one room heavily — hang blankets over windows and doorways.
    - Gather all household members into the smallest interior room.
    - Layer clothing: base layer, insulation, wind layer.
    - Body heat from 4 people warms a small room significantly.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Carbon monoxide kills silently. Any combustion heat source requires ventilation and a working CO detector with battery backup.

## Cooling Without Grid

- Close blinds and curtains on sun-facing windows during the day.
- Open windows at night for cross-ventilation.
- Wet towels on skin and in front of a battery-powered fan.
- Spend time in the lowest level of the home (basements are cooler).
- Stay hydrated — dehydration accelerates heat illness.

## Sanitation Plan

Without running water, sanitation becomes a health emergency within days.

- **Toilet:** Line the toilet bowl with a heavy-duty trash bag. Add cat litter or sawdust after each use. Seal and store bags outside.
- **Handwashing:** Set up a handwashing station with stored water and soap. Non-negotiable after every toilet use.
- **Trash:** Bag and seal all waste. Store outside, away from the home, off the ground.
- **Greywater:** Dish and wash water can be disposed of in the yard, away from water sources.

## Home First Aid Station

Supplement your personal first aid kit with a home station:

- Full trauma kit (tourniquet, chest seals, hemostatic gauze).
- 30-day supply of all prescription medications.
- OTC medications: pain relievers, antihistamines, anti-diarrheal, electrolyte packets.
- Thermometer, blood pressure cuff, pulse oximeter.
- First aid reference book (not just a phone app — phones die).

## Fire Safety

- Smoke detectors on every level with battery backup.
- Fire extinguishers: kitchen, garage, near any heating source.
- Escape plan with two exits from every room, practiced twice yearly.
- Meeting point outside the home.
- During grid-down scenarios, candle and lantern use increases fire risk dramatically. Use LED lanterns instead.

## Communication Plan

- Establish an out-of-area contact person the entire household can check in with.
- Post the communication plan on the refrigerator.
- Designate two meeting points: one near the home, one outside the neighborhood.
- See [Communication Plans](../preparedness/communication-plans.md) for detailed guidance.

## Document Protection

Store copies of these documents in a fireproof safe and in a separate off-site location:

- Identification (passports, driver's licenses, birth certificates).
- Insurance policies (home, auto, health, life).
- Property deeds and vehicle titles.
- Medical records and prescription lists.
- Financial account information.
- Digital backup: encrypted USB drive or secure cloud storage.

## Shelter-in-Place Supplies

For chemical, biological, or radiological events:

- Plastic sheeting, 4-6 mil thickness, pre-cut to window and door sizes.
- Duct tape.
- N95 or P100 respirators (2 per person).
- NOAA weather radio for alerts.

## Neighborhood Coordination

- Know your neighbors. Exchange phone numbers.
- Identify neighbors with medical training, mechanical skills, generators, or tools.
- Establish a neighborhood check-in plan after a disaster.
- Shared resources multiply capability: one generator can charge devices for several households.

## Common Mistakes

- **Storing water and forgetting it.** Water must be rotated or re-treated annually. Stagnant, untreated water breeds bacteria.
- **No cooking plan.** Raw rice and beans are useless without heat and water to cook them. Match your food storage to your cooking capability.
- **Generator indoors.** Every year people die running generators in garages or basements. Outdoors only, 20 feet (6 m) from the house.
- **Medications not rotated.** Expired medications lose potency. Rotate your emergency supply with your regular prescriptions.
- **Plan exists only in one person's head.** Every household member must know the plan. Write it down. Practice it.
- **Ignoring sanitation.** Waterborne illness from poor sanitation kills more people in prolonged emergencies than the disaster itself.
