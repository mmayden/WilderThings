# Financial Preparedness for Emergencies

> Protect your financial resources and identity documents so you can recover quickly when disaster disrupts normal banking, commerce, and infrastructure.

## At a Glance

- Keep $500-1,000 in small bills ($1, $5, $10, $20) at home in a secure, accessible location.
- Maintain copies of all critical documents in three places: home safe, off-site, and digital.
- Review insurance policies annually — disasters expose coverage gaps you did not know existed.
- Cash is king in the first 72 hours of any disaster. Electronic payment systems fail without power.
- Financial recovery after a disaster is faster when your documents are intact and accessible.

---

## Emergency Cash Reserve

### Why Cash

- ATMs require power and network connectivity. Both fail during disasters.
- Card readers require power and internet. Most go offline immediately.
- Banks may close for days or weeks after a major event.
- Small cash transactions work when everything else does not.

### How Much

- **Minimum:** $200 in small bills for a single person.
- **Recommended:** $500-1,000 per household.
- **Extended disruption:** 30 days of essential expenses in cash if feasible.

### Denomination Mix

| Bill | Quantity (for $500) | Purpose |
|------|-------------------|---------|
| $1 | 20 ($20) | Exact change, vending, small purchases |
| $5 | 20 ($100) | Food, fuel, supplies |
| $10 | 10 ($100) | Medium purchases |
| $20 | 14 ($280) | Larger purchases, lodging |

!!! tip
    Include $10 in quarters. Laundromats, payphones (where they exist), and vending machines may still operate on coin mechanisms during power outages.

### Storage

- Divide cash into two stashes: one in the home safe, one in your bug-out bag.
- Use waterproof containers or zip-lock bags.
- Do not store all cash in one location. Diversify like any financial asset.

## Document Protection

### Critical Documents Checklist

Protect originals and maintain copies of the following:

- [ ] Government-issued photo ID (driver's license, passport)
- [ ] Birth certificates for all household members
- [ ] Social Security cards
- [ ] Marriage certificate / divorce decree
- [ ] Property deeds and vehicle titles
- [ ] Insurance policies (home, auto, health, life, flood)
- [ ] Mortgage and loan documents
- [ ] Bank and investment account numbers
- [ ] Credit card numbers and company phone numbers
- [ ] Tax returns (last 2 years)
- [ ] Wills, powers of attorney, advance directives
- [ ] Medical records and prescription lists
- [ ] Professional licenses and certifications
- [ ] Pet records (vaccination, microchip numbers)

### Three-Location Rule

| Location | Format | Purpose |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Home fireproof safe | Originals + copies | Primary access |
| Off-site (bank safe deposit, trusted relative) | Copies | Survives home destruction |
| Digital (encrypted USB + secure cloud) | Scans / photos | Access from anywhere |

### Digital Backup

1. Scan or photograph every document on the checklist.
2. Store on an encrypted USB drive (use VeraCrypt, BitLocker, or FileVault).
3. Upload to a secure cloud service (Google Drive, iCloud, Dropbox) in an encrypted folder.
4. Update digital copies whenever a document changes.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Never store unencrypted scans of Social Security cards, passports, or financial documents on an unprotected cloud service. Encryption is mandatory.

## Insurance Review

Review all insurance policies annually with these questions:

- **Homeowner's / Renter's:** Does it cover the replacement value of your possessions? Most policies underinsure.
- **Flood:** Standard homeowner's policies do NOT cover flood damage. Separate flood insurance is required in flood-prone areas. FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) has a 30-day waiting period — buy before storm season.
- **Earthquake:** Not included in standard policies in most states. Purchased separately.
- **Auto:** Does your policy cover a total loss at current market value?
- **Health:** Do you know your out-of-network coverage? Disasters often force you to non-preferred providers.
- **Life and Disability:** Updated beneficiaries? Adequate coverage?

!!! note
    Document your possessions with a home inventory. Walk through every room, filming contents. Store the video using the three-location rule. This video is your proof of loss for insurance claims.

## Precious Metals

The precious metals debate is polarizing. Practical considerations:

- Gold and silver are not practical for daily transactions in most disaster scenarios.
- No merchant is equipped to verify authenticity or make change for a gold coin.
- If you choose to hold precious metals, keep them as a long-term store of value, not a short-term transaction medium.
- Small silver rounds (1 oz / 28 g) are more practical than gold for potential barter.
- Cash, food, water, and fuel are more immediately useful than precious metals in the first weeks of any disaster.

## Barter Goods

In extended disruptions, barter economies emerge. High-demand items:

| Category | Items | Trade Value |
|----------|-------|------------|
| Consumables | Batteries, lighters, toilet paper, soap | High — daily need |
| Medical | OTC pain relievers, antibiotic ointment, bandages | High — irreplaceable |
| Vices | Coffee, alcohol, tobacco | Very high — psychological demand |
| Tools | Duct tape, zip ties, tarps | Moderate — practical need |
| Fuel | Gasoline, propane, firewood | Very high — energy |

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Barter involves personal security risk. Never reveal the full extent of your supplies. Trade in neutral locations. Bring a partner.

## Banking Access During Outages

- Keep a paper record of all account numbers, routing numbers, and customer service phone numbers.
- Know your bank's disaster policies — many waive fees and extend payment deadlines after declared emergencies.
- FDIC-insured accounts are protected up to $250,000 per depositor per bank. Your money does not disappear when a branch closes.
- Credit unions and small banks may restore local services faster than national chains.
- Maintain accounts at two separate financial institutions. If one system is down, the other may still function.

## Identity Document Protection

Identity theft surges after disasters. Displaced people are vulnerable targets.

- Carry minimal identification during evacuation — only what you need.
- Shred sensitive documents before abandoning a property if time permits.
- Monitor credit reports after a disaster. Place a fraud alert or credit freeze if documents were exposed.
- The three credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) offer free fraud alerts lasting one year.

## Cryptocurrency Considerations

Cryptocurrency as a disaster financial tool has significant limitations:

- Requires internet access and power to transact — both fail during disasters.
- Volatile value makes it unreliable for planned emergency spending.
- Not widely accepted for essential goods and services.
- Useful as a geographically independent store of value accessible from any device with internet.
- If you hold cryptocurrency, memorize or securely store your recovery phrase using the three-location rule.

## Post-Disaster Financial Recovery

### First 72 Hours

1. Account for all household members. Safety before finances.
2. Document all damage with photographs and video before cleanup.
3. Contact your insurance company to begin the claims process.
4. Use cash for immediate needs.

### First Week

1. Contact your bank to report your situation and request accommodations.
2. Apply for FEMA disaster assistance (disasterassistance.gov or 1-800-621-3362).
3. Contact creditors to request payment deferrals.
4. File a police report if property was looted or documents were stolen.

### First Month

1. Replace critical identification documents (Social Security card, driver's license, passport).
2. Monitor credit reports and place fraud alerts if needed.
3. Track all disaster-related expenses for tax deduction eligibility.
4. Consult with a disaster recovery financial counselor (available free through FEMA and Red Cross).

!!! tip
    Keep all receipts for disaster-related expenses. Unreimbursed disaster losses may be tax-deductible as casualty losses in federally declared disaster areas.

## Common Mistakes

- **No cash on hand.** The most common financial preparedness failure. ATMs and card readers go down first. Keep cash at home.
- **Underinsured.** Most households have inadequate coverage. Review policies annually and photograph your possessions for proof of loss.
- **All documents in one location.** A house fire or flood destroys everything inside. Use the three-location rule.
- **No digital backups.** Paper copies burn, flood, and fade. Encrypted digital copies survive.
- **Ignoring flood insurance.** "It has never flooded here" is not a coverage plan. Flood insurance has a 30-day waiting period — buy before you need it.
- **Revealing supplies during barter.** Never trade from your home. Never reveal your full inventory. Operational security protects your household.
- **Waiting to file insurance claims.** File immediately. Early filers receive faster processing and payouts.

## Quick Reference

| Priority | Action | Timeline |
|----------|--------|----------|
| Keep $500-1,000 in small bills | At home and in bug-out bag | Now |
| Copy all critical documents | Three locations (home, off-site, digital) | Now |
| Review insurance policies | Annual check, update as needed | Annually |
| Create home inventory (video) | Walk-through of every room | Now, update annually |
| Place fraud alert if documents lost | Contact one credit bureau (it notifies others) | Within 24 hours |
| File FEMA assistance | disasterassistance.gov or 1-800-621-3362 | First week post-disaster |
| Track all disaster expenses | Save receipts for tax purposes | Ongoing |
