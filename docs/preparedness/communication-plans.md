# Emergency Communication Plans

> Establish reliable communication methods so your household can coordinate, report status, and call for help when normal channels fail.

## At a Glance

- Designate an out-of-area contact person — long-distance calls often work when local lines are jammed.
- Establish two physical meeting points: one near home, one outside the neighborhood.
- Text messages get through when voice calls cannot. Text first, call second.
- Own at least one communication method that does not depend on cell towers or the internet.
- Write the plan down, distribute copies, and practice it.

---

## Family Communication Plan

### Step 1: Designate an Out-of-Area Contact

Choose a trusted person who lives at least 100 miles (160 km) away. This person serves as a message relay hub.

- Every household member memorizes this person's phone number.
- After a disaster, each person checks in with the out-of-area contact by text or call.
- The contact relays status updates between household members who cannot reach each other directly.

!!! note
    Local cell towers overload within minutes of a major event. Long-distance connections to an out-of-area contact are far more likely to succeed.

### Step 2: Set Meeting Points

| Meeting Point | Location | When to Use |
|--------------|----------|-------------|
| Point A (near home) | Front yard, mailbox, or neighbor's house | Home evacuation, local emergency |
| Point B (neighborhood edge) | School, church, park within walking distance | Neighborhood-level evacuation |
| Point C (out of area) | Relative's home, predetermined town | Regional evacuation |

### Step 3: Write and Distribute the Plan

Include on a wallet-sized card for each household member:

- Out-of-area contact: name, phone, address.
- Meeting points A, B, and C with addresses.
- Household member phone numbers.
- Local emergency numbers (police, fire, hospital, utility companies).
- Medical information (allergies, medications, blood type).

!!! tip
    Laminate the card or seal it in packing tape. Paper disintegrates when wet.

### Step 4: Practice

- Run a communication drill every 6 months.
- Each household member texts the out-of-area contact with a status report.
- Meet at Point A and walk to Point B.
- Debrief: what worked, what did not, what needs updating.

## Radio Options

When cell towers, internet, and landlines are down, radio becomes your primary communication tool.

### Radio Types

| Radio Type | Range | License Required | Best For |
|-----------|-------|-----------------|----------|
| FRS (Family Radio Service) | 0.5-2 miles (0.8-3.2 km) | No | Family, short range |
| GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) | 2-25 miles (3.2-40 km) with repeater | Yes (FCC license, no exam) | Family, neighborhood |
| CB (Citizens Band) | 3-10 miles (5-16 km) | No | Vehicle convoys, truckers |
| Ham (Amateur Radio) | Local to worldwide | Yes (FCC license, exam required) | Long-range, emergency nets |

### FRS and GMRS

- FRS radios are inexpensive ($20-50 per pair) and legal to use without a license.
- GMRS radios offer more power (up to 50 watts) and repeater access. One license covers the entire household.
- Pre-program channel and privacy code for your family. Write them on the communication card.

!!! tip
    FRS Channel 1 with no privacy code is the most commonly monitored family channel. Use it if you need to reach strangers for help.

### Ham Radio

- Technician license (entry level) requires a 35-question multiple-choice exam. Study materials are free online.
- Technician class operators can access local repeaters (extends range to 50+ miles / 80+ km) and VHF/UHF frequencies.
- General and Extra class licenses unlock HF frequencies for continental and worldwide communication.
- During disasters, ham radio operators activate emergency nets. Frequencies 146.520 MHz (VHF national calling) and 446.000 MHz (UHF national calling) are monitored.

### CB Radio

- No license required. 40 channels available.
- Channel 9 is the universal emergency channel.
- Channel 19 is monitored by truckers — useful along highways.
- Limited range but ubiquitous. Nearly every truck stop and many vehicles have CB.

## Phone Alternatives When Towers Are Down

### Text Before Voice

Text messages (SMS) use a fraction of the bandwidth of voice calls. During network congestion, texts queue and deliver when capacity opens. Voice calls are dropped.

### Wi-Fi Calling

If cell towers are down but you have Wi-Fi (via generator or battery backup on your router), enable Wi-Fi calling on your phone. This routes calls and texts over the internet.

### Landlines

Traditional copper landlines (POTS — Plain Old Telephone Service) have independent power and often survive events that destroy cell towers. If you have one, keep a corded phone that does not require wall power.

### Satellite Communicators

| Device | Monthly Cost | Two-Way Text | SOS | Coverage |
|--------|-------------|-------------|-----|----------|
| Garmin inReach Mini 2 | $12-65 | Yes | Yes | Global (Iridium) |
| SPOT Gen4 | $12-50 | No (one-way) | Yes | Global (Globalstar) |
| Zoleo | $20-50 | Yes | Yes | Global (Iridium) |
| Apple iPhone 14+ (Emergency SOS) | Included | Limited | Yes | Limited (satellite) |

Satellite communicators work anywhere on Earth with a clear view of the sky. They are the most reliable emergency communication tool available.

!!! note
    Satellite communicators require a subscription plan. Activate and test before you need them. An untested device in a crisis is an expensive paperweight.

### Mesh Networking

Devices like goTenna and Meshtastic create ad-hoc networks between users without any infrastructure. Each device relays messages to the next, extending range.

- Range: 1-4 miles (1.6-6.4 km) per hop, multiple hops possible.
- No subscription required.
- Works best with a local group or neighborhood.
- Text-only, no voice.

## Written Message Protocols

When all electronic communication fails, use written messages.

- Leave notes at meeting points in a predetermined, weatherproof location (sealed in a zip-lock bag under a specific rock, inside a mailbox).
- Use a standard format: **Date, Time, Name, Location, Status, Destination, ETA.**
- Mark cleared buildings with the FEMA X-Code system or a simplified version your group understands.

## Visual and Audible Signals

- **Signal mirror:** Visible up to 10 miles (16 km) in sunlight. Aim the reflection at aircraft or rescue teams.
- **Whistle:** Three short blasts is the universal distress signal. Audible at 1 mile (1.6 km).
- **Bright cloth or panel:** Lay out a brightly colored tarp, sheet, or clothing in an open area to attract aerial attention.
- **Smoke:** A smoky fire (add green vegetation to flames) is visible for miles during daylight.

See [Signaling for Rescue](../navigation/signaling-for-rescue.md) for detailed techniques.

## Radio Check-In Schedule

Establish a schedule for radio check-ins:

1. Designate a primary frequency/channel and a backup.
2. Set check-in times: every 4 hours during daylight (e.g., 0800, 1200, 1600, 2000).
3. Each person transmits: call sign or name, location, status (OK / need help / moving to [destination]).
4. If no contact after 3 missed check-ins, the group initiates the search plan.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not transmit sensitive information (exact home address, security details, supply inventory) over open radio frequencies. Anyone can listen.

## Common Mistakes

- **Plan exists only in your head.** If you are incapacitated, nobody else knows the plan. Write it down and distribute it.
- **Only one communication method.** Cell phones are a single point of failure. Own at least one backup (radio, satellite, written protocol).
- **Untested radios.** Buy radios and test range in your area before an emergency. Terrain, buildings, and vegetation reduce stated ranges dramatically.
- **No out-of-area contact.** This is the single most effective element of a family communication plan. Set it up today.
- **Transmitting personal details on open frequencies.** Assume every radio transmission is public. Use code words for sensitive locations if needed.
- **Dead batteries.** Radios and satellite devices are useless without power. Stock spare batteries and keep devices charged.
- **Skipping practice.** A plan that has never been practiced fails under stress. Drill twice a year.
