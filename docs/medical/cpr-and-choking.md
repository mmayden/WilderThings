# CPR and Choking Response

> Restore circulation and clear airway obstructions using standardized resuscitation protocols when professional help is delayed or unavailable.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace hands-on CPR certification from the American Heart Association, Red Cross, or equivalent authority. Practice on manikins before you need these skills for real.

## At a Glance

- Push hard, push fast — compress the chest at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep at 100-120 compressions per minute for adults.
- Hands-only CPR (compressions without breaths) is effective and recommended for untrained bystanders.
- Begin CPR immediately if someone is unresponsive and not breathing normally — every minute without CPR reduces survival by 7-10%.
- Abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver) clear most choking obstructions in conscious adults and children.
- Infant choking protocol uses back blows and chest thrusts, never abdominal thrusts.

---

## When to Start CPR

Start CPR when a person meets **all** of the following criteria:

1. Unresponsive — does not react to shouting or a firm tap on the shoulders.
2. Not breathing normally — no chest rise, only gasping (agonal respirations), or no breath sounds.
3. No definite pulse within 10 seconds (check the carotid artery in the neck for adults/children, the brachial artery in the upper arm for infants).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Agonal breathing (irregular gasps, gurgling, or snoring sounds) is NOT normal breathing. It occurs in up to 40% of cardiac arrest victims. Begin CPR immediately if you see agonal breathing.

If you are unsure whether there is a pulse, start CPR. Performing CPR on someone with a pulse is far less harmful than withholding CPR from someone without one.

## Adult CPR (Age 12+)

### Preparation

1. Confirm the scene is safe.
2. Check responsiveness — shout "Are you okay?" and tap both shoulders firmly.
3. Call for help. Activate emergency medical services. Send someone for an AED if available.
4. Place the patient face-up on a firm, flat surface.

### Compressions

1. Kneel beside the patient's chest.
2. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, on the lower half of the sternum (breastbone).
3. Place your other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
4. Position your shoulders directly above your hands. Lock your elbows.
5. Compress straight down at least 2 inches (5 cm) but no more than 2.4 inches (6 cm).
6. Allow full chest recoil between compressions — do not lean on the chest.
7. Compress at a rate of 100-120 per minute (the tempo of "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees).
8. Minimize interruptions. Pauses in compressions should not exceed 10 seconds.

### Rescue Breaths (if trained)

1. After 30 compressions, open the airway using the head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver.
   - Place one hand on the forehead and tilt the head back.
   - Place two fingers under the bony part of the chin and lift upward.
2. Pinch the nose shut.
3. Create a complete seal over the patient's mouth with yours.
4. Deliver 2 breaths, each lasting about 1 second.
5. Watch for visible chest rise. If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and try again.
6. Resume compressions immediately after the second breath.

**Compression-to-breath ratio: 30:2** (30 compressions, 2 breaths, repeat).

### Hands-Only CPR

If you are untrained, unwilling to give breaths, or unable to create a seal:

1. Perform continuous chest compressions at 100-120 per minute.
2. Do not stop to give breaths.
3. Continue until help arrives, an AED is available, or the patient shows signs of life.

Hands-only CPR is nearly as effective as conventional CPR for the first several minutes of adult cardiac arrest.

### AED Use

1. Turn on the AED and follow voice prompts.
2. Expose the chest. Remove clothing, wipe dry if wet, shave excessive hair if pads do not adhere.
3. Apply pads: one on the upper right chest (below the collarbone), one on the lower left side (below the armpit).
4. Ensure no one is touching the patient during analysis and shock delivery.
5. If the AED advises a shock, confirm everyone is clear and press the shock button.
6. Resume CPR immediately after the shock — start with compressions.
7. The AED will re-analyze every 2 minutes.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not use an AED in standing water. Move the patient to a dry surface first. Moisture on the chest between the pads can cause arcing — dry the chest thoroughly before applying pads.

## Child CPR (Age 1-12)

The protocol is similar to adult CPR with these modifications:

### Compressions

1. Use one or two hands (depending on the child's size) on the lower half of the sternum.
2. Compress to a depth of about 2 inches (5 cm), or one-third the depth of the chest.
3. Rate: 100-120 per minute.
4. Allow full chest recoil.

### Rescue Breaths

1. Open the airway with head-tilt/chin-lift. Use a neutral (sniffing) position — do not over-extend the neck.
2. Deliver smaller breaths than for an adult — just enough to see the chest rise.
3. Ratio: **30:2** for a single rescuer, **15:2** for two rescuers.

### Key Difference

For children, respiratory failure is the most common cause of cardiac arrest (unlike adults, where it is typically cardiac). Rescue breaths are more important in pediatric CPR. Give 2 initial rescue breaths before starting compressions if the arrest is witnessed to be respiratory in origin.

## Infant CPR (Under Age 1)

### Compressions

1. Place the infant on a firm, flat surface.
2. **Two-finger technique (single rescuer):** Place two fingers on the sternum, just below the nipple line.
3. **Two-thumb technique (two rescuers):** Encircle the chest with both hands, thumbs side by side on the sternum just below the nipple line. This is the preferred method when two rescuers are available.
4. Compress to a depth of about 1.5 inches (4 cm), or one-third the depth of the chest.
5. Rate: 100-120 per minute.
6. Allow full chest recoil.

### Rescue Breaths

1. Place the infant's head in a neutral position (do not tilt back).
2. Cover the infant's mouth AND nose with your mouth.
3. Deliver tiny puffs of air — just enough to see the chest rise. Over-inflation causes gastric distension and vomiting.
4. Ratio: **30:2** for a single rescuer, **15:2** for two rescuers.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not shake an infant to check responsiveness. Flick the sole of the foot or tap the shoulder gently. Shaking can cause brain injury.

## CPR Duration and Stopping

Continue CPR until:

- Emergency medical services take over.
- An AED becomes available and delivers analysis/shock.
- The patient shows definite signs of life (purposeful movement, normal breathing).
- You are too physically exhausted to continue (switch rescuers every 2 minutes if possible).
- A qualified medical professional pronounces death.
- The scene becomes unsafe.

### Rescuer Fatigue

Effective compressions degrade after approximately 2 minutes, even when the rescuer does not feel tired. Rotate compressors every 2 minutes. Switch in less than 10 seconds.

## Recovery Position

Place an unconscious, breathing patient in the recovery position to maintain an open airway and allow fluids to drain.

1. Kneel beside the patient.
2. Place the arm nearest to you at a right angle to the body, palm up.
3. Bring the far arm across the chest and hold the back of their hand against the near cheek.
4. Grasp the far knee and pull it up so the foot is flat on the ground.
5. Pull the far knee toward you, rolling the patient onto their side.
6. Adjust the top leg so the hip and knee are at right angles.
7. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open.
8. Monitor breathing continuously.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not place a patient in the recovery position if you suspect a spinal injury. Use the jaw-thrust maneuver to open the airway and maintain spinal alignment. See [Fractures and Splints](fractures-and-splints.md) for spinal precautions.

## Choking: Conscious Adult and Child (Over Age 1)

### Recognizing Choking

- Universal choking sign: hands clutching the throat.
- Inability to speak, cough, or breathe.
- High-pitched sounds or wheezing when trying to inhale.
- Skin turning blue (cyanosis), especially lips and fingertips.
- If the patient can cough forcefully or speak, encourage continued coughing. Do not intervene physically — a strong cough generates more airway pressure than any maneuver you can perform.

### Heimlich Maneuver (Abdominal Thrusts)

1. Stand behind the patient.
2. Make a fist with one hand. Place the thumb side against the abdomen, above the navel and below the sternum (xiphoid process).
3. Grasp the fist with your other hand.
4. Deliver quick, sharp, inward-and-upward thrusts.
5. Each thrust should be a distinct, forceful movement — not a squeeze.
6. Continue until the object is expelled or the patient becomes unconscious.

### Chest Thrusts (Alternative)

Use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts for:

- Pregnant patients (late pregnancy).
- Obese patients whose abdomen you cannot encircle.

1. Stand behind the patient.
2. Place your fist on the center of the sternum (breastbone), between the nipples.
3. Grasp with the other hand.
4. Deliver sharp, backward thrusts.

### Self-Administered Heimlich

If you are alone and choking:

1. Make a fist and place it above your navel.
2. Grasp with the other hand and thrust inward and upward.
3. Alternatively, lean your upper abdomen over the back of a chair, countertop edge, or railing and thrust your body weight forward and downward.
4. Repeat forcefully until the obstruction clears.

## Choking: Conscious Infant (Under Age 1)

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never perform abdominal thrusts on an infant. The liver is proportionally large and unprotected — abdominal thrusts can cause fatal internal injury.

### Back Blows and Chest Thrusts

1. Hold the infant face-down along your forearm, supporting the head and jaw with your hand. Rest your forearm on your thigh for stability. Keep the infant's head lower than the chest.
2. Deliver 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades using the heel of your free hand.
3. Turn the infant face-up on your other forearm, keeping the head lower than the chest.
4. Deliver 5 chest thrusts using two fingers on the sternum, just below the nipple line. Use the same landmark and depth as infant CPR compressions.
5. Repeat the cycle of 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts until:
   - The object is expelled.
   - The infant begins to cry or breathe.
   - The infant becomes unconscious (switch to CPR — see below).

## Choking: Unconscious Victim (Any Age)

If a choking victim becomes unconscious:

1. Lower the patient to the ground carefully on their back.
2. Begin CPR immediately, starting with compressions.
3. **Before each set of rescue breaths**, open the mouth and look for a visible foreign object.
4. If you see an object, sweep it out with a finger. Use a hooked finger motion along the inside of the cheek.
5. Do not perform blind finger sweeps — you risk pushing the object deeper.
6. Attempt rescue breaths. If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and try again.
7. If breaths still do not go in, continue compressions. The compressions themselves may dislodge the object.
8. Continue this cycle until the obstruction clears or help arrives.

## Special Situations

### Drowning

1. Remove the patient from the water safely — do not become a victim.
2. Begin rescue breaths first: deliver 5 initial breaths before starting compressions.
3. Follow standard CPR protocol (30:2).
4. Do not attempt to drain water from the lungs — the Heimlich maneuver is not indicated for drowning unless a foreign body is suspected.
5. Vomiting is common during drowning resuscitation. Turn the head to the side, clear the airway, and continue.

### Opioid Overdose

1. Suspected opioid overdose signs: pinpoint pupils, slow or absent breathing, unresponsiveness.
2. Administer naloxone (Narcan) if available: 4 mg intranasal spray in one nostril, or 0.4 mg intramuscular injection.
3. Begin CPR if the patient is not breathing and has no pulse.
4. If the patient resumes breathing but remains unconscious, place in the recovery position.
5. Naloxone wears off in 30-90 minutes. The patient may stop breathing again. Monitor continuously.

### Hypothermic Cardiac Arrest

1. Check for a pulse for 60 seconds (not the standard 10) — the heart rate may be extremely slow.
2. If no pulse is detected, begin CPR.
3. Rewarm the patient — see [Hypothermia and Heatstroke](hypothermia-and-heatstroke.md).
4. Do not pronounce a hypothermic patient dead in the field. The principle: "They're not dead until they're warm and dead."

## Common Mistakes

- Compressing too shallow — this is the most common CPR error. Push hard enough to compress the chest at least 2 inches (5 cm) in adults.
- Compressing too fast — exceeding 120/min reduces refill time and decreases cardiac output.
- Leaning on the chest between compressions — this prevents full recoil and reduces blood flow by up to 25%.
- Interrupting compressions too long for breaths or pulse checks — keep pauses under 10 seconds.
- Hyperventilating the patient — excessive breaths increase intrathoracic pressure and reduce venous return. One breath per second, visible chest rise only.
- Performing abdominal thrusts on infants — use back blows and chest thrusts only.
- Performing blind finger sweeps on choking victims — visualize the object before sweeping.
- Stopping CPR when agonal breathing occurs — agonal breaths do not indicate recovery.
- Failing to tilt the head back sufficiently when delivering rescue breaths (the most common cause of failed ventilation).

## Quick Reference

### CPR at a Glance

| Parameter | Adult (12+) | Child (1-12) | Infant (Under 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compression depth | 2-2.4 in (5-6 cm) | ~2 in (5 cm) | ~1.5 in (4 cm) |
| Compression rate | 100-120/min | 100-120/min | 100-120/min |
| Hand position | 2 hands, center of chest | 1-2 hands, center of chest | 2 fingers or 2 thumbs, just below nipple line |
| Ratio (1 rescuer) | 30:2 | 30:2 | 30:2 |
| Ratio (2 rescuers) | 30:2 | 15:2 | 15:2 |
| Breath volume | Normal breath | Small breath | Tiny puff |

### Choking at a Glance

| Patient | Conscious | Unconscious |
|---|---|---|
| Adult | Abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) | CPR with airway checks |
| Child | Abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) | CPR with airway checks |
| Infant | 5 back blows + 5 chest thrusts | CPR with airway checks |
| Pregnant/Obese | Chest thrusts | CPR with airway checks |
| Self | Abdominal thrusts or lean over chair | N/A |
