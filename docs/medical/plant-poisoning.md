# Plant Poisoning: Recognition and Field Treatment

> Identify plant poisoning symptoms, administer first aid for ingestion and contact exposure, and avoid commonly confused toxic species.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Plant poisoning can be fatal. Never eat any plant you cannot identify with 100% certainty. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- When in doubt, do not eat it. No survival situation improves by adding a poisoning emergency.
- Do NOT induce vomiting for most plant poisonings — many plant toxins cause more damage on the way back up.
- Activated charcoal (1 g per kg body weight, or roughly 50 g for an adult) is the primary field treatment for ingestion if given within 1 hour.
- Cardiac and neurological symptoms after plant ingestion are immediate evacuation criteria.
- Poison ivy, oak, and sumac cause contact dermatitis — wash skin with soap and cool water within 10 minutes of exposure to prevent rash. Speed matters more than temperature.

## Types of Plant Poisoning

### Gastrointestinal Toxicity

The most common presentation. Toxins irritate or damage the GI tract lining.

**Symptoms:** Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramping, diarrhea (sometimes bloody). Onset typically 30 minutes to 6 hours after ingestion.

**Caused by:** Pokeweed berries, holly berries, daffodil bulbs, horse chestnut, many unidentified mushrooms.

### Neurological Toxicity

Toxins affect the central or peripheral nervous system.

**Symptoms:** Blurred vision, dilated or constricted pupils, dizziness, tremors, seizures, hallucinations, numbness/tingling, paralysis. Onset ranges from 15 minutes to several hours.

**Caused by:** Water hemlock, poison hemlock, jimsonweed (Datura), nightshade (*Atropa belladonna*), yew (*Taxus*).

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Water hemlock (*Cicuta*) is the most violently toxic plant in North America. Seizures begin within 15-60 minutes of ingestion and can be fatal. Evacuate immediately.

### Cardiac Toxicity

Toxins interfere with heart rhythm. These are the most immediately life-threatening.

**Symptoms:** Slow or irregular heartbeat, palpitations, low blood pressure, dizziness, chest pain, cardiac arrest. Onset varies: 30 minutes to several hours.

**Caused by:** Foxglove (*Digitalis*), oleander, lily of the valley, rhododendron/azalea, yew berries and foliage, monkshood (*Aconitum*).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Cardiac toxins have no field antidote. If any plant ingestion is followed by abnormal pulse or chest symptoms, evacuate immediately.

### Dermatological Toxicity (Contact)

Toxins cause skin inflammation on contact, without ingestion.

**Symptoms:** Itching, redness, blistering (urushiol reactions), burns (sap reactions), photosensitive rash. Onset: minutes to 72 hours.

**Caused by:** Poison ivy/oak/sumac (urushiol), giant hogweed (phototoxic sap), stinging nettle, wild parsnip.

## First Aid for Plant Ingestion

### Immediate Actions

1. Stop eating the plant. Remove any remaining plant material from the mouth.
2. Save a sample of the plant — leaves, berries, roots, flowers. Wrap in a cloth or bag for identification.
3. Note the time of ingestion and the amount consumed (even a rough estimate).
4. Rinse the mouth thoroughly with water. Spit it out — do not swallow.

### Activated Charcoal

Activated charcoal binds many plant toxins in the GI tract and prevents absorption. It is most effective within 1 hour of ingestion but may help up to 4 hours.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do NOT give activated charcoal to an unconscious or seizing patient — aspiration risk is lethal. Do NOT give if the patient has ingested a corrosive substance (burning/blistering of the mouth).

1. Mix 50 g of activated charcoal powder (roughly 1 g per kg body weight for adults) in 8 oz (250 mL) of water.
2. Stir into a slurry. It tastes gritty and unpleasant.
3. Have the patient drink it all. If the patient vomits, wait 10 minutes and re-dose.
4. Children: 1 g per kg body weight, or 25 g for small children.

### Vomiting

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Do NOT induce vomiting unless specifically instructed by poison control or a physician. Most plant toxins cause additional damage to the esophagus on the way back up. Seizure-causing plants (water hemlock, poison hemlock) create aspiration risk if the patient vomits and then seizes.

If vomiting occurs spontaneously:

1. Position the patient on their side (recovery position) to protect the airway.
2. Do not suppress spontaneous vomiting unless the patient is at risk of aspiration.
3. Save vomit for identification if possible.

### Monitoring

After any plant ingestion:

- Check pulse every 15 minutes for irregularities.
- Monitor breathing rate and mental status.
- Watch for progressive symptoms (escalation from GI to neuro or cardiac).
- Keep the patient hydrated with small sips of water.

### Evacuation Criteria

Evacuate immediately if any of the following develop:

- Seizures or tremors.
- Irregular, slow, or rapid heartbeat.
- Difficulty breathing.
- Confusion or altered mental status.
- Loss of consciousness.
- Severe or persistent vomiting preventing fluid intake.
- Known ingestion of water hemlock, oleander, foxglove, yew, or monkshood.

## Contact Dermatitis

### Poison Ivy, Poison Oak, and Poison Sumac

All three plants produce urushiol, an oily resin that causes allergic contact dermatitis in approximately 85% of people. The rash is not contagious and does not spread from blister fluid.

**Identification:**

- **Poison ivy** (*Toxicodendron radicans*): "Leaves of three, let it be." Grows as a vine or shrub. Eastern and central North America.
- **Poison oak** (*Toxicodendron diversilobum*): Similar leaf-of-three pattern, oak-shaped leaves. Western North America.
- **Poison sumac** (*Toxicodendron vernix*): 7-13 leaflets per stem, smooth-edged. Swamps and wetlands of eastern North America.

!!! note
    Urushiol remains active on clothing, tools, pet fur, and dead plants for months to years. Wash all contaminated items.

**Prevention:**

- Learn to identify all three plants in all seasons (they look different in spring, summer, and fall).
- Wear long sleeves and pants in areas where these plants grow.
- Apply barrier creams (bentoquatam/IvyBlock) before exposure if available.

**Treatment:**

1. Wash the exposed skin with soap and cool water (or rubbing alcohol if available) within 10 minutes of contact. Speed is what matters — urushiol binds to skin within about 10-30 minutes, and soap is what lifts the oil off.
2. Remove and bag contaminated clothing.
3. Avoid hot water while washing off the oil — it increases blood flow and spreads the oil more easily. (Once a rash is established and the urushiol is long gone, hot water is harmless and many people find it relieves itching temporarily.)
4. If a rash develops (typically 12-72 hours after exposure): apply calamine lotion or hydrocortisone cream.
5. Take oral antihistamines (diphenhydramine 25-50 mg every 6 hours) for itching.
6. Cool compresses relieve itch and inflammation.
7. Do not break blisters — intact blisters protect against infection.
8. Rash resolves in 1-3 weeks without treatment.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Burning poison ivy, oak, or sumac releases urushiol in smoke. Inhaling the smoke causes severe lung inflammation and is a medical emergency. Never burn brush that may contain these plants.

### Giant Hogweed and Wild Parsnip

These plants cause phytophotodermatitis — sap on skin combined with sunlight produces severe chemical burns and blistering.

**Treatment:**

1. Wash sap off skin immediately with soap and water.
2. Cover the affected area to block sunlight for 48 hours.
3. If burns develop, treat as second-degree burns: cool water, clean dressing, pain management.
4. Blistering and hyperpigmentation can last months.

## Universal Edibility Test

The full protocol lives in one place — **[Foraging Basics](../food/foraging-basics.md#the-universal-edibility-test)** — so there is only ever one version to follow. Read the warnings there before using it.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    The Universal Edibility Test does NOT detect all toxins, including several that kill.

    - **Water hemlock, poison hemlock, death camas, foxglove, oleander, monkshood, and yew** can pass the tasting stages. Some taste mild or pleasant.
    - Cardiac glycosides (oleander, foxglove) and amatoxins (death cap) produce no immediate reaction and kill hours to days later.
    - **Never use this test on mushrooms.**

    Treat it as a last resort when starvation is a genuine threat, not as a way to make an unknown plant safe. Positive identification of a few known species is always better.

!!! note
    Cooking can neutralize some toxins but concentrate others. If you plan to eat the plant cooked, perform the test with the cooked version.

## Commonly Confused Edible and Toxic Species

### North America

| Edible | Toxic Look-Alike | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) | Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*) | Hemlock has smooth purple-blotched stems; wild carrot has hairy green stems |
| Wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace) | Water hemlock (*Cicuta*) | Water hemlock grows in wet areas, has chambered root with yellow resin |
| Elderberry (*Sambucus nigra*) | Water hemlock (*Cicuta*) | Elderberry has opposite compound leaves and flat-topped flower clusters |
| Wild garlic/onion | Death camas (*Zigadenus*) | Wild onion always smells like onion; death camas has no onion odor |
| Blueberries | Nightshade berries | Blueberries have a crown (calyx) on the end; nightshade berries are smooth |
| Morel mushrooms | False morel (*Gyromitra*) | True morels are hollow inside when sliced lengthwise; false morels have chambered, cotton-like interior |

### Europe

| Edible | Toxic Look-Alike | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Wild garlic (ramsons) | Lily of the valley | Ramsons smell strongly of garlic when crushed; lily of the valley does not |
| Wild garlic (ramsons) | Autumn crocus (*Colchicum*) | Ramsons appear in spring; autumn crocus leaves appear in spring but lack garlic odor |
| Chanterelle mushrooms | Jack-o'-lantern (*Omphalotus*) | Chanterelles have false gills (blunt ridges); jack-o'-lanterns have true blade-like gills |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If you cannot distinguish the edible species from its toxic look-alike with 100% certainty, do not eat it. A single misidentified plant can be fatal.

## Common Mistakes

- **Inducing vomiting after plant ingestion.** Most plant toxins cause additional esophageal and airway damage when vomited. Use activated charcoal instead.
- **Assuming a plant is safe because animals eat it.** Many animals tolerate toxins that are lethal to humans. Deer eat yew and poison ivy without harm.
- **Relying on taste as a safety indicator.** Many lethal plants (death camas, water hemlock root) taste mild or even pleasant.
- **Applying the universal edibility test to mushrooms.** Amatoxin (death cap, destroying angel) causes no symptoms for 6-24 hours, then destroys the liver. The edibility test cannot detect it in time.
- **Washing off urushiol slowly, or without soap.** The oil binds to skin within 10-30 minutes. Soap and cool water within 10 minutes is what prevents the rash — not water temperature alone. ("Hot water opens your pores" is a myth; pores do not open and close with temperature.)
- **Assuming all parts of a "safe" plant are edible.** Rhubarb stalks are edible; rhubarb leaves contain lethal levels of oxalic acid. Elderberries are edible when cooked; raw berries, leaves, and stems are toxic.
- **Burning brush containing poison ivy/oak.** Urushiol in smoke causes severe respiratory inflammation. Clear these plants by pulling (with gloves) or herbicide — never fire.

## Quick Reference

**Plant Ingestion Protocol:**

1. Remove plant from mouth. Save a sample.
2. Rinse mouth with water.
3. Give activated charcoal (50 g in water) within 1 hour if conscious.
4. Do NOT induce vomiting.
5. Monitor pulse, breathing, mental status every 15 minutes.
6. Evacuate if: seizures, irregular heartbeat, breathing difficulty, confusion.

**Contact Dermatitis Protocol (Poison Ivy/Oak/Sumac):**

1. Wash with soap and cool water within 10 minutes — speed matters more than temperature.
2. Remove contaminated clothing.
3. Apply calamine or hydrocortisone for rash.
4. Antihistamines for itching.
5. Do not break blisters.

**Universal Edibility Test:** see [Foraging Basics](../food/foraging-basics.md#the-universal-edibility-test) for the protocol (~24 hours per plant part) and its limits. It cannot detect water hemlock, poison hemlock, death camas, foxglove, oleander, monkshood, or yew.
