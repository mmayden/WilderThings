# Bites and Stings: Identification and Field Treatment

> Recognize, treat, and triage venomous and non-venomous bites and stings when professional medical care is unavailable.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace
    professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical
    provider. Envenomation can be fatal. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Most bites and stings are not life-threatening — anaphylaxis and envenomation are the two emergencies that kill.
- Never cut, suck, or tourniquet a bite wound. Immobilize the limb, keep it at or below heart level, and evacuate.
- Recognize anaphylaxis early: hives, throat swelling, wheezing, dropping blood pressure. Administer epinephrine immediately.
- Remove stingers and ticks promptly — delay worsens envenomation and disease transmission.
- Photograph or clearly note the animal's appearance if safe to do so. Identification guides treatment.

## Anaphylaxis

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Anaphylaxis can kill within minutes. Treat it before addressing the bite or sting itself.

### Recognition

Anaphylaxis typically develops within 5-30 minutes of exposure. Watch for:

- **Skin:** Widespread hives, flushing, itching, swelling beyond the sting site.
- **Airway:** Throat tightness, hoarse voice, stridor, difficulty swallowing.
- **Breathing:** Wheezing, shortness of breath, rapid breathing.
- **Circulation:** Rapid weak pulse, dizziness, pale/blue skin, loss of consciousness.

Two or more body systems involved after a known exposure = anaphylaxis. Act immediately.

### Epinephrine Administration

1. Use an auto-injector (EpiPen) or draw 0.3 mg (adult) / 0.15 mg (child under 66 lbs / 30 kg) of 1:1,000 epinephrine.
2. Inject into the outer mid-thigh. Inject through clothing if necessary.
3. Hold in place for 10 seconds.
4. Note the time of injection.
5. If no improvement in 5-15 minutes, administer a second dose.
6. Position the patient lying down with legs elevated unless breathing is difficult — then allow them to sit upright.
7. Evacuate immediately. Anaphylaxis can recur after appearing to resolve (biphasic reaction), typically 1-12 hours later and occasionally up to 72 hours. Feeling better is not the same as being out of danger.

!!! tip
    Administer diphenhydramine (Benadryl) 25-50 mg orally as a secondary measure if available. It does not replace epinephrine.

## Bite and Sting Treatment by Type

=== "Snake Bites"

    ### Snake Bites

    !!! danger "WARNING"
        Do NOT cut the wound, suck out venom, apply a tourniquet, apply ice, or use electric shock. All are harmful and none work.

    #### Identification

    Note these features if observed safely:

    - Head shape (triangular vs. rounded).
    - Pupil shape (vertical slits vs. round).
    - Fang marks: two distinct punctures suggest a venomous pit viper; smaller tooth rows suggest non-venomous.
    - Coloring and pattern. Photograph if possible.

    !!! note
        An estimated 20-50% of venomous snake bites are "dry bites" with no venom injected. Treat every bite as envenomation until proven otherwise.

    #### Immediate Treatment

    1. Move the victim away from the snake's striking range (at least 6 feet / 2 m).
    2. Keep the victim calm and still. Increased heart rate accelerates venom spread.
    3. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing from the bitten limb before swelling begins.
    4. Immobilize the bitten limb in a neutral position at or slightly below heart level.
    5. Mark the leading edge of swelling with a pen and note the time.
    6. Clean the wound gently with water. Do not scrub.
    7. Apply a pressure immobilization bandage (PIB) for elapid bites only (coral snakes, cobras, mambas): wrap the entire limb firmly with an elastic bandage from the bite toward the heart at the pressure of an ankle sprain wrap.

    !!! warning "CAUTION"
        Do NOT use a pressure immobilization bandage for pit viper bites (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths). Trapping cytotoxic venom in tissue increases local damage.

    #### Evacuation Criteria

    Evacuate ALL suspected venomous snake bites. Antivenom is the definitive treatment and cannot be replicated in the field.

    #### Regional Notes

    - **North America:** Pit vipers (rattlesnake, copperhead, cottonmouth) and coral snakes. "Red touches yellow, kill a fellow" is unreliable outside the U.S.
    - **Australia:** Assume all snake bites are elapid. Apply PIB immediately.
    - **Southeast Asia/Africa:** Many species. Assume venomous until confirmed otherwise.

    See [Venomous Snakes](../wildlife/venomous-snakes.md) for species identification.

=== "Spider Bites"

    ### Spider Bites

    Most spider bites cause only local pain and swelling. Two genera pose serious threats in North America: *Latrodectus* (widow spiders) and *Loxosceles* (recluse spiders).

    #### Black Widow (*Latrodectus*)

    **Identification:** Shiny black body, red hourglass marking on underside (females). Found throughout North America, especially in undisturbed sheltered areas.

    **Symptoms:** Sharp pinprick pain at the bite. Within 30-60 minutes: severe muscle cramping (especially abdomen and back), sweating, nausea, elevated blood pressure, difficulty breathing.

    **Treatment:**

    1. Clean the bite with soap and water.
    2. Apply a cold pack (wrapped, not directly on skin) for 10 minutes on, 10 minutes off.
    3. Administer ibuprofen or acetaminophen for pain.
    4. Monitor for systemic symptoms for 6 hours.
    5. Evacuate if muscle cramps are severe, if the victim is a child or elderly, or if breathing difficulty develops.

    #### Brown Recluse (*Loxosceles*)

    **Identification:** Light brown, violin-shaped marking on cephalothorax, six eyes in three pairs. Found in south-central and southeastern U.S.

    **Symptoms:** Mild sting at bite, often unnoticed. Over 2-8 hours: increasing pain, central blister, expanding area of redness. Over days: necrotic ulcer with purple/black center (not all bites necrose).

    **Treatment:**

    1. Clean with soap and water.
    2. Apply a cold pack intermittently.
    3. Immobilize and elevate the affected area.
    4. Do NOT debride, excise, or apply heat.
    5. Evacuate if the wound develops necrosis, if systemic symptoms (fever, rash, dark urine) develop, or if the bite is on the face or genitals.

    See [Venomous Spiders](../wildlife/venomous-spiders.md) for identification details.

=== "Scorpion Stings"

    ### Scorpion Stings

    Most scorpion stings cause local pain comparable to a bee sting. The bark scorpion (*Centruroides sculpturatus*) in the southwestern U.S. and Mexico is the primary North American exception.

    #### Bark Scorpion — Severe Envenomation Signs

    - Numbness and tingling at the sting site spreading to the limb.
    - Roving eye movements (opsoclonus) — a distinctive sign.
    - Muscle twitching, excessive salivation, difficulty swallowing.
    - Respiratory distress (children are most vulnerable).

    #### Treatment

    1. Clean the sting site with soap and water.
    2. Apply a cold pack for 10 minutes on, 10 minutes off.
    3. Administer pain relievers (ibuprofen, acetaminophen).
    4. Do NOT apply a tourniquet or make incisions.
    5. Monitor for systemic symptoms for 4-6 hours.
    6. Evacuate children, elderly, or anyone showing neurotoxic signs.

    !!! tip
        Shake out boots, clothing, and sleeping bags before use in scorpion territory. Scorpions are nocturnal and seek dark, confined spaces.

=== "Insect Stings"

    ### Insect Stings (Bee, Wasp, Hornet)

    #### Stinger Removal

    Remove a honeybee stinger as fast as possible. The method (scraping vs. pulling) matters less than speed — the venom sac continues pumping for 2-3 minutes after detachment.

    1. Scrape the stinger out with a flat edge (credit card, knife blade) or pull it out with fingers or tweezers.
    2. Clean the site with soap and water.
    3. Apply a cold pack to reduce swelling.

    !!! note
        Wasps and hornets do not leave stingers behind. They can sting repeatedly.

    #### Normal Local Reaction

    Pain, redness, and swelling at the sting site lasting up to 48 hours. Treat with cold compresses and oral antihistamines.

    #### Large Local Reaction

    Swelling greater than 4 inches (10 cm) in diameter, lasting up to 7 days. Not anaphylaxis, but uncomfortable. Treat with antihistamines and ibuprofen.

    #### Multiple Stings

    More than 10-20 stings can cause systemic toxicity even without allergy: vomiting, diarrhea, muscle breakdown, kidney failure. Evacuate anyone with more than 20 stings.

    See [Insect Threats](../wildlife/insect-threats.md) for avoidance strategies.

=== "Tick Bites"

    ### Tick Bites

    Ticks transmit Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, babesiosis, and other infections. Disease transmission risk increases with attachment duration — most require 24-48 hours of attachment.

    #### Removal

    1. Grasp the tick as close to the skin surface as possible with fine-tipped tweezers.
    2. Pull upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk.
    3. If mouthparts break off in the skin, remove them with tweezers if easily accessible. If not, leave them and let the skin heal.
    4. Clean the bite area and your hands with rubbing alcohol or soap and water.

    !!! danger "WARNING"
        Do NOT use heat (match, cigarette), petroleum jelly, nail polish, or other "suffocation" methods. These cause the tick to regurgitate into the wound, increasing infection risk.

    #### Post-Removal Monitoring

    - Note the date and location of the bite.
    - Watch for a bull's-eye rash (erythema migrans) for 30 days — indicates Lyme disease.
    - Watch for fever, headache, muscle aches, or rash within 2 weeks — indicates possible tick-borne illness.
    - If prophylactic doxycycline is available and the tick was engorged (attached >36 hours) in a Lyme-endemic area: a single dose of 200 mg within 72 hours of removal reduces Lyme risk.

=== "Marine Stings"

    ### Marine Stings (Jellyfish, Man-of-War, Sea Urchin)

    #### Jellyfish and Portuguese Man-of-War

    !!! danger "WARNING: vinegar is not for every species"
        **Vinegar helps for tropical box jellyfish. It makes Portuguese man-of-war / bluebottle (*Physalia*) stings WORSE** — it triggers unfired nematocysts to discharge. Identify the species, or default to hot water.

    !!! warning "CAUTION"
        Never rinse with fresh water — the osmotic change fires unfired nematocysts. Never apply urine (ineffective and unsanitary).

    **All stings — first steps:**

    1. Remove the victim from the water.
    2. Remove visible tentacles with tweezers, a stick, or gloved hands. Never with bare skin.

    **Then, by species:**

    | Sting | Treatment |
    |---|---|
    | Tropical box jellyfish (*Chironex*), Irukandji | Douse with vinegar (5% acetic acid) for 30 seconds. Then evacuate urgently. |
    | Portuguese man-of-war / bluebottle (*Physalia*) | **No vinegar.** Rinse with seawater, then hot water immersion. |
    | Unknown / temperate species | Rinse with seawater, then hot water immersion. |

    3. Hot water immersion: as hot as tolerable without scalding, 110-113°F (43-45°C), for 20-45 minutes. This is the best-evidenced pain treatment for most stings.
    4. Administer pain medication as needed.

    !!! note
        Box jellyfish (*Chironex fleckeri*) stings in northern Australian and Indo-Pacific waters can kill within minutes. Apply vinegar immediately, prepare for CPR, and evacuate.

    #### Sea Urchin Spines

    1. Soak the affected area in hot water, 110-113°F (43-45°C), for 30-90 minutes to break down venom proteins.
    2. Remove accessible spines with tweezers. Do not crush spines deeper into tissue.
    3. Spines too deep to remove will often dissolve or be expelled naturally over weeks.
    4. Monitor for infection.

## Common Mistakes

- **Applying a tourniquet to a snake bite.** Traps venom in the limb, causing massive tissue destruction. Immobilize instead.
- **Sucking venom from a wound.** Does not extract meaningful venom. Introduces oral bacteria into the wound.
- **Delaying epinephrine during anaphylaxis.** Antihistamines do not stop anaphylaxis. Epinephrine is the only first-line treatment. Give it early.
- **Using fresh water on jellyfish stings.** Osmotic change causes remaining nematocysts to fire. Use seawater.
- **Using vinegar on a man-of-war sting.** Vinegar is for tropical box jellyfish. On *Physalia* it triggers nematocyst discharge and worsens the sting.
- **Twisting or jerking ticks during removal.** Breaks off mouthparts and may cause regurgitation into the wound. Pull straight up with steady force.
- **Ignoring "mild" bites in the field.** Infection develops over days. Clean every bite wound and monitor daily.

## Quick Reference

| Bite/Sting | First Action | Do NOT | Evacuate If |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snake (pit viper) | Immobilize limb, remove jewelry | Cut, suck, tourniquet, ice | All suspected venomous bites |
| Snake (elapid) | Pressure immobilization bandage | Delay bandaging | All elapid bites |
| Spider (widow) | Clean, cold pack, pain relief | — | Severe cramps, breathing difficulty |
| Spider (recluse) | Clean, cold pack, elevate | Debride, apply heat | Necrosis, systemic symptoms |
| Scorpion | Clean, cold pack, pain relief | Tourniquet, incision | Neurotoxic signs, children |
| Bee/wasp | Remove stinger fast, cold pack | Squeeze stinger | Anaphylaxis, >20 stings |
| Tick | Pull straight up with tweezers | Heat, petroleum jelly | Rash, fever within 30 days |
| Jellyfish (box/tropical) | Vinegar 30 s, then evacuate | Fresh water, urine | Always — can be lethal |
| Man-of-war / bluebottle | Seawater rinse, hot water soak | **Vinegar**, fresh water, urine | Breathing difficulty, large sting |

**Anaphylaxis Protocol:**

1. Epinephrine 0.3 mg IM (outer thigh) — repeat in 5-15 min if needed.
2. Position patient supine with legs elevated.
3. Administer antihistamine if available.
4. Evacuate immediately.
