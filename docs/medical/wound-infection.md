# Wound Infection: Recognition, Treatment, and Evacuation

> Identify, treat, and manage wound infections in the field before they become systemic and life-threatening.

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Systemic infection (sepsis) is a medical emergency. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- All wounds in a survival setting will become contaminated. Aggressive irrigation at the time of injury is the single most effective infection-prevention measure.
- Infection signs: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaking from the wound, and fever.
- Red streaking toward the heart (lymphangitis) or fever above 101°F (38.3°C) indicates the infection has spread beyond the wound. Evacuate.
- Irrigate wounds with at least 500 mL (1 pint) of the cleanest water available under pressure before closing or dressing.
- Sepsis kills faster than blood loss in prolonged field scenarios. Recognize it early and evacuate.

## Infection Timeline

Understanding the typical infection timeline helps distinguish normal healing from dangerous progression.

| Time After Injury | Normal Healing | Infection |
|---|---|---|
| 0-6 hours | Bleeding stops, clot forms | Window for effective irrigation |
| 6-12 hours | Mild redness, swelling (inflammatory response) | Too early to distinguish from normal inflammation |
| 12-48 hours | Swelling peaks then begins to recede | Increasing redness, warmth, worsening pain |
| 48-72 hours | Edges begin to heal, pain decreasing | Purulent drainage, expanding redness, fever |
| 3-7 days | Granulation tissue forming | Red streaking, abscess formation, systemic signs |
| 7+ days | Progressive closure | Tissue death, sepsis risk |

!!! note
    In warm, humid environments (tropics, swamps), this timeline accelerates. Expect infection signs 12-24 hours earlier than the table above.

## Recognizing Infection

### Local Signs (at the wound)

- **Increasing redness** that expands beyond the wound edges over hours to days.
- **Warmth** over and around the wound compared to surrounding tissue.
- **Swelling** that worsens after the first 24 hours rather than improving.
- **Purulent drainage** — pus (white, yellow, green, or brown fluid). Clear or slightly blood-tinged drainage is normal.
- **Increasing pain** at the wound, especially if it was previously improving.
- **Foul smell** from the wound.
- **Wound edges separating** or tissue breaking down.

### Regional Signs (infection is spreading)

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Regional signs mean the infection has moved beyond the wound. Begin antibiotics if available and prepare to evacuate.

- **Red streaking** (lymphangitis) — red lines extending from the wound toward the trunk. Follow the streaks toward the heart. Mark the leading edge with a pen and note the time.
- **Swollen, tender lymph nodes** — groin nodes for leg wounds, armpit nodes for arm wounds, neck nodes for head/face wounds.
- **Cellulitis** — diffuse redness, warmth, and swelling spreading across a broad area without a clear abscess.

### Systemic Signs (infection in the bloodstream)

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Any two of the following signs suggest sepsis. This is immediately life-threatening. Evacuate by the fastest means available.

- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) or hypothermia below 96.8°F (36°C).
- Heart rate above 90 beats per minute at rest.
- Respiratory rate above 20 breaths per minute.
- Confusion, altered mental status, extreme fatigue.
- Shaking chills (rigors).

## Prevention

### Irrigation

Irrigation is the most important infection-prevention step. The goal is to physically flush bacteria and debris from the wound under pressure.

1. Use the cleanest water available: potable water, disinfected water, or saline (1 teaspoon / 5 mL salt per 1 quart / 1 liter water).
2. Fill a clean plastic bag, water bottle with a hole poked in the cap, or a syringe (at least 20 mL).
3. Direct a forceful stream into the wound from 2 inches (5 cm) away.
4. Use at least 500 mL (1 pint) for a small wound, 1-2 liters (1-2 quarts) for large or contaminated wounds.
5. Irrigate until all visible debris is removed and the fluid runs clear.

!!! tip
    A syringe or squeeze bottle produces roughly 8-12 psi of pressure — the effective range for removing bacteria without driving debris deeper. Pouring water over a wound is not irrigation.

### Debridement

Remove dead, damaged, or contaminated tissue before dressing. Dead tissue is a bacterial growth medium.

1. Wash your hands. Wear gloves if available.
2. Use a sterile blade, scissors, or clean tweezers.
3. Remove only tissue that is clearly dead (gray, black, or mushy) or foreign material.
4. Stop if you encounter bleeding tissue — that tissue is alive.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not debride wounds near tendons, nerves, blood vessels, joints, or the face without surgical training. Irrigate aggressively instead.

### Wound Closure vs. Open Management

- **Close** clean wounds less than 6 hours old with low contamination risk: clean cuts, surgical wounds.
- **Leave open** wounds that are more than 6 hours old, heavily contaminated, caused by bites, or caused by crushing mechanisms.
- Pack open wounds loosely with moist (saline-dampened) gauze. Change the packing at least once daily.

## Field Treatment of Infection

### Step 1: Open the Wound

If a closed wound shows infection signs, remove closures (stitches, butterfly strips, staples) and open the wound to allow drainage.

### Step 2: Irrigate Aggressively

Flush the wound with at least 1 liter (1 quart) of the cleanest water available under pressure. Irrigate twice daily until infection signs begin to improve.

### Step 3: Apply Warm Soaks

Soak the wound in warm water or apply warm, moist compresses for 20-30 minutes, three to four times daily. Heat increases blood flow to the area, bringing white blood cells and antibodies.

### Step 4: Antibiotics

Begin oral antibiotics if available and infection signs are worsening despite local care.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    The dosing table below is for emergency field use only. Confirm no drug allergies before administering. Complete the full course even if symptoms improve.

#### Antibiotic Dosing Table

| Antibiotic | Adult Dose | Frequency | Duration | Covers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) | 875/125 mg | Every 12 hours | 7-10 days | Broad spectrum, bite wounds |
| Cephalexin (Keflex) | 500 mg | Every 6 hours | 7-10 days | Skin/soft tissue, cellulitis |
| Doxycycline | 100 mg | Every 12 hours | 7-10 days | Broad spectrum, tick-borne illness |
| Ciprofloxacin | 500 mg | Every 12 hours | 7-10 days | Gram-negative, GI, urinary |
| Metronidazole (Flagyl) | 500 mg | Every 8 hours | 7-10 days | Anaerobic bacteria (deep/puncture wounds) |
| Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim DS) | 160/800 mg | Every 12 hours | 7-10 days | MRSA, skin abscesses |

!!! note
    For bite wounds (human or animal), use amoxicillin-clavulanate as first choice. For suspected MRSA (abscesses, boils not responding to standard antibiotics), use trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline.

### Step 5: Drain Abscesses

An abscess is a walled-off pocket of pus. Antibiotics alone cannot clear it — the pus must be drained.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not attempt to drain abscesses near major blood vessels (neck, groin, armpit), near the eyes, or on the face unless you have surgical training. Evacuate.

1. Clean the skin over the abscess with antiseptic.
2. Using a sterile blade, make a single incision along the long axis of the abscess through the most prominent point.
3. Make the incision large enough to allow full drainage — at least half the length of the abscess.
4. Express all pus by gentle pressure around the edges. Do not squeeze aggressively.
5. Irrigate the cavity with clean water or saline.
6. Pack the cavity loosely with moist gauze to keep it open and draining.
7. Change packing daily. The wound heals from the inside out over days to weeks.

## Cellulitis vs. Abscess

| Feature | Cellulitis | Abscess |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Diffuse redness, warm, flat | Localized swelling, raised, fluctuant |
| Borders | Indistinct, spreading | Defined edges |
| Palpation | Firm, no fluid wave | Soft center, fluid moves under pressure |
| Treatment | Antibiotics, warm soaks | Incision and drainage + antibiotics |
| Pain | Diffuse | Focal, throbbing |

## Sepsis Recognition and Response

Sepsis is infection that has entered the bloodstream and triggered a systemic inflammatory response. Without IV antibiotics and fluid resuscitation, mortality is high.

### Early Sepsis Signs

- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) or temperature below 96.8°F (36°C).
- Heart rate above 90 bpm at rest.
- Respiratory rate above 20 breaths per minute.
- Known wound infection plus any of the above.

### Severe Sepsis / Septic Shock

- Confusion or altered mental status.
- Systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg (if you can measure it) or unable to stand without dizziness.
- Mottled, cool extremities despite fever.
- Urine output drops to nearly zero.

### Field Response

1. Begin oral antibiotics immediately — use the broadest-spectrum option available.
2. Push oral fluids aggressively if the patient can swallow: water, ORS, any drinkable fluid.
3. Manage fever with ibuprofen or acetaminophen.
4. Keep the patient warm.
5. Evacuate by the fastest available means. Sepsis requires IV antibiotics and IV fluid resuscitation.

## Common Mistakes

- **Closing contaminated wounds.** Suturing or taping shut a dirty wound traps bacteria inside and guarantees infection. Leave contaminated wounds open.
- **Pouring water over a wound and calling it irrigated.** Effective irrigation requires pressure — use a syringe, squeeze bottle, or punctured bottle cap.
- **Stopping antibiotics when symptoms improve.** Incomplete courses breed resistant bacteria. Finish the full course.
- **Ignoring red streaking.** Lymphangitis means the infection is spreading through the lymphatic system. This is not a "wait and see" situation.
- **Packing an abscess cavity too tightly.** Tight packing prevents drainage and creates a new closed space. Pack loosely.
- **Using topical antibiotics (Neosporin) on deep wounds.** Topical antibiotics penetrate less than 2 mm. Deep infections require systemic (oral) antibiotics.

## Quick Reference

**Infection Red Flags — Evacuate Immediately:**

- Red streaking from the wound toward the trunk.
- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) with a wound.
- Confusion or altered mental status.
- Rapid heart rate (>90 bpm) plus rapid breathing (>20/min).
- Wound on the face, neck, hands, joints, or genitals with spreading redness.

**Daily Wound Care Routine:**

1. Remove old dressing.
2. Irrigate with clean water under pressure.
3. Inspect for redness, swelling, pus, streaking, and smell.
4. Mark redness borders with a pen and note the time.
5. Apply warm soak for 20-30 minutes if infection is present.
6. Repack open wounds with moist gauze.
7. Redress with clean bandage.

**Field Saline Recipe:**

1 teaspoon (5 mL) table salt per 1 quart (1 liter) of disinfected water.
