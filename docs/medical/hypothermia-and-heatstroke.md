# Hypothermia and Heatstroke

> Recognize, prevent, and treat cold and heat injuries in the field using protocols adapted from wilderness medicine and military standards.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. Temperature emergencies can be fatal. This guide does not replace professional medical training or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Hypothermia begins at a core temperature below 95 degF (35 degC) and can kill at temperatures well above freezing, especially when wet and windy.
- Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency defined by core temperature above 104 degF (40 degC) with altered mental status — cool aggressively and immediately.
- Afterdrop — a continued decline in core temperature after rewarming begins — can trigger cardiac arrest in moderate-to-severe hypothermia patients.
- Frostbite should not be thawed in the field if there is any chance of refreezing — refreezing causes far more tissue destruction than remaining frozen.
- Prevention is vastly more effective than treatment for all temperature-related injuries.

## Hypothermia

### How Heat Is Lost

Understand these mechanisms to prevent and treat hypothermia:

| Mechanism | Description | Example | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radiation | Body emits infrared heat to cooler surroundings | Standing in cold air | Insulate, cover head and neck |
| Conduction | Direct contact with cold surfaces | Sitting on cold ground, wet clothing | Insulate from ground, remove wet layers |
| Convection | Moving air or water strips heat | Wind, swimming | Windproof outer layer, shelter |
| Evaporation | Water changing to vapor absorbs heat | Sweating, wet clothing | Stay dry, manage exertion |
| Respiration | Inhaling cold air, exhaling warm moist air | Breathing in subzero conditions | Cover mouth with scarf or balaclava |

### Stages of Hypothermia

#### Mild Hypothermia: 95-90 degF (35-32 degC)

**Recognition:**

- Shivering — vigorous, uncontrollable. This is the body's primary heat-generation mechanism.
- Impaired fine motor coordination — difficulty with zippers, buttons, knots.
- Pale, cold skin. Goosebumps.
- Increased heart rate and respiratory rate.
- Mild confusion, poor judgment, apathy ("I'm fine, leave me alone").
- Increased urination (cold diuresis).

**Treatment:**

1. Remove from the cold environment. Get into shelter, out of wind and precipitation.
2. Remove all wet clothing. Replace with dry layers.
3. Insulate from the ground — use a sleeping pad, branches, dry leaves, or any barrier.
4. Add insulation — sleeping bag, blankets, extra clothing. Cover the head, neck, and torso.
5. Provide warm, sweet drinks if the patient is alert and can swallow. No alcohol or caffeine.
6. Provide high-calorie food — sugar, chocolate, energy bars. Shivering burns 400+ calories per hour.
7. Apply external heat sources: chemical heat packs, warm water bottles, or warmed rocks wrapped in cloth. Place against the neck, armpits, and groin (areas where major blood vessels are near the skin surface).
8. Encourage gentle movement — mild exercise generates heat. Do not allow exhaustive activity.

Mild hypothermia responds well to field rewarming. Most patients recover fully.

#### Moderate Hypothermia: 90-82 degF (32-28 degC)

**Recognition:**

- Shivering slows and eventually stops — this is an ominous sign, not improvement.
- Severe mental impairment — confusion, slurred speech, irrational behavior.
- Loss of fine and gross motor coordination — stumbling, inability to walk.
- Paradoxical undressing — the patient removes clothing due to a false sensation of warmth.
- Muscle rigidity.
- Drowsiness, difficult to rouse.
- Heart rate slows. Breathing becomes shallow.

**Treatment:**

1. Handle the patient gently. Rough movement can trigger cardiac arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation) in moderate-to-severe hypothermia.
2. Place the patient in a horizontal position. Do not allow them to sit up or stand.
3. Remove wet clothing — cut it off rather than having the patient move.
4. Insulate aggressively: place the patient in a hypothermia wrap.

**Hypothermia Wrap (Burrito Wrap):**

1. Lay a ground insulation layer (sleeping pad, branches).
2. Lay a vapor barrier (tarp, plastic sheet, emergency blanket reflective side in).
3. Lay insulation (sleeping bag, blankets).
4. Place the patient on top.
5. Place heat sources at the neck, armpits, and groin.
6. Wrap the insulation around the patient.
7. Wrap the vapor barrier over everything — the goal is to trap heat and block wind and moisture.
8. Cover the head, leaving the face exposed for airway monitoring.

5. Do not give food or drink to a patient with altered mental status — aspiration risk.
6. Monitor breathing and pulse continuously.
7. Evacuate. Moderate hypothermia requires professional medical care.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Afterdrop — when rewarming begins, cold blood from the extremities returns to the core, causing core temperature to drop further. This can trigger cardiac arrest. Rewarm the core (torso, neck, head) first. Do not rub or warm the extremities actively in moderate-to-severe cases.

#### Severe Hypothermia: Below 82 degF (28 degC)

**Recognition:**

- No shivering.
- Unconscious or semi-conscious.
- Rigid muscles.
- Very slow or undetectable pulse (check for 60 seconds).
- Very slow or absent respirations.
- Dilated pupils (this alone does not indicate death).
- Skin may appear gray or blue.
- May appear dead.

**Treatment:**

1. Handle with extreme care — the heart is extremely irritable and prone to fibrillation from any jarring.
2. Check pulse and breathing for a full 60 seconds before initiating CPR.
3. If no pulse or breathing is detected, begin CPR. See [CPR and Choking](cpr-and-choking.md) for hypothermic arrest protocol.
4. Apply a hypothermia wrap as described above.
5. If rescue breathing is needed, warm and humidify the breaths if possible (breathe through a warm cloth).
6. Do not attempt active external rewarming of extremities.
7. Evacuate immediately. Severe hypothermia patients need core rewarming (warm IV fluids, warm lavage) available only in a hospital setting.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not pronounce a hypothermic patient dead in the field. The cold protects the brain and organs. Cases of full recovery after prolonged cardiac arrest in severe hypothermia are documented. Continue CPR and rewarming. "They are not dead until they are warm and dead."

## Frostbite

### Classification

| Degree | Appearance | Sensation | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frostnip | Red, then white or gray | Painful, tingling | Full recovery |
| 1st degree | White/yellowish plaque, surrounding redness | Numbness, then burning on rewarming | Full recovery |
| 2nd degree | Clear or milky blisters, redness | Numbness, then severe pain on rewarming | Usually recovers |
| 3rd degree | Blood-filled blisters, blue-gray skin | Numbness, loss of sensation | Tissue loss likely |
| 4th degree | Mottled, deep red to black, firm | No sensation | Amputation likely |

### Field Treatment

1. **Do not thaw frostbitten tissue if there is any possibility of refreezing.** Thaw-refreeze cycles cause far worse damage. A patient can walk on a frozen foot with minimal additional damage, but not on a thawed one.
2. Protect the frozen area from further injury. Do not rub, massage, or apply snow.
3. Remove wet clothing and constrictive jewelry (rings, watches) before swelling occurs.
4. Insulate the affected area. Place dry padding between frostbitten fingers or toes.
5. Do not break blisters.
6. Do not apply direct heat (fire, heater, hot water bottle) — frozen tissue burns easily because the patient cannot feel temperature.

### Controlled Rewarming (Only When Refreezing Can Be Prevented)

1. Prepare water at 98.6-102 degF (37-39 degC). Test with an uninjured hand — it should feel warm but comfortable, not hot.
2. Immerse the frostbitten area completely.
3. Maintain the water temperature by adding warm water as it cools. Do not let it exceed 102 degF (39 degC).
4. Rewarming takes 20-40 minutes. The process is complete when the tissue is soft, pliable, and red or purple.
5. Rewarming is extremely painful. Administer the strongest available pain medication before beginning — ibuprofen 800 mg plus acetaminophen 1000 mg at minimum. Opioids are appropriate if available.
6. After thawing, loosely wrap with dry, sterile dressings. Place padding between digits.
7. Elevate the affected extremity.
8. Give ibuprofen 600 mg every 8 hours — it has specific anti-prostaglandin effects that reduce frostbite tissue loss.
9. Do not allow weight-bearing on thawed feet.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Frostbitten tissue looks worse before it looks better. Final determination of tissue viability takes weeks. Do not amputate or debride frostbitten tissue in the field.

## Heat Illness

### Contributing Factors

- High ambient temperature and humidity.
- Direct sun exposure.
- Heavy physical exertion.
- Dehydration.
- Restrictive clothing or heavy gear.
- Medications: antihistamines, beta-blockers, diuretics, stimulants, antidepressants.
- Alcohol consumption.
- Obesity, poor physical conditioning.
- History of prior heat illness.
- Age extremes (children and elderly are more vulnerable).

### Heat Cramps

**Recognition:** Painful muscle spasms, usually in the calves, thighs, or abdomen, during or after heavy exertion in heat.

**Treatment:**

1. Rest in a cool, shaded area.
2. Drink an electrolyte solution: 1/4 to 1/2 teaspoon of salt per 1 quart (1 liter) of water. Commercial sports drinks are adequate.
3. Gently stretch and massage the affected muscle.
4. Do not resume activity until cramps have fully resolved and adequate hydration is restored.

### Heat Exhaustion

**Recognition:**

- Core temperature elevated but below 104 degF (40 degC).
- Heavy sweating — skin is cool and clammy.
- Weakness, fatigue, dizziness.
- Headache, nausea, vomiting.
- Rapid, weak pulse.
- Pale or flushed skin.
- Mild confusion but oriented — the patient knows who they are, where they are, and what happened.
- Muscle cramps.
- Dark, concentrated urine.

**Treatment:**

1. Move to shade or the coolest available environment.
2. Remove excess clothing and gear.
3. Lay the patient down, elevate the legs 6-12 inches (15-30 cm).
4. Cool actively:
   - Apply cool, wet cloths to the neck, armpits, and groin.
   - Fan the patient to promote evaporative cooling.
   - Spray or sponge the skin with water and fan.
5. Rehydrate orally: small, frequent sips of cool electrolyte solution. Target 1-2 liters (1-2 quarts) over the first hour.
6. Monitor for progression to heatstroke — check mental status every 5 minutes.
7. Rest for the remainder of the day. Resume activity gradually over 24-48 hours.

### Heatstroke

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency. Without aggressive cooling, mortality exceeds 50%. Every minute of delay in cooling increases organ damage and death risk.

**Recognition — Heat Exhaustion vs. Heatstroke:**

| Feature | Heat Exhaustion | Heatstroke |
|---|---|---|
| Core temperature | Below 104 degF (40 degC) | Above 104 degF (40 degC) |
| Mental status | Alert, mildly confused | Altered — confusion, combativeness, seizures, unconsciousness |
| Sweating | Heavy | May be present (exertional) or absent (classic) |
| Skin | Cool, clammy | Hot, may be dry or wet |
| Severity | Moderate — treat and monitor | Critical — cool immediately or patient dies |

The defining distinction is **altered mental status**. If the patient is confused, combative, delirious, having seizures, or unconscious and the core temperature is elevated, treat as heatstroke.

**Heatstroke Treatment:**

1. **Cool first, transport second.** Do not delay cooling to evacuate. Begin cooling immediately.
2. Move to shade.
3. Remove all clothing.
4. **Best method — cold water immersion:** Submerge the patient in cold water (any natural water source — creek, pond, lake) up to the neck. This is the gold standard and cools fastest. If a tub or container is available, fill it.
5. **Alternative methods** (use the most aggressive available):
   - Pour cold water continuously over the entire body while fanning aggressively.
   - Apply ice packs or cold packs to the neck, armpits, groin, palms, and soles of the feet.
   - Wrap in a wet sheet and fan.
   - Evaporative cooling: mist skin with water and maximize airflow.
6. Target a core temperature of 101 degF (38.3 degC). If you do not have a thermometer, cool for at least 15-20 minutes or until the patient's mental status noticeably improves.
7. Monitor the airway. Heatstroke patients may seize or vomit.
8. Place in the recovery position if unconscious. See [CPR and Choking](cpr-and-choking.md).
9. If the patient can drink, provide cool fluids slowly.
10. Do not give aspirin or acetaminophen — they are ineffective against heatstroke and may worsen organ damage.
11. Evacuate to definitive medical care after initial cooling. Heatstroke patients need monitoring for rhabdomyolysis, kidney failure, and coagulopathy.

### Exertional vs. Classic Heatstroke

- **Exertional heatstroke** — occurs in healthy, active individuals during intense physical activity. Often still sweating. Common in military, athletes, laborers.
- **Classic heatstroke** — occurs in elderly, chronically ill, or medicated individuals during heat waves. Often NOT sweating (hot, dry skin). Develops over days.

Both are treated identically: aggressive, immediate cooling.

## Hydration Guidelines

### Prevention of Heat Illness

- Pre-hydrate: drink 500 mL (about 1 pint) of water 2 hours before exertion in heat.
- During activity: drink 250 mL (about 1 cup) every 15-20 minutes.
- Do not exceed 1.5 liters (1.5 quarts) per hour — overhydration causes hyponatremia (low blood sodium), which can be fatal.
- Add electrolytes for activity lasting longer than 1 hour or in heavy sweating: 1/4 teaspoon salt per liter of water, or use commercial electrolyte tablets.
- Monitor urine color — target pale yellow. Dark urine indicates dehydration. Clear urine with frequent urination may indicate overhydration.

### Oral Rehydration Solution (Field-Expedient)

- 1 quart (1 liter) clean water
- 6 teaspoons sugar
- 1/2 teaspoon salt

Dissolve completely. Sip frequently. This formula approximates the World Health Organization oral rehydration solution.

## Cold Weather Prevention

### The COLD Acronym

- **C**lean — keep clothing clean for maximum insulation.
- **O**verlayer — dress in layers (base layer wicks moisture, insulation layer traps air, outer layer blocks wind and water).
- **L**oose — wear clothing loose to allow air circulation and avoid constricting blood flow.
- **D**ry — stay dry. Change out of wet clothing immediately. Moisture increases heat loss by 25x.

### Additional Prevention

- Eat high-calorie food frequently — your body needs fuel to generate heat. Consume 4,000-6,000 calories daily in cold environments.
- Stay hydrated — dehydration impairs circulation and heat distribution.
- Cover the head, neck, and hands — these areas have high surface-area-to-mass ratios and lose heat rapidly.
- Keep moving — but do not overexert and sweat.
- Use the buddy system — check each other for signs of hypothermia and frostbite regularly. Individuals often do not recognize their own symptoms.
- Insulate from the ground. Conductive heat loss to cold ground is rapid.

## Common Mistakes

- Giving alcohol to a hypothermia patient — alcohol dilates blood vessels, accelerating heat loss.
- Placing a moderate-to-severe hypothermia patient in a warm shower or bath — rapid external rewarming of extremities drives cold blood to the core and can cause fatal afterdrop and arrhythmia.
- Rubbing frostbitten skin with snow or hands — this causes further mechanical tissue damage.
- Thawing frostbite in the field when refreezing is possible — thaw-refreeze cycles destroy tissue that would otherwise survive.
- Confusing heat exhaustion with heatstroke — the critical difference is altered mental status. If in doubt, treat as heatstroke.
- Giving aspirin or acetaminophen for heatstroke — these drugs do not lower core temperature in heatstroke and may increase liver or kidney damage.
- Stopping cooling efforts too early — cool until core temperature reaches 101 degF (38.3 degC) or mental status clearly improves.
- Overhydrating — hyponatremia is as dangerous as dehydration. Do not exceed 1.5 L/hour.
- Ignoring early mild symptoms — both hypothermia and heat illness are progressive. Early intervention prevents emergencies.
- Allowing a hypothermia patient to walk or stand — exertion and upright posture circulate cold blood to the core and can trigger cardiac arrest.

## Quick Reference

### Hypothermia Stages

| Stage | Temp Range | Key Signs | Field Treatable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild | 95-90 °F (35-32 °C) | Shivering (vigorous), confusion, clumsiness | Yes |
| Moderate | 90-82 °F (32-28 °C) | Shivering stops, severe confusion, paradoxical undressing | Partially — evacuate |
| Severe | Below 82 °F (28 °C) | Unconscious, rigid, appears dead | No — evacuate immediately |

### Heat Illness Progression

| Stage | Mental Status | Skin | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heat cramps | Normal | Sweating | Rest, electrolytes, stretch |
| Heat exhaustion (<104 °F / 40 °C) | Alert, mild confusion | Cool, clammy, sweating | Cool, hydrate, rest |
| Heatstroke (>104 °F / 40 °C) | Confused, combative, seizing, or unconscious | Hot, dry or wet | Cool aggressively NOW, evacuate |

### Frostbite Decision Matrix

| Question | Answer | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Can refreezing be prevented? | No | Keep frozen, protect, evacuate |
| Can refreezing be prevented? | Yes | Rewarm in 98.6-102 degF (37-39 degC) water for 20-40 min |
| Blisters present? | Clear fluid | Do not break, protect |
| Blisters present? | Blood-filled | Do not break, protect — deep injury |
| Can patient walk on affected feet? | Feet are frozen | Yes, if necessary for evacuation |
| Can patient walk on affected feet? | Feet are thawed | No — carry or sled the patient |
