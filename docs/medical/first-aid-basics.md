# First Aid Basics: Wound Care, Burns, and Bleeding Control

> Stop bleeding, prevent infection, and manage burns using field-expedient first aid when professional help is unavailable.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Control life-threatening bleeding first — everything else is secondary.
- Clean all wounds thoroughly to prevent infection, which kills more people in survival situations than the initial injury.
- Never remove an embedded object from a wound; stabilize it in place.
- Cool burns immediately with clean, cool water for at least 10 minutes.
- Reassess every wound at least twice daily for signs of infection.

---

## Wound Classification

### Cuts and Lacerations

A **cut** is a clean, sharp-edged wound. A **laceration** is a jagged, torn wound with irregular edges. Lacerations carry a higher infection risk due to tissue damage.

Assess every wound for:

- Depth — can you see fat, muscle, tendon, or bone?
- Length — wounds longer than 1 inch (2.5 cm) often need closure.
- Location — face, hands, joints, and genitals require special attention.
- Contamination — dirt, debris, rust, animal matter.

### Puncture Wounds

Puncture wounds appear small on the surface but can be deep. They carry the highest infection risk because debris gets driven deep and the wound seals over, creating an anaerobic environment ideal for tetanus and other infections.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Puncture wounds to the chest, abdomen, neck, or head are medical emergencies. Do not probe them. Stabilize and evacuate immediately.

### Abrasions

Abrasions (scrapes, road rash) remove the outer skin layers. They are painful, prone to contamination, and heal slowly without proper care.

## Bleeding Control

Address bleeding in this order — move to the next step only if the current one fails.

### Step 1: Direct Pressure

1. Apply firm, direct pressure using the cleanest material available.
2. Use your palm over a folded cloth or gauze pad.
3. Press hard — effective pressure causes discomfort to the patient.
4. Maintain pressure continuously for at least 10 minutes. Do not lift to check.
5. If blood soaks through, add more material on top. Do not remove the original dressing.

### Step 2: Elevation

Raise the injured limb above the level of the heart while maintaining direct pressure. Gravity reduces arterial pressure at the wound site.

### Step 3: Pressure Points

If direct pressure and elevation fail, compress the artery upstream of the wound against underlying bone.

| Body Region | Pressure Point | Location |
|---|---|---|
| Arm | Brachial artery | Inner upper arm, between bicep and tricep |
| Leg | Femoral artery | Groin crease, midpoint between hip bone and pubic bone |
| Scalp/Temple | Temporal artery | Just in front of the ear |
| Neck | Carotid artery | Side of neck — compress ONE side only, never both |

### Step 4: Wound Packing

For deep wounds that direct pressure cannot control:

1. Open the wound with gloved fingers to visualize the bleeding source.
2. Pack gauze tightly into the wound, starting at the deepest point.
3. Fill the entire wound cavity. Use hemostatic gauze (QuikClot, Celox) if available.
4. Apply direct pressure on top for a minimum of 3 minutes (5 minutes for hemostatic agents).

### Step 5: Tourniquet

Apply a tourniquet when:

- Direct pressure fails on an extremity wound.
- A limb is partially or fully amputated.
- Multiple casualties require you to move on to the next patient.
- You cannot reach the wound (e.g., trapped limb).

**Application procedure:**

1. Place the tourniquet 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) above the wound. Never place directly on a joint.
2. Route the strap through the buckle and pull tight.
3. Turn the windlass until bleeding stops.
4. Lock the windlass in place.
5. Mark the time of application on the tourniquet, the patient's forehead, or tape — write "TQ" and the time.
6. Do not loosen or remove the tourniquet once applied unless directed by a medical professional.
7. A properly applied tourniquet causes significant pain. This is expected.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A tourniquet that is too loose increases bleeding by occluding venous return without stopping arterial flow. Tighten until the bleeding stops and the distal pulse is absent.

**Improvised tourniquet:** Use a strip of material at least 1.5 inches (4 cm) wide. Never use wire, paracord, or narrow material — these cause tissue necrosis without effectively stopping blood flow. A triangular bandage folded into a cravat with a stick windlass is the most effective improvised option.

## Wound Cleaning

### When to Clean

Clean every wound that is not actively hemorrhaging. Delay cleaning of life-threatening bleeds until hemorrhage is controlled.

### Irrigation Procedure

1. Use the cleanest water available. Potable drinking water is acceptable. Sterile saline (0.9%) is ideal.
2. Fill a clean container or zip-lock bag. Puncture a small hole in the bag or use a syringe.
3. Irrigate under pressure — 8 psi is the target. A 20 mL syringe with an 18-gauge needle produces this pressure. Squeezing a bag with a pinhole approximates it.
4. Use at least 500 mL (about 1 pint) per inch of wound length.
5. Direct the stream into the wound from multiple angles.
6. Remove all visible debris with clean tweezers.

### Debridement

Remove dead, contaminated, or devitalized tissue (gray, black, or non-bleeding tissue) using a clean blade or scissors. Healthy tissue bleeds when cut and appears pink or red.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not irrigate puncture wounds aggressively — you risk pushing debris deeper. Soak puncture wounds in clean warm water with mild soap or diluted antiseptic instead.

### Antiseptics

| Solution | Dilution | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Povidone-iodine (Betadine) | 1% solution (dilute 10% stock 1:10) | Wound irrigation after initial water flush |
| Chlorhexidine | 0.05% aqueous | Wound irrigation — do not use near ears or eyes |
| Dilute bleach | 0.5 teaspoon per 1 quart (1 liter) | Last resort wound irrigation |
| Hydrogen peroxide | Do not use | Damages healthy tissue, delays healing |

## Wound Closure

### When to Close

Close a wound only if **all** of the following are true:

- The wound is less than 6 hours old (12 hours for face wounds).
- It is thoroughly cleaned and free of debris.
- The edges can be approximated without tension.
- There is no sign of infection.
- It is not a bite wound (human or animal).

### Closure Methods

**Adhesive strips (Steri-Strips, butterfly bandages):**

1. Dry the skin on both sides of the wound.
2. Apply tincture of benzoin to the skin (not the wound) to improve adhesion.
3. Place strips perpendicular to the wound, 1/8 inch (3 mm) apart.
4. Alternate sides — do not pull both edges from one side.
5. Leave small gaps between strips for drainage.

**Tissue adhesive (skin glue):**

1. Hold wound edges together.
2. Apply a thin layer across the closed wound surface.
3. Hold for 60 seconds.
4. Do not use inside the wound or on wet surfaces.

**Suturing (field expedient):**

Use only if trained. Improper suturing traps infection and worsens outcomes. If you must suture, use simple interrupted stitches spaced 1/4 inch (6 mm) apart, entering and exiting 1/8 inch (3 mm) from wound edges.

## Bandaging Techniques

### Basic Principles

- Cover the wound completely.
- Secure the dressing without restricting circulation.
- Check distal circulation (pulse, sensation, color, warmth) after applying any bandage.
- Change dressings at least once daily, or whenever wet, soiled, or loosened.

### Pressure Dressing

1. Place an absorbent pad directly over the wound.
2. Wrap an elastic bandage (ACE wrap, Israeli bandage) firmly over the pad.
3. Each wrap should overlap the previous by one-half.
4. Secure the end with clips, tape, or by tucking.

### Figure-Eight Bandage (Joints)

1. Anchor the bandage below the joint.
2. Wrap diagonally across the joint.
3. Circle above the joint.
4. Wrap diagonally back across the joint.
5. Repeat, creating an X pattern over the joint.

### Spiral Bandage (Limbs)

1. Anchor at the narrowest point (distal end).
2. Wrap upward, overlapping each turn by one-half to two-thirds.
3. Secure at the widest point.

### Improvised Dressings

- Clean cloth, t-shirt material, or bandana as a dressing.
- Duct tape as adhesive.
- Plastic wrap (cling film) as a moisture barrier over burns.
- Maxi pads or diapers as absorbent wound pads.
- Sphagnum moss (dried, clean) as an antiseptic wound packing — used historically, but carries contamination risk.

## Burn Treatment

### Classification

| Degree | Depth | Appearance | Sensation | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st (Superficial) | Epidermis only | Red, dry, no blisters | Painful | 3-5 days |
| 2nd (Partial thickness) | Epidermis + dermis | Red, wet, blisters | Very painful | 2-4 weeks |
| 3rd (Full thickness) | Through dermis | White, brown, or charred; dry, leathery | Painless (nerves destroyed) | Requires grafting |

### Estimating Burn Area

Use the **Rule of Nines** for adults:

- Head and neck: 9%
- Each arm: 9%
- Chest (front): 18%
- Back: 18%
- Each leg: 18%
- Groin: 1%

For small or irregular burns, the patient's palm (including fingers) equals approximately 1% of total body surface area (TBSA).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Burns greater than 20% TBSA in adults (10% in children or elderly) are life-threatening emergencies requiring IV fluid resuscitation and evacuation.

### First-Degree Burn Treatment

1. Cool with clean, cool (not cold) running water for at least 10 minutes.
2. Apply aloe vera or a bland moisturizer.
3. Take ibuprofen (400-800 mg) or acetaminophen (500-1000 mg) for pain.
4. Protect from sun exposure during healing.

### Second-Degree Burn Treatment

1. Cool with clean, cool running water for 10-20 minutes.
2. Do not pop blisters — they are a natural sterile dressing.
3. If a blister breaks, clean gently and apply antibiotic ointment.
4. Cover with a non-adherent dressing (Telfa, plastic wrap, or a clean, smooth cloth).
5. Change dressings daily. Watch for infection.
6. Elevate burned extremities to reduce swelling.

### Third-Degree Burn Treatment

1. Do not cool with water — hypothermia is a greater immediate risk.
2. Cover with a clean, dry, non-adherent dressing.
3. Treat for shock: lay flat, elevate legs 6-12 inches (15-30 cm), keep warm.
4. Monitor airway — inhalation burns cause progressive airway swelling.
5. Evacuate immediately.

### Burns: Do Not

- Apply ice, butter, toothpaste, or egg whites.
- Use cotton balls or fluffy material directly on burns (fibers embed in tissue).
- Remove clothing that is stuck to a burn — cut around it.
- Wrap circumferential burns tightly — swelling needs room to expand.

## Infection Recognition

Check every wound for these signs at least twice daily:

- **Redness** spreading beyond the wound edges (mark the edge with a pen and monitor expansion).
- **Warmth** around the wound compared to surrounding tissue.
- **Swelling** that increases rather than decreases after 48 hours.
- **Purulent drainage** (thick, yellow, green, or foul-smelling discharge).
- **Red streaks** extending from the wound toward the heart (lymphangitis) — this is a medical emergency.
- **Fever** above 100.4 degF (38 degC).
- **Increased pain** after an initial improvement period.

### Field Infection Management

1. Reopen and re-irrigate the wound.
2. Apply topical antibiotic ointment (bacitracin, mupirocin, or triple antibiotic).
3. If oral antibiotics are available: amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily, or cephalexin 500 mg four times daily.
4. Change dressings three times daily with re-irrigation.
5. Elevate the affected area.
6. Evacuate if infection worsens or red streaking develops.

## Common Mistakes

- Removing a tourniquet too early because the patient complains of pain.
- Using hydrogen peroxide or full-strength Betadine on open wounds — both damage healthy tissue.
- Packing a wound loosely — ineffective packing fails to tamponade bleeding.
- Closing contaminated or bite wounds — this seals in bacteria and creates abscesses.
- Changing dressings too frequently on healing wounds, disrupting new tissue formation.
- Failing to check distal circulation after applying bandages or splints.
- Ignoring puncture wounds because they "look small."
- Cooling large burns with cold water, causing hypothermia.

## Quick Reference

| Problem | Immediate Action | Time-Critical? |
|---|---|---|
| Arterial bleed (spurting, bright red) | Direct pressure, pack, tourniquet | Yes — minutes |
| Venous bleed (steady, dark red) | Direct pressure, elevation | Yes — minutes to hours |
| Deep laceration | Clean, close if appropriate, dress | Moderate — close within 6 hours |
| Puncture wound | Soak, do not probe, monitor for infection | Moderate — watch 48+ hours |
| 1st-degree burn | Cool water 10 min, moisturize | No |
| 2nd-degree burn | Cool water 10-20 min, non-adherent dressing | Moderate — infection risk |
| 3rd-degree burn | Dry sterile dressing, treat shock, evacuate | Yes — life-threatening |
| Infection (red streaks) | Reopen wound, irrigate, antibiotics, evacuate | Yes — hours |
