# Dehydration and Waterborne Illness

> Recognize and treat dehydration and waterborne diseases in the field when clean water access is limited or compromised.

> **DISCLAIMER:** This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Severe dehydration and cholera can kill within hours. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Dehydration kills faster than starvation. A person loses function at 5% body water loss and risks death at 10%.
- Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is the most effective field treatment for dehydration — water alone is insufficient because it lacks electrolytes.
- All surface water in the wilderness should be treated as contaminated. Boil, filter, or chemically treat before drinking.
- Most waterborne illnesses cause diarrhea, which accelerates dehydration. Break the cycle with ORS and, when available, antibiotics.
- Dark yellow or amber urine is the earliest reliable sign of dehydration in the field.

## Dehydration

### Causes

- Inadequate water intake.
- Diarrhea and vomiting (the most common accelerator in survival situations).
- Excessive sweating from heat, exertion, or fever.
- High altitude (increased respiratory water loss).
- Burns (fluid loss through damaged skin).

### Stages of Dehydration

| Stage | Body Water Loss | Signs | Field Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild | 1-3% (~1-2 lbs / 0.5-1 kg) | Thirst, dark urine, dry mouth, slight headache | Oral fluids, ORS |
| Moderate | 3-6% (~2-4 lbs / 1-2 kg) | Decreased urine output, rapid pulse, dizziness on standing, sunken eyes, loss of skin turgor | ORS aggressively, rest in shade |
| Severe | 6-10% (~4-7 lbs / 2-3 kg) | No urine output, rapid weak pulse, confusion, unconsciousness, cool/mottled extremities | ORS if conscious; evacuate immediately |

### Skin Turgor Test

Pinch the skin on the back of the hand and release. In a hydrated person, skin snaps back immediately. In moderate dehydration, the skin "tents" and takes 2-3 seconds to flatten. In severe dehydration, the tent persists for more than 3 seconds.

!!! note
    Skin turgor is unreliable in elderly patients (skin loses elasticity with age) and in cold environments (skin stiffens). Use urine color and mental status as additional indicators.

### Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS)

ORS replaces both water and electrolytes lost through diarrhea, vomiting, and sweating. It is absorbed even during active diarrhea because glucose-coupled sodium transport remains functional.

#### Standard ORS Recipe (WHO formula)

Per 1 quart (1 liter) of clean (disinfected) water:

- 6 level teaspoons (30 mL) sugar.
- 1/2 level teaspoon (2.5 mL) table salt.

Stir until fully dissolved. Discard after 24 hours if not refrigerated.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Too much salt causes hypernatremia and worsens dehydration. Too much sugar causes osmotic diarrhea. Measure carefully. The solution should taste no saltier than tears.

#### Alternative ORS Ingredients

If sugar is unavailable:

- Honey: 6 teaspoons per quart/liter (do not give honey to children under 1 year).
- Crushed overripe banana or cooked rice water for glucose source (less precise but functional).

If pre-mixed ORS packets are available, follow packet instructions.

#### Administration

- **Mild dehydration:** Drink 1-2 cups (250-500 mL) per hour for 4 hours.
- **Moderate dehydration:** Drink 2-4 cups (500 mL-1 liter) per hour for 4 hours. Sip continuously rather than gulping.
- **Severe dehydration with consciousness:** Sip ORS constantly. Aim for 4-6 quarts (4-6 liters) over 6-8 hours.
- **Severe dehydration with unconsciousness:** Position in recovery position. ORS cannot be given to an unconscious patient. Evacuate for IV fluids.

!!! tip
    If the patient vomits, wait 10 minutes, then resume ORS in smaller, more frequent sips — 1 tablespoon every 1-2 minutes.

### Field Alternatives for IV Fluids

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Rectal and subcutaneous fluid administration are emergency techniques only. They carry infection risk and should be used only when oral rehydration has failed and evacuation is delayed.

**Rectal Rehydration (Proctoclysis):**

Fluids administered rectally are absorbed through the rectal mucosa. This technique works when the patient cannot keep oral fluids down.

1. Use clean, body-temperature water or ORS.
2. Insert a lubricated flexible tube (catheter, clean flexible tubing) 4 inches (10 cm) into the rectum.
3. Elevate the fluid bag or bottle 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) above the patient.
4. Administer slowly — 200-400 mL per hour. If cramping occurs, slow the rate.
5. Target 1-2 liters over 4-6 hours.

## Waterborne Illnesses

!!! danger "WARNING"
    All surface water — streams, rivers, lakes, ponds — should be treated as contaminated even if it appears clear. Treat all water before drinking.

### Overview by Pathogen

=== "Giardia"

    ### Giardia (*Giardia lamblia*)

    **Source:** Contaminated surface water worldwide. Cysts survive cold water and chlorination at standard doses.

    **Incubation:** 1-3 weeks after ingestion.

    **Symptoms:** Explosive, foul-smelling diarrhea (often greasy/floating), gas, bloating, nausea, cramping, sulfurous belching. Low-grade fever occasionally. Symptoms wax and wane over weeks.

    **Field Treatment:**

    1. Push ORS aggressively to offset fluid loss.
    2. Metronidazole 250 mg three times daily for 5-7 days (if available).
    3. Alternative: Tinidazole 2 g single dose.

    **Prevention:** Boiling (1 minute at sea level, 3 minutes above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m), filtration to 1 micron or smaller, or chemical treatment with appropriate contact time.

=== "Cryptosporidium"

    ### Cryptosporidium (*Cryptosporidium parvum*)

    **Source:** Contaminated water and fecal-oral contact. Oocysts are resistant to chlorine and iodine at standard concentrations.

    **Incubation:** 2-10 days.

    **Symptoms:** Profuse, watery diarrhea, cramping, nausea, low-grade fever. Lasts 1-3 weeks in healthy adults. Can be fatal in immunocompromised individuals.

    **Field Treatment:**

    1. ORS for fluid replacement — this is the primary treatment.
    2. No effective field antibiotic. The illness is self-limiting in healthy adults.
    3. Nitazoxanide 500 mg twice daily for 3 days if available.

    !!! warning "CAUTION"
        Standard iodine and chlorine tablets do NOT reliably kill Cryptosporidium. Boiling or filtration (1 micron absolute or UV) is required.

=== "Cholera"

    ### Cholera (*Vibrio cholerae*)

    **Source:** Contaminated water and food in endemic regions (South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, Haiti, parts of Central/South America).

    **Incubation:** 12 hours to 5 days.

    **Symptoms:** Sudden onset of painless, massive watery diarrhea ("rice water stool"), vomiting, rapid dehydration. Can lose 1 quart (1 liter) of fluid per hour. Death from dehydration within 12-24 hours without treatment.

    !!! danger "WARNING"
        Cholera can kill a healthy adult in under 24 hours through dehydration alone. ORS must begin immediately.

    **Field Treatment:**

    1. Begin ORS immediately — match intake to output. A severely dehydrated cholera patient needs 4-8 quarts (4-8 liters) in the first 4-6 hours.
    2. Doxycycline 300 mg single dose (reduces duration and fluid loss by ~50%).
    3. Alternative: Azithromycin 1 g single dose.
    4. Continue ORS until diarrhea resolves (typically 3-5 days).

=== "E. coli"

    ### Pathogenic E. coli

    **Source:** Fecal contamination of water and food. Multiple pathogenic strains exist.

    **Incubation:** 1-10 days depending on strain.

    **Symptoms:** Watery or bloody diarrhea, cramping, nausea, vomiting. Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC/O157:H7) causes bloody diarrhea and can cause kidney failure (hemolytic uremic syndrome).

    !!! danger "WARNING"
        Do NOT give antibiotics for suspected EHEC (bloody diarrhea without fever). Antibiotics may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome. Hydrate and evacuate.

    **Field Treatment (non-bloody diarrhea):**

    1. ORS for fluid replacement.
    2. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 3 days (if available and diarrhea is non-bloody).
    3. Alternative: Azithromycin 500 mg daily for 3 days.

=== "Hepatitis A"

    ### Hepatitis A

    **Source:** Fecal-oral route via contaminated water or food. Common in regions with poor sanitation.

    **Incubation:** 15-50 days (average 28 days).

    **Symptoms:** Fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, loss of appetite, low-grade fever. After several days: dark urine, pale stools, jaundice (yellowing of skin and eyes).

    **Field Treatment:**

    1. No specific antiviral treatment exists. The illness is self-limiting (2-6 weeks).
    2. Rest and hydration.
    3. Avoid alcohol (additional liver stress).
    4. Maintain caloric intake — small, frequent meals.
    5. Evacuate if jaundice develops, if the patient cannot keep fluids down, or if confusion develops (indicates liver failure).

    **Prevention:** Hepatitis A vaccine provides long-term immunity. Consider vaccination before travel to endemic regions.

## Prevention

### Water Treatment Methods

| Method | Effective Against | Time Required | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling | All pathogens | 1 min (3 min above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m) | Most reliable method |
| Filtration (0.2 micron) | Bacteria, protozoa | Immediate | Does not remove viruses |
| Filtration + purifier | All pathogens | Immediate | Purifiers add chemical or UV stage |
| Chlorine dioxide | All pathogens | 30 min (4 hours for crypto) | Effective against Cryptosporidium |
| Iodine | Bacteria, most viruses, Giardia | 30 min | NOT reliable for Cryptosporidium |
| UV (SteriPEN/SODIS) | All pathogens | 90 sec (SteriPEN) / 6 hours (SODIS) | Water must be clear |

### Hygiene Practices

- Wash hands with soap and water (or ash and water) before eating, after defecation, and before treating wounds.
- Dig latrines at least 200 feet (60 m) from any water source and at least 200 feet from camp.
- Do not share water bottles or eating utensils during a diarrheal illness.

## Common Mistakes

- **Drinking untreated water because it looks clean.** Clear mountain streams routinely carry Giardia and Cryptosporidium from animal feces upstream.
- **Replacing fluids with water only.** Diarrhea depletes sodium, potassium, and glucose. Plain water dilutes remaining electrolytes and can cause hyponatremia. Use ORS.
- **Rationing water in a hot environment.** Drink when thirsty. Ration sweat (stay in shade, minimize exertion), not water.
- **Using iodine tablets and assuming all pathogens are killed.** Cryptosporidium is iodine-resistant. Boil or filter.
- **Giving antibiotics for bloody diarrhea without fever.** Suspected EHEC (E. coli O157:H7) can worsen with antibiotics. Hydrate and evacuate.
- **Ignoring early signs of dehydration.** Thirst means you are already 1-2% dehydrated. Drink proactively.

## Quick Reference

**ORS Recipe (per 1 quart / 1 liter clean water):**

- 6 teaspoons sugar + 1/2 teaspoon salt. Stir. Discard after 24 hours.

**Dehydration Assessment:**

| Check | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urine color | Dark yellow | Amber/brown | None |
| Skin turgor | Normal | Slow return (2-3 sec) | Tenting (>3 sec) |
| Mental status | Normal | Irritable, dizzy | Confused, unconscious |
| Pulse | Normal | Rapid | Rapid, weak, thready |
| Action | ORS, 1-2 cups/hr | ORS, 2-4 cups/hr | Evacuate |

**Waterborne Illness Quick ID:**

| Disease | Onset | Key Feature | Antibiotic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Giardia | 1-3 weeks | Sulfurous belching, greasy stool | Metronidazole |
| Cryptosporidium | 2-10 days | Watery diarrhea, chlorine-resistant | Self-limiting |
| Cholera | 12 hrs-5 days | Massive rice-water stool | Doxycycline |
| E. coli | 1-10 days | Watery or bloody diarrhea | Ciprofloxacin* |
| Hepatitis A | 15-50 days | Jaundice, dark urine | None (supportive) |

*Do not give antibiotics if bloody diarrhea without fever (suspect EHEC).
