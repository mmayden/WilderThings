# Improvised Medicine: Field Treatment Without Commercial Supplies

> Treat wounds, manage pain, and prevent infection using natural and improvised materials when no medical kit is available.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. It does not replace
    professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical
    provider. Improvised treatments carry higher risk than standard medical care. Seek
    professional help as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Clean water under pressure is the most important wound care tool you have — irrigate aggressively before applying any improvised dressing.
- Honey (raw, unprocessed) is the strongest evidence-based natural antiseptic. Apply directly to wounds and change dressings daily.
- Willow bark contains salicin (a precursor to aspirin). Chew the inner bark or brew as tea for mild to moderate pain relief.
- Sphagnum moss is absorbent, mildly acidic, and has documented antiseptic properties. Use as wound packing or dressing material.
- Never delay evacuation to attempt improvised treatment for deep wounds, suspected fractures with skin breaks, or signs of systemic infection.

## Natural Antiseptics

### Honey

Raw, unprocessed honey has broad-spectrum antibacterial properties. Medical-grade honey (Manuka) is ideal, but any raw honey works in a field setting.

1. Irrigate the wound thoroughly with clean water first.
2. Apply a layer of honey approximately 1/8 inch (3 mm) thick directly over the wound.
3. Cover with the cleanest available dressing material (cloth, large leaves, inner bark).
4. Change the dressing and reapply honey every 12-24 hours.

!!! note
    Honey's antibacterial action comes from hydrogen peroxide production, low pH, and high osmolarity. These properties are destroyed by heat — do not boil honey before use.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Never apply honey to wounds in patients with known bee-product allergies. Do not use honey from unknown sources that may contain grayanotoxins (rhododendron/azalea regions).

### Sphagnum Moss

Sphagnum moss (peat moss) was used as wound dressing through both World Wars. It absorbs up to 20 times its dry weight in fluid and creates a mildly acidic environment hostile to bacteria.

1. Harvest sphagnum moss from bogs, wetlands, or moist forest floors. Identify by its pale green or tan color and sponge-like texture.
2. Wring out excess water. If possible, rinse with clean water.
3. Pack loosely into or over the wound as an absorbent dressing.
4. Secure with strips of cloth, cordage, or bark.
5. Replace when saturated — at least every 12 hours.

!!! tip
    Sphagnum moss is most abundant in boreal forests, peat bogs, and temperate rainforests. In arid or tropical environments, use other methods.

### Tannin-Rich Solutions

Tannins from bark and plants are astringent and have mild antibacterial and antifungal properties. Use as wound washes, not primary wound packing.

=== "Oak Bark"
    Boil a double handful of crushed inner oak bark in 1 quart (1 liter) of water for 20 minutes. Cool to body temperature. Use as an irrigation solution or soak.

=== "Pine Bark / Needles"
    Boil crushed pine inner bark or a large handful of needles in 1 quart (1 liter) of water for 15 minutes. Cool before use. Also provides mild vitamin C supplementation if consumed.

=== "Tea (if available)"
    Brew strong black or green tea. The tannic acid acts as an astringent wash. Steep 3-4 bags or a large handful of loose leaves in 1 cup (240 mL) of boiled water for 10 minutes.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Tannin solutions are wound washes, not substitutes for thorough irrigation with clean water. Irrigate first, then apply tannin solution as a secondary rinse.

### Pine Resin

Fresh pine, spruce, or fir resin has mild antiseptic properties and creates a natural waterproof barrier over small wounds.

1. Collect soft, sticky resin from tree wounds or sap blisters.
2. Warm gently between your fingers to soften.
3. Apply a thin layer directly over small, clean cuts or abrasions.
4. Cover with a leaf or cloth dressing.

!!! note
    Pine resin works best on small, superficial wounds. Do not use on deep or puncture wounds — it traps debris and prevents drainage.

## Pain Management

### Willow Bark

The inner bark of willow trees (*Salix* species) contains salicin, which the body converts to salicylic acid — the active compound in aspirin.

**Identification:** Willows grow near water. Look for narrow, lance-shaped leaves, flexible branches, and rough, furrowed bark on mature trees. White willow (*Salix alba*) and black willow (*Salix nigra*) are most common in North America and Europe.

**Preparation:**

=== "Chewing"
    Strip a thumb-sized piece of inner bark (the moist, lighter layer beneath the outer bark). Chew slowly for 10-15 minutes. Swallow the saliva; spit out the fiber.

=== "Tea"
    Shave or crush 2 tablespoons of inner bark. Steep in 1 cup (240 mL) of boiled water for 15-20 minutes. Strain and drink. Repeat every 4-6 hours as needed.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Willow bark has the same contraindications as aspirin. Do not give to children under 16 (Reye's syndrome risk), patients with bleeding disorders, active GI bleeding, or known aspirin allergy. It increases bleeding — do not use when hemorrhage control is a concern.

**Effective dose:** Approximately 1-2 grams of inner bark per dose provides roughly 60-120 mg of salicin. Expect mild to moderate pain relief, not full analgesic effect.

### Cold Therapy

Cold reduces swelling, slows nerve conduction (reducing pain), and limits inflammatory response.

1. Wrap ice, snow, or cold water in a cloth. Never apply ice directly to skin.
2. Apply to the injury for 15-20 minutes.
3. Remove for at least 40 minutes before reapplying.
4. Repeat 3-4 times daily for the first 48 hours after injury.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not use cold therapy on patients with hypothermia, frostbite, or impaired circulation. In cold environments, weigh pain relief against heat loss.

### Other Field Analgesics

| Source | Preparation | Effect | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clove (if available) | Chew whole clove or apply oil to gums | Dental pain (eugenol is a local anesthetic) | Topical use only |
| Elevation | Raise injured limb above heart level | Reduces throbbing, swelling | Not effective for torso injuries |
| Distraction / breathing | Slow, deep breaths; 4-count inhale, 4-count exhale | Reduces pain perception | Supplement, not replacement |

## Improvised Bandages and Dressings

When commercial bandages are unavailable, prioritize cleanliness over perfection.

### Dressing Materials (inner layer, touches wound)

- **Boiled cloth strips.** Tear clean cotton clothing into strips. Boil for 10 minutes if possible. Wring dry before applying.
- **Sphagnum moss.** See above. Excellent absorbency.
- **Inner bark of cedar, basswood, or birch.** Peel thin, flexible sheets. Softer inner layers work best.
- **Large, non-irritating leaves.** Plantain (*Plantago major*), mullein, or burdock leaves. Wash before use.

### Securing Bandages

- **Cloth strips** torn from clothing, spiral-wrapped over the dressing.
- **Cordage** made from plant fibers — see [Cordage](../tools-and-craft/cordage.md) for techniques.
- **Flexible bark strips** wrapped and tied.
- **Adhesive pine resin** applied to edges of a leaf dressing to hold it in place.

!!! tip
    A triangular bandage (sling) can be improvised from any large piece of fabric cut or folded diagonally. Use for arm immobilization, wound binding, or as a pressure pad.

### Improvised Butterfly Closures

For clean cuts that need edge approximation:

1. Cut thin adhesive strips from duct tape, athletic tape, or tree resin–coated cloth.
2. Apply strips perpendicular to the wound, pulling edges together.
3. Space strips 1/4 inch (6 mm) apart along the wound length.
4. Leave gaps between strips to allow drainage.

## Splinting with Natural Materials

Immobilize suspected fractures and severe sprains to prevent further injury and reduce pain. See [Fractures and Splints](../medical/fractures-and-splints.md) for detailed techniques.

### Splint Materials

| Material | Best For | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Straight, rigid sticks | Long bone fractures (forearm, lower leg) | Use two — one on each side of the limb |
| Bark slabs | Wrist, ankle, forearm | Moldable when wet, rigid when dry |
| Rolled clothing / sleeping pad | Padding between splint and skin | Prevents pressure sores |
| Cordage or cloth strips | Securing splint | Tie above and below the fracture, not over it |

### Splinting Principles

1. Immobilize the joint above and below the suspected fracture.
2. Pad all bony prominences (knuckles, ankles, elbows) to prevent pressure injury.
3. Splint in the position found. Do not attempt to straighten an angulated fracture unless circulation to the extremity is compromised.
4. Check circulation distal to the splint (fingers/toes) every 30 minutes: color, warmth, sensation, pulse if palpable.
5. Loosen immediately if the extremity turns blue, white, cold, or numb.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Open fractures (bone visible or protruding through skin) carry extreme infection risk. Irrigate the wound, cover with the cleanest dressing available, splint, and evacuate. Do not push bone back through the skin.

## Managing Infection in the Field

Without antibiotics, infection management depends entirely on mechanical cleaning and drainage.

1. **Irrigate twice daily** with the cleanest water available under pressure.
2. **Apply warm compresses** for 20-30 minutes, 3-4 times daily. Heat draws blood flow and immune cells to the area.
3. **Keep wounds open.** Do not close an infected wound. Pack loosely with moist dressing material to allow continued drainage.
4. **Apply honey** to the wound bed after each irrigation and before redressing.
5. **Monitor and mark.** Track redness borders with charcoal or a pen. Note the time. Expanding redness over hours is spreading infection.

See [Wound Infection](../medical/wound-infection.md) for full infection recognition and management.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Red streaking from the wound toward the trunk, fever above 101°F (38.3°C), confusion, or rapid heart rate at rest mean the infection is systemic. No improvised treatment can address sepsis. Evacuate immediately.

## When NOT to Attempt Field Treatment

Some conditions exceed the capability of improvised medicine. Attempting treatment wastes time and may cause further harm.

- **Suspected spinal injury.** Immobilize and evacuate. Do not move the patient unless the scene is unsafe.
- **Open fractures with arterial bleeding.** Control bleeding, splint, evacuate.
- **Abdominal or chest wounds with evisceration or sucking sounds.** Cover, seal, evacuate.
- **Severe burns covering more than 15% of body surface area.** Cool, cover, evacuate.
- **Signs of sepsis.** See above. IV antibiotics and fluids are required.
- **Dental abscesses with facial swelling.** Risk of airway compromise. Evacuate.
- **Snake envenomation with systemic symptoms.** Antivenom is the treatment. Immobilize the limb and evacuate.

!!! note
    The decision to treat in the field vs. evacuate depends on distance to care, available transport, patient condition, and environmental hazards. When in doubt, evacuate.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using improvised remedies instead of basic wound hygiene.** No natural antiseptic substitutes for aggressive irrigation with clean water. Irrigate first — always.
- **Applying plant material to wounds without washing it.** Unwashed leaves, bark, and moss introduce soil bacteria. Rinse all materials with clean water before wound contact.
- **Consuming unidentified plants for pain relief.** Many plants with folk medicinal reputations are toxic. Use only positively identified species.
- **Packing wounds too tightly with moss or bark.** Tight packing prevents drainage and creates an anaerobic environment that promotes dangerous bacterial growth.
- **Over-relying on improvised splints without rechecking circulation.** A splint that swells beneath its bindings becomes a tourniquet. Check fingers and toes every 30 minutes.
- **Delaying evacuation to "try one more thing."** Improvised medicine buys time. It does not replace definitive care.

## Quick Reference

**Natural Antiseptic Priority:**

1. Clean water irrigation (always first)
2. Raw honey — apply directly to wound
3. Sphagnum moss — absorbent wound packing
4. Tannin wash (oak/pine bark tea) — secondary rinse
5. Pine resin — small wound sealant only

**Improvised Pain Relief:**

| Method | Dose / Application | Onset | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Willow bark tea | 2 tbsp bark in 1 cup water, every 4-6 hrs | 30-60 min | 3-4 hrs |
| Cold therapy | 15-20 min on, 40 min off | Immediate | During application |
| Elevation | Raise limb above heart | Immediate | While elevated |

**Evacuate Immediately If:**

- Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) with wound
- Red streaking from wound toward trunk
- Bone visible through skin
- Confusion or altered mental status
- Abdominal or chest penetration
- Uncontrolled bleeding despite pressure
