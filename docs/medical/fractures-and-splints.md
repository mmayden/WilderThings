# Fractures and Splints

> Identify fractures, immobilize injuries using improvised and commercial splints, and recognize spinal injuries requiring extreme caution.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. Improper splinting or moving a patient with a spinal injury can cause permanent paralysis or death. This guide does not replace professional medical training. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Splint injuries in the position found — do not attempt to straighten or realign deformed limbs unless circulation is compromised.
- Always immobilize the joint above and the joint below the fracture site.
- Check circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) distal to the injury before and after splinting.
- Suspect spinal injury in any patient with a mechanism of injury involving significant force — falls greater than standing height, vehicle accidents, diving injuries, or direct blows to the head or spine.
- An open fracture (bone piercing skin) is a surgical emergency. Cover the wound, splint, and evacuate.

---

## Fracture Types

### Closed Fracture

The bone is broken but the skin is intact. Signs include:

- Deformity — angulation, shortening, or rotation compared to the uninjured side.
- Swelling and bruising at the injury site.
- Point tenderness — pain localized to a specific spot when pressed.
- Crepitus — a grinding sensation or sound when the fracture site moves (do not intentionally test for this).
- Loss of function — inability or unwillingness to use the limb.
- Guarding — the patient instinctively protects the area.

### Open (Compound) Fracture

The bone has broken through the skin or the skin has been lacerated by the force that caused the fracture. This creates a direct path for infection to reach the bone (osteomyelitis).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never push a protruding bone back into the wound. Cover the exposed bone with a moist, sterile dressing, splint around it, and evacuate immediately.

**Open fracture management:**

1. Control bleeding with direct pressure around (not on) the protruding bone.
2. Irrigate the wound with at least 1 liter of clean water.
3. Cover with a sterile, moist dressing — saline-soaked gauze is ideal.
4. Splint the limb in the position found, building up padding around the protrusion.
5. Administer antibiotics if available: cephalexin 500 mg four times daily, or amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily. Begin within 3 hours of injury.
6. Evacuate. Open fractures require surgical debridement.

### Greenstick Fracture

Common in children. The bone bends and partially breaks, like a green twig. The limb may appear bent but not fully deformed. Splint as you would a closed fracture.

### Stress Fracture

A hairline crack from repetitive overuse. Presents as localized pain that worsens with activity and improves with rest. No deformity. Diagnose by point tenderness over the bone. Treatment: rest and offload the affected bone.

## Fracture Assessment

### Systematic Examination

1. **Look** — compare both sides. Note swelling, bruising, deformity, wounds, skin color distal to the injury.
2. **Feel** — palpate along the bone gently. Identify the point of maximum tenderness. Check for crepitus (but do not intentionally manipulate).
3. **Move** — ask the patient to actively move the extremity. Do not force passive motion through a suspected fracture. Note range-of-motion limitations and pain.

### CSM Check (Circulation, Sensation, Movement)

Perform this check before and after every splinting procedure, and recheck every 15-30 minutes.

| Check | How | Normal Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Circulation | Press a fingernail or toenail until it blanches white, then release. Check pulse distal to the injury. | Color returns in less than 2 seconds (capillary refill). Pulse present. |
| Sensation | Touch the skin distal to the injury. Ask "Can you feel this?" | Patient reports normal feeling. No numbness or tingling. |
| Movement | Ask the patient to wiggle fingers or toes. | Active movement without excessive pain. |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If CSM is absent — no pulse, no sensation, no movement — the limb is in jeopardy. Gently realign the limb toward its anatomical position using steady traction until circulation returns, then splint in that position. This is the one situation where you move a deformed fracture.

## Splinting Principles

### General Rules

1. **Splint in position found** unless CSM is compromised.
2. **Immobilize one joint above and one joint below** the fracture.
3. **Pad all bony prominences** — ankles, knees, elbows, wrists. Pressure sores develop rapidly under splints.
4. **Secure the splint firmly but not tightly.** You should be able to slide one finger under the wrapping.
5. **Elevate** the splinted limb when possible to reduce swelling.
6. **Apply cold** — ice or cold water in a bag, wrapped in cloth, 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off. Never apply ice directly to skin.
7. **Recheck CSM** after application. If CSM worsens, loosen or reposition the splint immediately.
8. **Document** the time of splinting and CSM findings.

### Padding

Adequate padding prevents:

- Pressure sores over bony prominences.
- Nerve compression causing numbness.
- Circulation impairment from rigid materials pressing into tissue.

Use any soft material: clothing, towels, moss, dried grass, foam, bubble wrap, socks.

## Splinting by Body Region

### Forearm and Wrist

1. Place padding in the palm — a rolled sock or bandage. This maintains the hand in the "position of function" (as if holding a can).
2. Place a rigid splint along the palmar side of the forearm, extending from the fingertips to past the elbow.
3. Place a second splint on the dorsal (back) side if available.
4. Secure with wraps at the hand, mid-forearm, and above the elbow. Avoid wrapping directly over the fracture site.
5. Support with a sling and swathe.

### Upper Arm (Humerus)

1. Place a padded rigid splint along the outer side of the arm.
2. Wrap to secure, leaving the elbow at approximately 90 degrees.
3. Apply a sling to support the forearm.
4. Apply a swathe (wrap around the torso) to prevent the arm from swinging.

### Shoulder and Collarbone (Clavicle)

1. Place the arm in a sling with the elbow at 90 degrees.
2. Apply a swathe to bind the arm against the torso.
3. Place padding (rolled towel) in the armpit.
4. Do not attempt to reduce a shoulder dislocation without training — you risk damaging the axillary nerve and blood vessels.

### Finger

1. Buddy-tape the injured finger to an adjacent uninjured finger.
2. Place padding between the fingers to prevent skin maceration.
3. For severely angulated finger fractures, splint in a slightly flexed position using a tongue depressor, popsicle stick, or small twig, padded and taped.

### Femur (Thighbone)

Femur fractures are serious injuries. The thigh can hold 1-2 liters of internal blood loss.

**Improvised traction splint procedure:**

1. Cut or find two sturdy poles — one extending from the armpit to 12 inches (30 cm) past the foot, one from the groin to 12 inches (30 cm) past the foot.
2. Pad the groin and armpit thoroughly.
3. Secure the poles to the body at the chest, hip, and above and below the knee. Do not wrap directly over the fracture.
4. Attach an ankle hitch: wrap padding around the ankle, then tie a loop that can be connected to the foot end of the outer pole.
5. Apply traction by pulling the foot-end of the loop until the injured leg is the same length as the uninjured leg. The patient should report decreased pain when traction is correct.
6. Secure the traction in place.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not apply a traction splint if the fracture is near the knee, the pelvis is fractured, or the lower leg is also fractured. In these cases, immobilize the entire leg using padded board splints on both sides, from armpit to foot.

### Lower Leg (Tibia/Fibula)

1. Place padded splints on both sides of the leg, extending from above the knee to past the foot.
2. Alternatively, place one splint along the back of the leg from the buttock to past the foot.
3. Wrap at the thigh, above and below the knee, mid-calf, and the ankle/foot.
4. Immobilize the foot at 90 degrees to the leg if possible.

### Ankle and Foot

1. Apply a pillow splint: wrap a pillow or folded blanket around the ankle and foot.
2. Secure with tape or wraps.
3. Alternatively, build an L-shaped splint from rigid material, padded, along the back of the calf and under the foot.
4. Do not remove the boot or shoe if swelling is already present — the boot acts as a compression dressing.

### Pelvis

Pelvic fractures are life-threatening. The pelvis can hold 3+ liters of internal blood loss.

1. Wrap a sheet, belt, jacket, or commercial pelvic binder tightly around the hips at the level of the greater trochanters (the bony bumps on the outer hips).
2. Tie or clip the binder snugly — it should compress the pelvis to reduce the fracture space and control bleeding.
3. Do not log-roll or repeatedly move the patient. Every movement can disrupt clots.
4. Treat for shock — see [Trauma and Triage](trauma-and-triage.md).

## Slings and Swathes

### Triangular Bandage Sling

1. Place one point of the triangle at the elbow of the injured arm.
2. Bring the upper end over the shoulder on the uninjured side.
3. Bring the lower end up and over the shoulder on the injured side.
4. Tie the ends behind the neck with a square knot. Pad the knot.
5. Fold and pin or tie the material at the elbow to secure.
6. The hand should be slightly higher than the elbow (about 4 inches / 10 cm above elbow level).

### Improvised Sling

- Pin the sleeve of a buttoned shirt to the chest of the shirt.
- Tuck the hand inside a buttoned jacket between buttons.
- Use a belt, scarf, rope, or strip of fabric looped around the neck and under the forearm.

### Swathe

Wrap a broad band of material around the torso and over the splinted/slung arm to prevent movement. The swathe prevents the arm from swinging away from the body.

## Spinal Injury Precautions

### Suspect Spinal Injury When

- The mechanism of injury involves significant force to the head, neck, or back:
  - Falls greater than standing height (approximately 5 feet / 1.5 meters).
  - Motor vehicle, bicycle, or horseback accidents.
  - Diving into shallow water.
  - Direct blows or penetrating injuries to the spine.
  - Lightning strikes.
- The patient complains of neck or back pain.
- The patient has numbness, tingling, or weakness in the extremities.
- The patient is unconscious with an unknown mechanism of injury.

### When NOT to Move Someone

Do not move a patient if you suspect spinal injury **unless:**

- The scene is immediately dangerous (fire, flood, structural collapse, active threat).
- You cannot access the airway to manage a life-threatening obstruction or perform CPR.
- You need to roll the patient to control life-threatening hemorrhage on the posterior body.

In all other cases, stabilize the spine in place and wait for evacuation resources.

### Manual Spinal Stabilization

1. Kneel above the patient's head.
2. Place your hands on both sides of the head, covering the ears.
3. Hold the head in a neutral position aligned with the spine. Do not pull traction.
4. Maintain this hold continuously until the patient is fully immobilized on a backboard or until professional rescuers take over.
5. If you must release, build improvised padding (rolled towels, clothing, sandbags) on both sides of the head and tape across the forehead and chin.

### Log Roll

When you must turn a spinal injury patient (to assess the back, place on a backboard, or manage vomiting):

1. One person maintains inline head stabilization.
2. Two or more people position themselves along the patient's body.
3. On command from the person at the head, roll the patient as a single unit, keeping the spine aligned.
4. The head, shoulders, hips, and legs must all move together simultaneously.
5. Assess or intervene, then roll back.

### Improvised Spinal Immobilization

- Use a door, tabletop, wide plank, or ironing board as a backboard.
- Pad the natural curves of the spine (behind the neck and lower back).
- Secure the patient to the board at the forehead, chest, hips, and legs.
- Immobilize the head last — secure the torso first to prevent the head from moving relative to the body.
- Tape or tie across the forehead (not the neck) with padding on both sides.

## Splinting Materials from Nature

| Natural Material | Best Used For | Preparation |
|---|---|---|
| Straight hardwood sticks | Arm and leg splints | Select straight pieces, pad with cloth, moss, or grass |
| Bark slabs (thick bark from fir, cottonwood, or birch) | Curved splints for forearms and lower legs | Peel a slab large enough to wrap around the limb |
| Cattail leaves (bundled) | Padding under rigid splints | Bundle thick layers; surprisingly rigid when compressed |
| Bamboo (split) | Rigid splints for any limb | Split lengthwise, smooth sharp edges, pad generously |
| Rolled newspaper or magazines | Rigid forearm or lower-leg splints | Roll tightly and tape; surprisingly strong |
| Sam Splint equivalent | Shape a thin, green branch into a curve | Bend a pliable branch into a U or T shape |
| Mud or clay (dried) | Emergency cast for forearm | Apply wet, mold into shape, allow to dry. Fragile — temporary only |

### Constructing a Natural Splint

1. Select two pieces of rigid material longer than the injured segment (extend beyond the joints above and below).
2. Pad all surfaces that will contact skin — use clothing, moss, dried grass, or bark strips.
3. Place splints on opposite sides of the limb.
4. Secure using strips of cloth, vines, cordage, boot laces, or belts at four or more points.
5. Do not tie directly over the fracture site.
6. Check CSM immediately and every 15 minutes after.

## Dislocations

### Recognition

- Joint appears deformed — compare to the other side.
- The limb is locked in an abnormal position.
- Patient cannot move the joint through normal range of motion.
- Swelling and severe pain at the joint.

### Field Reduction (Wilderness/Austere Setting Only)

Attempt field reduction only when evacuation is delayed more than several hours and when CSM distal to the dislocation is compromised. Attempted reduction can worsen the injury if performed incorrectly.

**Shoulder (anterior dislocation — most common):**

1. Lay the patient face down on a raised surface (table, rock ledge) with the affected arm hanging straight down.
2. Attach 10-15 pounds (4.5-7 kg) of weight to the wrist (a bag of water or rocks).
3. Allow gravity and the weight to pull the arm downward for 20-30 minutes. Muscle spasm often releases, and the joint relocates with a palpable "clunk."
4. Alternatively, have the patient lie on their back. Slowly abduct the arm to 90 degrees, then externally rotate (turn the forearm outward) slowly. Stop if pain increases sharply.
5. After reduction, immobilize with a sling and swathe.
6. Recheck CSM.

**Finger dislocation:**

1. Grasp the finger beyond the dislocated joint.
2. Apply steady traction (pull) along the axis of the finger.
3. Gently guide the joint back into alignment.
4. Buddy-tape to the adjacent finger and splint.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never attempt field reduction of hip, knee, elbow, or spinal dislocations without advanced training. Incorrect technique can sever blood vessels or permanently damage nerves.

## Common Mistakes

- Splinting too tightly — restricting circulation causes more damage than the fracture itself. Check CSM every 15 minutes.
- Failing to immobilize the joints above and below the fracture — the splint is ineffective if the fracture can still move.
- Attempting to straighten a deformed limb when circulation is intact — splint in position found.
- Removing footwear from an ankle injury after swelling has begun — the shoe provides compression.
- Applying ice directly to skin — wrap ice in cloth. Frostbite atop a fracture is a preventable disaster.
- Moving a suspected spinal injury patient without a compelling reason (immediate danger to life).
- Using narrow cordage (paracord, wire) to secure splints — this creates pressure points that impair circulation.
- Forgetting to pad bony prominences under the splint — pressure sores develop within hours.
- Neglecting to check and document CSM before and after splinting.

## Quick Reference

| Injury | Immobilize | Key Concern | Evacuate? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finger fracture | Buddy tape + small splint | Loss of fine motor function | When possible |
| Forearm fracture | Elbow to fingertips, sling | Compartment syndrome (swelling in tight spaces) | Yes |
| Humerus fracture | Shoulder to elbow, sling + swathe | Radial nerve damage (wrist drop) | Yes |
| Clavicle fracture | Sling + swathe | Usually heals without surgery | When possible |
| Femur fracture | Traction splint or full-leg immobilization | 1-2 L internal blood loss, shock | Urgent |
| Tibia/fibula fracture | Knee to foot | Compartment syndrome | Yes |
| Pelvis fracture | Pelvic binder, do not move | 3+ L blood loss, life-threatening | Emergency |
| Spinal injury | Full spinal immobilization | Paralysis risk | Emergency — do not move |
| Open fracture (any) | Splint around wound, moist dressing | Infection, surgical emergency | Emergency |
