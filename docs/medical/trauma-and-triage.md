# Trauma Assessment and Field Triage

> Systematically assess trauma patients, prioritize casualties in mass-casualty situations, and manage life-threatening injuries using TCCC and wilderness medicine protocols.

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    This guide is for educational and emergency preparedness purposes only. Trauma management requires hands-on training and practice. This guide does not replace professional medical training, certification, or the judgment of a licensed medical provider. Seek professional care as soon as it becomes available.

## At a Glance

- Stop massive hemorrhage first — it kills faster than airway or breathing problems in trauma.
- Use the MARCH mnemonic for tactical trauma: Massive hemorrhage, Airway, Respiration, Circulation, Hypothermia/Head injury.
- The primary survey (ABCDE) takes 60-90 seconds and identifies all immediately life-threatening conditions.
- START triage categorizes casualties by survivability in under 30 seconds per patient.
- Treat shock aggressively — lay flat, control bleeding, keep warm, and evacuate.

---

## Scene Safety and Approach

Before touching any patient:

1. **Assess the scene for hazards** — fire, unstable structures, downed power lines, traffic, active threats, chemical exposure, hostile actors.
2. **Determine the mechanism of injury** — this tells you what injuries to suspect before you examine the patient.
3. **Count the patients** — if multiple casualties exist, shift to triage mode immediately.
4. **Use personal protective equipment** — gloves at minimum. Eye protection if blood exposure is likely.
5. **Establish a plan** — decide who does what before rushing in. Disorganized responders become additional casualties.

### Mechanism of Injury Clues

| Mechanism | Suspect |
|---|---|
| Fall > standing height | Spinal injury, pelvis fracture, bilateral calcaneus (heel) fractures, wrist fractures |
| Vehicle accident (unrestrained) | Cervical spine, aortic tear, liver/spleen laceration, femur fracture |
| Blast | Primary: lung blast injury, TBI; Secondary: penetrating fragments; Tertiary: blunt trauma from being thrown |
| Penetrating (knife, bullet) | Organ damage along the wound track, vascular injury |
| Crush injury (prolonged entrapment) | Crush syndrome — hyperkalemia upon release, kidney failure |

## MARCH Protocol (Tactical Trauma)

The MARCH mnemonic reorders traditional ABCDE to prioritize the leading cause of preventable death in trauma: hemorrhage.

### M — Massive Hemorrhage

1. Identify and control all life-threatening external bleeding immediately.
2. Apply direct pressure to compressible hemorrhage.
3. Pack deep wounds with hemostatic gauze (QuikClot Combat Gauze, Celox). Pack tightly from the deepest point outward. Hold pressure for 3-5 minutes.
4. Apply a tourniquet to any extremity hemorrhage that direct pressure cannot control. Place it 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) above the wound. See [First Aid Basics](first-aid-basics.md) for full tourniquet protocol.
5. For junctional hemorrhage (groin, axilla, neck) where a tourniquet cannot be placed, use wound packing with direct pressure. Commercial junctional tourniquets (SAM JT, CRoC) are ideal if available.
6. Note the time of tourniquet application.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not delay hemorrhage control to assess the airway. A patient can bleed to death from a femoral artery wound in 3-5 minutes. Hemorrhage kills faster than any airway problem in trauma.

### A — Airway

1. Open the airway. Use a jaw-thrust maneuver if spinal injury is suspected; head-tilt/chin-lift if not.
2. Clear visible obstructions — blood, vomit, teeth, debris. Use a finger sweep only if the object is visible.
3. Place an unconscious patient with an intact airway in the recovery position (if spinal injury is not suspected).
4. Insert a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) if available — lubricate and insert into one nostril with the bevel toward the septum. Size: measure from the nostril to the earlobe.
5. Insert an oropharyngeal airway (OPA) if the patient has no gag reflex. Size: measure from the corner of the mouth to the earlobe.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not insert an NPA if there is suspected mid-face or basilar skull fracture (raccoon eyes, Battle's sign behind the ear, CSF leaking from the nose or ears). Use an OPA instead.

### R — Respiration

Expose the chest and assess breathing:

1. **Look** — chest rise symmetry, open wounds, flail segments (paradoxical movement).
2. **Listen** — breath sounds bilaterally, equal or unequal.
3. **Feel** — chest wall stability, subcutaneous emphysema (crackling sensation under the skin indicating air leak).

**Manage immediately:**

- **Open pneumothorax (sucking chest wound):** An open wound in the chest wall that allows air into the pleural space.
  1. Apply a chest seal (vented if available) — commercial chest seals (HyFin, SAM) are ideal.
  2. Improvised seal: petroleum gauze, plastic wrap, or any airtight material taped on three sides (creating a flutter valve) or on all four sides if using a vented seal.
  3. If a fully sealed dressing is used and the patient worsens (increasing difficulty breathing, dropping oxygen), suspect tension pneumothorax and burp the seal — lift one corner to release trapped air, then reseal.

- **Tension pneumothorax:** Air enters the pleural space with each breath but cannot escape, progressively compressing the lung and shifting the heart.

  Field signs:
  - Severe, worsening respiratory distress.
  - Absent breath sounds on the affected side.
  - Tracheal deviation AWAY from the affected side (late sign — do not wait for this).
  - Jugular vein distention (JVD) — engorged neck veins (may be absent if also hypotensive from blood loss).
  - Hypotension, tachycardia, cyanosis.
  - Hyperresonance (hollow, drum-like sound) when tapping the affected side.
  - Subcutaneous emphysema spreading from the chest.

  **Field treatment — needle decompression:**
  1. Locate the 2nd intercostal space at the mid-clavicular line (two finger-widths below the collarbone, at the midpoint of the collarbone) on the affected side. Alternative: 5th intercostal space at the anterior axillary line (nipple level, at the front of the armpit).
  2. Insert a 14-gauge needle (at least 3.25 inches / 8 cm long for adults) perpendicular to the chest wall, just above the top of the 3rd rib (to avoid the neurovascular bundle that runs beneath each rib).
  3. A rush of air confirms the diagnosis.
  4. Leave the catheter in place. Secure with tape.
  5. Reassess breathing. The patient should improve rapidly.
  6. This is a temporizing measure — definitive treatment requires a chest tube.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Needle decompression is a life-saving but invasive procedure. Perform it only when you are confident in the diagnosis and the patient is deteriorating. An incorrect diagnosis results in an iatrogenic pneumothorax.

- **Flail chest:** Three or more adjacent ribs broken in two or more places, creating a free-floating segment that moves paradoxically (inward during inhalation).
  1. Support the flail segment with a hand or a light bulky dressing held loosely in place. Positioning the patient injured-side-down can splint the segment if no spinal injury is suspected and breathing improves.
  2. Give pain control if available. Pain is what stops these patients breathing deeply, and shallow breathing is what kills them.
  3. The real danger is the underlying pulmonary contusion. Monitor for worsening respiratory distress.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do **not** strap, tape, sandbag, or bind the chest to immobilize a flail segment. PHTLS states that efforts to stabilize the flail segment by such means are **contraindicated** — they restrict chest wall motion, impair ventilation, and accelerate respiratory failure in a patient whose lung is already injured. Management is pain relief, ventilatory support, and monitoring for deterioration.

### C — Circulation (Shock)

Assess and treat circulatory shock. See the Shock section below for details.

### H — Hypothermia / Head Injury

1. Prevent hypothermia in every trauma patient. Trauma patients lose thermoregulatory ability. The "lethal triad" of trauma death is hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy. See [Hypothermia and Heatstroke](hypothermia-and-heatstroke.md).
2. Manage head injuries — see the Head Injury section below.

## Primary Survey (ABCDE)

The civilian primary survey uses the ABCDE framework. Complete it in 60-90 seconds.

### A — Airway (with cervical spine protection)

- Is the patient talking? If yes, the airway is open.
- If not talking, open and assess as described in the MARCH section.
- Maintain cervical spine immobilization if mechanism suggests spinal injury.

### B — Breathing

- Respiratory rate: count for 15 seconds and multiply by 4.
- Normal adult: 12-20 breaths/min.
- Tachypnea (>20): pain, anxiety, chest injury, shock, metabolic acidosis.
- Bradypnea (<12): head injury, drug effect, fatigue, impending respiratory failure.
- Assess for the three immediately treatable chest injuries: open pneumothorax, tension pneumothorax, flail chest.

### C — Circulation

- Check pulse: rate, rhythm, strength.
- **A weak or absent radial pulse with a palpable carotid indicates poor perfusion — treat for shock.** Use this as a yes/no perfusion check, not a blood pressure estimate.
- Assess skin: color, temperature, moisture. Cool, pale, clammy skin indicates shock.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    You may have been taught the "80/70/60 rule" — radial pulse means systolic BP of at least 80 mmHg, femoral 70, carotid 60. **It is unreliable and has been dropped from ATLS since the 8th edition.**

    When it was tested against measured pressures in hypovolemic trauma patients, it predicted correctly in only 25% of cases and **overestimated** blood pressure in half of them, by an average of 34 mmHg. Overestimation is the dangerous direction: it makes a patient in serious shock look stable.

    What does hold true is the **order** in which pulses disappear — radial first, then femoral, then carotid. Use that as a rough perfusion trend, not a blood pressure number. Judge shock by the whole picture: pulse quality, skin, and mental status.
- Capillary refill: press a fingernail and release. Normal refill is under 2 seconds.
- Control any remaining hemorrhage not addressed in the M step.

### D — Disability (Neurological Status)

Use AVPU for rapid assessment:

| Level | Response |
|---|---|
| **A**lert | Eyes open spontaneously, oriented, follows commands |
| **V**erbal | Responds to voice (eyes open, withdraws, moans) |
| **P**ain | Responds only to painful stimulus (sternal rub, nail bed pressure) |
| **U**nresponsive | No response to any stimulus |

Check pupils: size, equality, reactivity to light. Unequal pupils after head trauma suggest herniation — this is a neurosurgical emergency.

### E — Exposure / Environment

1. Expose the patient completely — cut away clothing to find hidden injuries. Check the back by log-rolling.
2. Immediately cover and insulate after examination to prevent hypothermia.
3. Look for injuries you might have missed: posterior bleeding, impaled objects, deformities, bruising patterns.

## Secondary Survey

Perform the secondary survey only after all life-threatening conditions identified in the primary survey have been addressed. This is a head-to-toe systematic examination.

### SAMPLE History

Gather this information from the patient or bystanders:

- **S**igns and symptoms — what happened, what hurts?
- **A**llergies — medications, foods, environmental.
- **M**edications — current prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, supplements.
- **P**ast medical history — chronic conditions, surgeries, hospitalizations.
- **L**ast oral intake — when did they last eat or drink?
- **E**vents leading to the injury — what happened before, during, and after?

### Head-to-Toe Examination

Examine systematically. Use "look, feel, listen" at each body region:

1. **Head and face** — palpate the skull for depressions, lacerations, swelling. Check the ears for blood or clear fluid (CSF). Check the eyes for pupil size, equality, and tracking. Check the nose for deformity and drainage. Check the mouth for loose teeth, lacerations, airway obstructions.
2. **Neck** — palpate the cervical spine for tenderness, step-offs, or deformity. Check for JVD, tracheal position, subcutaneous emphysema. Do not remove a cervical collar if one is in place.
3. **Chest** — palpate each rib, the sternum, and clavicles. Listen for breath sounds in four quadrants (if a stethoscope is available). Check for paradoxical motion, crepitus.
4. **Abdomen** — palpate all four quadrants gently. Note rigidity, guarding, distension, or tenderness. A rigid, board-like abdomen indicates peritonitis.
5. **Pelvis** — apply gentle inward and downward pressure once. If unstable or painful, stop immediately and apply a pelvic binder. Do not rock the pelvis back and forth. See [Fractures and Splints](fractures-and-splints.md).
6. **Extremities** — check all four limbs for deformity, swelling, lacerations, pulse, sensation, movement. Compare sides.
7. **Back** — log-roll (with spinal precautions if indicated) and inspect the entire posterior surface. Run a gloved hand along the spine from the base of the skull to the tailbone, feeling for deformity or tenderness.

### Vital Signs

Take and record baseline vital signs. Reassess every 5-15 minutes depending on patient stability.

| Vital Sign | Normal Adult Range | Concerning Values |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | 60-100 bpm | >100 (tachycardia in trauma = shock until proven otherwise) |
| Respiratory rate | 12-20 breaths/min | <10 or >30 |
| Blood pressure (systolic) | 100-140 mmHg | <90 (hypotension — late sign of shock) |
| Temperature | 97.8-99.1 degF (36.5-37.3 degC) | <95 degF (35 degC) hypothermia |
| Pulse oximetry (if available) | 95-100% | <90% |

## Shock Recognition and Treatment

### Types of Shock in Trauma

| Type | Cause | Key Signs |
|---|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | Blood loss (most common in trauma) | Tachycardia, cool/pale/clammy skin, weak pulse, altered mental status |
| Obstructive | Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade | JVD, muffled heart sounds (tamponade), absent breath sounds (tension) |
| Neurogenic | Spinal cord injury | Bradycardia (slow heart rate) with hypotension, warm/dry skin below injury level |
| Septic | Infection (delayed presentation) | Fever, warm/flushed skin initially, progresses to cool/clammy |

### Estimated Blood Loss by Class

| Class | Blood Loss | Heart Rate | Mental Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | <750 mL (<15%) | Normal | Normal |
| II | 750-1500 mL (15-30%) | 100-120 | Anxious |
| III | 1500-2000 mL (30-40%) | 120-140 | Confused |
| IV | >2000 mL (>40%) | >140 or absent | Lethargic/unconscious |

### Shock Treatment (Field)

1. **Control the bleeding source.** Shock treatment is futile if hemorrhage continues.
2. Lay the patient flat. Elevate the legs 6-12 inches (15-30 cm) if no spinal injury, pelvic fracture, or head injury is suspected.
3. Keep the patient warm — insulate from the ground and cover with blankets. Prevent hypothermia aggressively.
4. Do not give oral fluids to a patient with altered mental status, abdominal trauma, or who may need surgery.
5. If the patient is alert with no abdominal injury, small sips of water are acceptable.
6. If IV access and fluids are available, administer a 500 mL bolus of normal saline or lactated Ringer's. Reassess after each bolus. Target a systolic BP of 80-90 mmHg in hemorrhagic shock (permissive hypotension) — aggressive fluid administration dilutes clotting factors and can worsen bleeding.
7. Reassess vital signs every 5 minutes.
8. Evacuate. Definitive shock treatment requires surgery and blood products.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not elevate the legs of a patient with a suspected head injury — this increases intracranial pressure. Keep the head of the bed elevated 30 degrees for isolated head injuries.

## Head Injury Management

### Classification

- **Mild (concussion):** GCS 13-15, brief or no loss of consciousness, headache, dizziness, confusion.
- **Moderate:** GCS 9-12, prolonged confusion, repeated vomiting, worsening headache.
- **Severe:** GCS 3-8, prolonged unconsciousness, focal neurological deficits, seizures.

### Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

| Response | Score |
|---|---|
| **Eye Opening** | |
| Spontaneous | 4 |
| To voice | 3 |
| To pain | 2 |
| None | 1 |
| **Verbal** | |
| Oriented | 5 |
| Confused | 4 |
| Inappropriate words | 3 |
| Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| None | 1 |
| **Motor** | |
| Obeys commands | 6 |
| Localizes pain | 5 |
| Withdraws from pain | 4 |
| Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| Extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| None | 1 |

**Total: 3-15.** Document and track over time. A declining GCS is more significant than a single reading.

### Field Management of Head Injury

1. Maintain the airway — vomiting and loss of consciousness are common. Suction or position to drain.
2. Control scalp bleeding — scalp wounds bleed profusely. Use direct pressure. Avoid clamping without visualization.
3. Elevate the head 30 degrees (unless spinal injury is suspected with an unstable mechanism).
4. Do not pack the ears or nose if clear fluid (CSF) is draining — this indicates a basilar skull fracture. Allow drainage and cover loosely.
5. Monitor neurological status using GCS and pupil checks every 15 minutes.
6. Note and document any seizure activity — duration, type, and body parts involved.
7. Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen (increase bleeding). Acetaminophen is acceptable for headache.
8. Keep the patient calm and minimize stimulation.

### Signs of Deterioration (Herniation)

These indicate rising intracranial pressure and impending brain herniation — a fatal progression without surgical intervention:

- Decreasing level of consciousness (declining GCS).
- Unilateral pupil dilation (blown pupil) — the pupil on the side of the lesion dilates and stops reacting to light.
- Cushing's triad: hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations — a late, pre-terminal sign.
- Posturing: decorticate (arms flexed, legs extended) or decerebrate (all extremities extended) — indicates severe brain injury.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A single dilated, non-reactive pupil after head trauma is a neurosurgical emergency. Hyperventilate the patient briefly (20 breaths/min in an adult) as a temporizing measure to reduce intracranial pressure. This buys minutes, not hours. Evacuate immediately.

## START Triage (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment)

Use START triage when casualties exceed available resources. Assess each patient in under 30 seconds.

### Triage Categories

| Category | Color | Priority | Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate | RED | 1st | Life-threatening but salvageable with immediate intervention |
| Delayed | YELLOW | 2nd | Serious but can tolerate a delay of hours without dying |
| Minor | GREEN | 3rd (Walking wounded) | Ambulatory, minor injuries |
| Expectant/Dead | BLACK | Last | Dead or injuries incompatible with survival given available resources |

### START Triage Algorithm

**Step 1: Can the patient walk?**
- Yes → tag GREEN (Minor). Direct them to a collection point. Move to the next patient.
- No → proceed to Step 2.

**Step 2: Is the patient breathing?**
- No → open the airway (head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw thrust).
  - Starts breathing → tag RED (Immediate).
  - Does not start breathing → tag BLACK (Expectant/Dead). Move to the next patient.
- Yes → proceed to Step 3.

**Step 3: What is the respiratory rate?**
- Greater than 30 breaths/minute → tag RED (Immediate). Move to the next patient.
- Less than 30 → proceed to Step 4.

**Step 4: What is the capillary refill?**
- Greater than 2 seconds (or radial pulse absent) → control any major bleeding, tag RED (Immediate). Move to the next patient.
- 2 seconds or less (or radial pulse present) → proceed to Step 5.

**Step 5: Can the patient follow simple commands?** ("Squeeze my fingers," "What is your name?")
- No → tag RED (Immediate).
- Yes → tag YELLOW (Delayed).

### Triage Principles

- **Do the greatest good for the greatest number.** This means not spending all resources on one unsalvageable patient while others die of treatable injuries.
- Triage is dynamic. Reassess patients as resources become available or as conditions change.
- Triage decisions are among the hardest in medicine. Accept that imperfect decisions made quickly save more lives than perfect decisions made slowly.
- Tag patients clearly — use commercial triage tags, colored tape, or write directly on the patient's forehead.

## Field Decision-Making

### Evacuation Priorities

Evacuate these patients first:

1. Uncontrolled hemorrhage despite tourniquet and packing.
2. Tension pneumothorax after needle decompression (needs chest tube).
3. Declining neurological status (head injury with herniation signs).
4. Open fractures.
5. Abdominal trauma with signs of peritonitis (rigid abdomen).
6. Pelvic fracture with shock.
7. Burns greater than 20% TBSA.
8. Crush injury patients before release from entrapment (if possible — crush syndrome can cause cardiac arrest upon release).

### When to Stay vs. When to Go

| Factor | Stay and Treat | Go (Evacuate) |
|---|---|---|
| Bleeding | Controlled with pressure/tourniquet | Uncontrolled, worsening |
| Airway | Patent, maintainable | Threatened, burns to face/neck |
| Breathing | Adequate after intervention | Deteriorating despite intervention |
| Neuro status | Stable or improving | Declining |
| Environment | Safe, shelter available | Hostile, exposed |
| Resources | Adequate for current needs | Exhausted or insufficient |
| Distance to help | Very far, transport dangerous | Feasible and transport available |

### Documentation in the Field

Write directly on the patient with a marker if no forms are available:

- Time of initial assessment.
- Injuries found.
- Treatments given (especially tourniquet time, medications, doses).
- Vital signs.
- Allergies.
- Triage category.

Use the patient's forehead, chest, or a piece of tape. Information that travels with the patient saves lives at the next level of care.

## Common Mistakes

- Tunnel vision on one dramatic injury while missing a second life-threatening problem — always complete the full primary survey.
- Focusing on the airway before controlling massive hemorrhage — in trauma, hemorrhage kills faster.
- Moving a patient with a suspected spinal injury before immobilization.
- Not rechecking tourniquet effectiveness — a tourniquet that has loosened during transport is worse than no tourniquet.
- Failing to check the back — log-roll every trauma patient. Posterior wounds are missed constantly.
- Over-resuscitating with IV fluids — aggressive fluid boluses dilute clotting factors and raise blood pressure, which dislodges clots and restarts hemorrhage. Target 80-90 mmHg systolic (permissive hypotension).
- Pronouncing a hypothermic patient dead in the field.
- Spending excessive time on one patient during mass casualty events — triage demands 30 seconds per patient, not 30 minutes.
- Not documenting tourniquet time — without this, downstream providers cannot make informed decisions about limb salvage.
- Elevating the legs of a patient with a head injury — this worsens intracranial pressure.
- Removing impaled objects — stabilize them in place. Removal can cause uncontrolled hemorrhage. The only exception is objects in the cheek obstructing the airway.
