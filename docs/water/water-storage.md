# Water Storage

> Store, treat, and transport water safely to maintain a reliable supply between collection trips.

## At a Glance

- Plan for a minimum of 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day — double in hot climates or during heavy exertion.
- Purify water BEFORE storing it. Contaminated water does not improve with time.
- Keep stored water covered, shaded, and off the ground to prevent recontamination.
- Improvised containers work — birch bark, bamboo, animal organs, clay pots, and plastic bottles all hold water.
- Rotate stored water every 48–72 hours in warm conditions to prevent bacterial regrowth.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never store unpurified water alongside purified water without clear, permanent marking. Cross-contamination from a single mistake causes illness that accelerates dehydration — the opposite of your goal.

## Volume Planning

### Daily Requirements

| Condition | Minimum Per Person |
|---|---|
| Temperate, low activity | 1 gallon (3.8 L) |
| Hot climate, moderate activity | 1.5 gallons (5.7 L) |
| Desert or heavy exertion | 2–3 gallons (7.5–11.4 L) |
| Cold climate (altitude, dry air) | 1.5 gallons (5.7 L) |

These numbers include drinking and cooking water only. Hygiene and wound care require additional volume.

### Group Planning

Multiply daily individual needs by group size and planned storage duration. For a group of four in temperate conditions with a 3-day cache: 4 people x 1 gallon x 3 days = 12 gallons (45.4 liters) minimum.

!!! tip
    Always store 25% more than calculated needs. Spills, leaks, and container failures happen. A reserve prevents crisis.

## Improvised Containers

### Birch Bark

Birch bark is naturally waterproof due to its high oil content. Suitable in northern forests across North America, Scandinavia, and northern Asia.

1. Peel a sheet of bark 18 x 24 inches (45 x 60 cm) from a dead or downed birch. Do not strip living trees — this kills them.
2. Fold into a rectangular trough or cone shape.
3. Pin corners and seams with thin wooden pegs or sew with spruce root cordage.
4. Seal seams with pine or spruce resin heated to liquid and applied with a stick.
5. Capacity: roughly 1–2 quarts (1–2 liters) per container depending on size.

### Bamboo Sections

Available throughout tropical and subtropical Asia, Central America, and parts of the southeastern United States.

1. Cut a bamboo stalk at a node (the solid joint). Leave the node intact — it forms a natural watertight bottom.
2. Cut again 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) above, just below the next node.
3. Hollow out any internal membrane with a stick.
4. Capacity: 1–3 cups (240–720 mL) per section, depending on stalk diameter.
5. Seal the open top with a leaf plug and cordage.

!!! tip
    Bamboo sections with both nodes intact make excellent canteens for transport. Drill or burn a small fill hole through one node.

### Clay Pots

If you have access to clay soil and fire, shape a water vessel.

1. Collect fine clay free of rocks and roots.
2. Form a pot with walls 3/8–1/2 inch (1–1.3 cm) thick. Thinner walls crack; thicker walls fire unevenly.
3. Let air-dry for 24–48 hours in shade. Direct sun causes cracking.
4. Fire in a hot campfire for 3–4 hours, surrounded by coals.
5. Let cool slowly in the ash. Do not quench with water.

Clay pots are porous and lose water through evaporation. This is a drawback for storage but an advantage for cooling — evaporative loss keeps the remaining water cooler than ambient temperature.

### Animal Stomach and Bladder

A cleaned animal stomach or bladder functions as a flexible, durable water bag. Used historically across every continent where large game is hunted.

1. Remove the stomach or bladder intact during field dressing.
2. Turn inside out. Scrape away all fat, tissue, and membrane fragments.
3. Rinse thoroughly with clean water at least 3 times.
4. Inflate and let dry briefly to check for holes.
5. Fill with purified water and tie the opening with cordage.
6. Capacity: 1–4 quarts (1–3.8 liters) depending on animal size. A deer stomach holds roughly 2 quarts (1.9 liters).

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Animal containers spoil within 2–3 days in warm weather. Rinse daily with purified water. Replace when the material becomes slimy or develops odor.

### Plastic Bottles and Found Containers

Scavenged bottles are the easiest improvised containers in most scenarios.

- Clear PET bottles (soda, water bottles) are food-safe and UV-resistant for SODIS treatment.
- Rinse any found container with purified water before use. Scrub the interior with sand and water.
- Avoid containers that previously held chemicals, fuel, or detergent. Residues persist and contaminate water.

### Tarp and Plastic Sheeting

Fold a tarp or sheet of plastic into a basin shape. Weigh down corners with rocks. Works for temporary collection and short-term storage, not transport.

Line a pit with plastic sheeting for larger-volume ground-level storage — up to 10–20 gallons (38–76 liters) depending on sheet size. Cover with a second sheet to prevent contamination.

## Long-Term Storage Treatment

### Bleach Dosing

Use regular, unscented household bleach containing 5.25–8.25% sodium hypochlorite.

| Water Volume | Bleach (5.25%) | Bleach (8.25%) | Wait Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 quart (1 L) | 2 drops | 2 drops | 30 min |
| 1 gallon (3.8 L) | 8 drops (1/8 tsp) | 6 drops | 30 min |
| 5 gallons (19 L) | 40 drops (1/2 tsp) | 30 drops (3/8 tsp) | 30 min |
| 55 gallons (208 L) | 1 tablespoon | 2.5 teaspoons | 30 min |

After 30 minutes, the water should have a faint chlorine smell. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 30 minutes.

!!! note
    Bleach loses potency over time. A bottle more than 1 year old may require double dosing. Bleach stored above 90°F (32°C) degrades faster.

### Re-Treatment Schedule

Chlorine dissipates from stored water. Re-treat every 48–72 hours in warm conditions, every 5–7 days in cool conditions. Check for chlorine smell before drinking — if absent, re-dose.

## Contamination Prevention

### Container Sanitation

1. Scrub interiors with sand, gravel, or a cloth soaked in dilute bleach solution (1 tablespoon per gallon / 15 mL per 3.8 L).
2. Rinse three times with purified water.
3. Air-dry inverted in sunlight for 2–4 hours. UV exposure reduces surface bacteria.

### Storage Practices

- Keep containers sealed or covered at all times. Use leaves, cloth, or lids.
- Store off the ground on a platform of sticks or rocks. Ground contact wicks heat and invites insects.
- Keep in shade. Direct sunlight promotes algae growth in translucent containers.
- Designate one container for scooping from the source, another for purified drinking water. Never mix.
- Handle drinking water containers with clean hands only. Use a pouring spout or ladle — do not dip hands in.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    A single dirty hand dipped into a clean water container contaminates the entire supply. Establish and enforce strict handling discipline, especially in group survival situations.

## Water Caching

### Cache Placement

For extended survival or planned routes, cache water at strategic points.

1. Place caches at intervals of 1 day's travel or less.
2. Choose shaded locations away from animal trails to reduce discovery and contamination.
3. Bury containers 6–12 inches (15–30 cm) below ground to insulate from temperature swings.
4. Mark caches with stacked rocks, cut branches, or flagging. Use at least two visible markers from the approach direction.
5. Record cache locations with compass bearings from at least two landmarks.

### Cache Containers

- Sealed plastic bottles or jerry cans are ideal.
- Double-bag in plastic to prevent groundwater seepage.
- Treat cached water with bleach at double the standard dose — it must remain safe for weeks.

!!! note
    Cached water freezes in winter. Bury below the frost line or insulate with dry leaves and soil. Frozen containers may crack — use flexible plastic, not rigid glass or thin-wall containers.

## Water Transport

### Carrying Over Distance

- A gallon of water weighs 8.3 pounds (3.8 kg). Plan loads accordingly.
- Use a shoulder yoke (a stick across the shoulders with containers hung from each end) to distribute weight evenly for distances over 1/4 mile (400 m).
- Lash multiple smaller containers to a pack frame rather than carrying one large, unstable container.
- Wrap containers in cloth or vegetation to insulate and reduce sloshing.

### Transport Vessels

| Container | Capacity | Weight (Full) | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|
| PET bottle (2 L) | 0.5 gal (2 L) | 4.4 lb (2 kg) | High |
| Bamboo section | 1–3 cups (0.25–0.7 L) | 1–2 lb (0.5–1 kg) | Medium |
| Animal stomach | 1–2 qt (1–2 L) | 3–5 lb (1.4–2.3 kg) | Low |
| Birch bark trough | 1–2 qt (1–2 L) | 3–5 lb (1.4–2.3 kg) | Low |
| Tarp bundle | 2–5 gal (7.5–19 L) | 17–42 lb (7.7–19 kg) | Medium |

## Ice and Snow Storage

In cold climates, frozen water is abundant but requires energy to convert to liquid.

- Pack clean snow into containers and melt near (not over) a fire. Direct heat scorches the container bottom before the snow melts, producing a burnt taste.
- Collect ice from frozen lakes or streams. Ice yields more water per volume than snow — roughly 3:1 advantage. One gallon of ice yields about 0.9 gallons (3.4 liters) of water. One gallon of snow yields about 0.3 gallons (1.1 liters).
- Store snowmelt as liquid, not as snow. Snow insulates itself and does not melt efficiently in bulk.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not eat snow or ice directly. Consuming frozen water drops core body temperature and accelerates hypothermia. Always melt first.

## Evaporation Prevention

Water loss through evaporation is significant in arid and hot environments.

- Cover all containers. An uncovered bucket loses 1–2 inches (2.5–5 cm) of depth per day in dry heat.
- Use narrow-mouth containers. Less surface area exposed to air means less evaporation.
- Store underground or in shade. Ground temperature 12 inches (30 cm) down is 10–20°F (6–11°C) cooler than surface temperature.
- Place a layer of clean leaves or a cloth on the water surface inside wide-mouth containers to reduce air contact.

## Common Mistakes

- **Storing unpurified water.** Bacteria multiply in standing water. Warm, stagnant water becomes more dangerous over time, not less. Purify before storing.
- **Using chemical containers for water.** Fuel cans, bleach jugs (unless thoroughly cleaned), and pesticide containers leach toxic residues that no field method removes.
- **Ignoring re-treatment.** Chlorine dissipates. Water treated with bleach 5 days ago may no longer be protected. Re-dose on schedule.
- **Caching without marking.** A buried water cache is useless if you cannot find it. Mark with multiple visible indicators and record compass bearings.
- **Underestimating volume needs.** One gallon per day is a minimum for temperate, low-activity conditions. Heat, exertion, illness, and altitude all increase demand. Plan for 50% more than the minimum.
- **Eating snow for hydration.** The energy cost of melting snow internally drains body heat. In cold conditions, this accelerates hypothermia. Melt snow externally first.

## Quick Reference

- **Minimum daily water:** 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person — double in heat or exertion
- **Bleach dose:** 8 drops per gallon (5.25% bleach), wait 30 minutes
- **Re-treat schedule:** Every 48–72 hours (warm), every 5–7 days (cool)
- **Container priority:** Sealed plastic > clay pot > bamboo > birch bark > animal organ
- **Ice vs. snow yield:** Ice produces 3x more water per volume than snow
- **Cache depth:** 6–12 inches (15–30 cm), below frost line in winter
- **Water weight:** 8.3 lb per gallon (1 kg per liter)
