# Desert and Sea Water Procurement

> Techniques for finding, extracting, and producing drinkable water in arid deserts and open ocean — the two environments where dehydration kills fastest.

## At a Glance

- In extreme heat, you lose 1–1.5 quarts (1–1.5 L) of water per hour through sweat. Replace it or die within 1–3 days.
- Solar stills, transpiration bags, and dew collection work in deserts but produce small volumes. Use all methods simultaneously.
- Seawater is lethal to drink untreated. Improvised distillation is the only reliable way to make it safe.
- Most cacti are not safe water sources. Only a few species yield drinkable liquid, and several cause vomiting that accelerates dehydration.
- Ration sweat, not water. Drink when you have water. Reduce activity and stay shaded during peak heat.

## Water Discipline in Extreme Heat

Water discipline determines whether you survive a desert or open-ocean scenario. The goal is to minimize loss, not restrict intake.

### The "Ration Sweat, Not Water" Rule

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not ration water by sipping small amounts. If you have water, drink it. Rationing water while dehydrated impairs judgment and accelerates heat illness. Ration your sweat instead — reduce physical activity.

- Rest during the hottest hours (1000–1600). Move at dawn, dusk, or night.
- Stay in shade. Shade reduces water loss by up to 50%.
- Keep clothing on. Loose, light-colored clothing slows evaporation and extends the cooling effect of sweat.
- Breathe through your nose. Mouth breathing loses more moisture.
- Avoid eating if water is scarce. Digestion requires water — protein-heavy foods require the most.

### Minimum Water Requirements

| Temperature | At Rest in Shade | Light Activity | Heavy Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90°F (32°C) | 1 gal / 3.8 L per day | 1.5 gal / 5.7 L | 2+ gal / 7.5+ L |
| 105°F (40°C) | 1.5 gal / 5.7 L per day | 2 gal / 7.5 L | 3+ gal / 11+ L |
| 120°F (49°C) | 2 gal / 7.5 L per day | 3 gal / 11 L | Survival unlikely |

!!! note
    These are minimum replacement rates to prevent decline. They do not account for recovery from an existing deficit.

## Signs of Dehydration Progression

Recognize dehydration stages early. Each stage narrows your window for self-rescue.

| Stage | Water Deficit | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 1–3% body weight | Thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, fatigue |
| Moderate | 4–6% body weight | Headache, dizziness, rapid pulse, no urine output |
| Severe | 7–10% body weight | Confusion, stumbling, vision problems, muscle spasms |
| Critical | 10%+ body weight | Delirium, seizures, unconsciousness, death |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    By the time you stop sweating in extreme heat, you are in a medical emergency. Seek shade immediately and drink any available water.

## Solar Stills

### Ground Solar Still

A ground still extracts moisture from soil and added plant material through evaporation and condensation.

1. Dig a cone-shaped hole 3 feet (1 m) wide and 2 feet (0.6 m) deep. Choose a spot with the most sun exposure.
2. Place a clean container at the center bottom of the hole.
3. If available, add a drinking tube running from the container out past the edge of the plastic — this lets you drink without dismantling the still.
4. Add moisture sources around the container: green vegetation, crushed cacti pads, urine-soaked cloth, or even saltwater.
5. Cover the hole with a clear plastic sheet (poncho, trash bag, or survival blanket works).
6. Seal all edges with soil or rocks. Make it airtight.
7. Place a fist-sized stone on the center of the plastic, directly above the container. The sheet should dip to a low point about 1 inch (2.5 cm) above the container rim.
8. Condensation forms on the underside of the plastic and drips into the container.

**Yield:** 0.5–1 quart (0.5–1 L) per day in moist soil with full sun. Yield drops sharply in dry sand without added moisture.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    A solar still in dry sand with no added vegetation or liquid yields almost nothing. The energy spent digging may cost more sweat than the still produces. Always add moisture sources.

!!! tip
    Build 3–5 stills to produce meaningful water. Spread them across the area to exploit different soil moisture levels.

### Inflatable Solar Still (Commercial)

Inflatable solar stills (such as the Aquamate or military-issue models) are designed for life rafts and sea survival.

1. Inflate the still and fill the reservoir with seawater.
2. Place the still in direct sunlight with the clear dome facing up.
3. Seawater evaporates inside the dome. Fresh water condenses on the inner surface and drains into a separate collection chamber.
4. Drain collected fresh water through the spigot into a container.

**Yield:** 1–2.5 quarts (1–2.4 L) per day depending on model and sunlight hours.

!!! note
    Inflatable solar stills require 30–60 minutes of sunlight before the first water appears. Performance drops significantly in overcast conditions.

## Transpiration Bags

Transpiration bags capture water vapor released by living plants through their leaves.

1. Select a sun-facing branch with abundant green leaves on a non-toxic tree or shrub.
2. Place a clear plastic bag over the leafy end of the branch.
3. Tie the bag opening tightly around the branch.
4. Position the bag so one corner hangs lower than the rest. Place a small stone in the low corner to create a collection point.
5. Leave the bag in place during peak sunlight hours.
6. Collect water in the late afternoon. Retie and repeat.

**Yield:** 0.25–1 cup (60–240 mL) per bag per day. Use 10–20 bags simultaneously.

=== "Desert Plants (Good)"
    - Mesquite — reliable producer in arid regions.
    - Desert willow — high transpiration rate.
    - Palo verde — good yield in Sonoran Desert.
    - Cottonwood — excellent where found near dry washes.

=== "Desert Plants (Avoid)"
    - Oleander — all parts toxic; water will be poisonous.
    - Desert milkweed — produces toxic sap.
    - Jimsonweed (Datura) — highly toxic; avoid contact.
    - Creosote bush — extremely low yield and unpleasant taste.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Transpiration water from toxic plants is toxic. Positively identify the plant before attaching a bag. When in doubt, do not use it.

## Dew Collection

Dew forms even in deserts when nighttime temperatures drop below the dew point. Desert dew is most common in coastal deserts and at higher elevations.

### Ground-Level Collection

1. Spread a tarp, plastic sheet, or space blanket on the ground before nightfall. Angle it slightly so water runs to one corner.
2. Place a container at the low point.
3. Collect at first light before the sun evaporates the dew.

### Vegetation Wipe Method

1. Wrap absorbent cloth around your shins and ankles.
2. Walk through any available vegetation before sunrise.
3. Wring the cloth into a container when saturated.
4. Repeat until dew evaporates — usually within 1–2 hours after sunrise.

**Yield:** A few ounces (50–200 mL) in arid deserts. Up to 1 quart (1 L) in coastal desert or humid conditions. Low effort, high value relative to energy cost.

!!! tip
    Metal surfaces collect dew efficiently. Leave vehicle hoods, aluminum foil, or metal containers exposed overnight and wipe them at dawn.

## Water from Cacti and Succulents

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Most cacti do not provide safe drinking water. Many contain oxalic acid, alkaloids, or other compounds that cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea — all of which accelerate fatal dehydration. Only use positively identified species.

### Safe or Marginally Safe Species

=== "Prickly Pear (Opuntia)"
    The safest and most widely available cactus water source. Found across North America, Central America, and the Mediterranean.

    - **Fruit (tunas):** Peel the reddish-purple fruit carefully. The pulp is edible and contains drinkable juice. Use a stick or cloth to handle — glochids (tiny hair-like spines) cause intense irritation.
    - **Pads (nopales):** Peel the outer skin. Chew the moist inner flesh for water and spit out the fiber. Mild flavor.

=== "Barrel Cactus (Ferocactus)"
    Last resort only. Found in the Sonoran, Chihuahuan, and Mojave deserts.

    - Cut the top off the cactus. Mash the white inner pulp into a paste.
    - Squeeze the paste through cloth to extract liquid.
    - The liquid is acidic and bitter. Drink in small quantities only — large amounts cause nausea and diarrhea.
    - **Never** eat the pulp itself. Only drink the extracted liquid.

=== "Fishhook Barrel (Ferocactus wislizeni)"
    Marginally better tasting than other barrel cacti. Same extraction method. Same caution about quantity.

### Toxic or Dangerous Species — Do Not Use

- **Saguaro** (*Carnegiea gigantea*) — contains alkaloids that cause vomiting.
- **Organ pipe cactus** (*Stenocereus thurberi*) — mildly toxic alkaloids.
- **Cholla** (*Cylindropuntia*) — very low moisture, extremely difficult to handle due to barbed spines.
- **Candelilla** (*Euphorbia antisyphilitica*) — caustic latex sap. Not a true cactus.
- **Euphorbia species** — many produce toxic milky sap. Avoid all milky-sapped succulents.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    If you cannot positively identify a cactus, do not eat or drink from it. The risk of vomiting and diarrhea in a dehydration scenario is lethal.

## Seawater Desalination

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never drink seawater. Seawater contains approximately 3.5% salt. Your kidneys require more water to flush the salt than the seawater provides, creating a net water loss that accelerates death.

### Improvised Distillation

Distillation is the only field-expedient method to make seawater safe. The principle: boil seawater, capture the steam, condense it, and collect the fresh water.

#### Pot-and-Tube Method

1. Fill a pot, can, or metal container with seawater.
2. Attach a tube (rubber hose, bamboo, hollowed kelp stem) to the lid or cover so steam is channeled into the tube.
3. Run the tube downhill or through a cooling medium (wet cloth wrapped around the tube, the tube submerged in cool seawater).
4. Place a collection container at the tube's open end.
5. Heat the seawater to a steady boil. Steam travels through the tube, condenses as it cools, and drips out as fresh water.

**Yield:** 1–2 cups (240–480 mL) per hour depending on heat source and tube cooling efficiency.

#### Two-Container Method (No Tube)

1. Place a small, empty container inside a larger pot of seawater.
2. The small container must sit above the water level — prop it on rocks or a wire stand.
3. Invert a lid or curved surface over the pot so the low point is directly above the small container.
4. Heat the seawater. Steam condenses on the inverted lid and drips into the small container.
5. Monitor constantly — do not let seawater boil into the collection container.

!!! tip
    At sea without fire, use sunlight. Place seawater in a dark container inside a clear plastic bag. The bag acts like a solar still — evaporated water condenses on the bag's inner surface. Tilt the bag so condensation runs to a collection point away from the saltwater.

### Condensation Collectors at Sea

On a life raft or boat without fuel, use passive methods.

- **Plastic sheet tent:** Drape clear plastic over a frame above a container of seawater. Solar heat evaporates the water. Condensation forms on the plastic and drains to a collection point.
- **Rain catchment:** In ocean survival, rain is your primary water source. Rig tarps, sails, or any fabric to funnel rain into containers. A single rainstorm can provide days of water.
- **Sponge and cloth collection:** At night, moisture condenses on cool surfaces. Wipe the exterior of the raft, tarp, or any smooth surface with a sponge or cloth at dawn. Wring into a container.

!!! note
    Sea fog and mist provide collectible moisture. Hang cloth or mesh vertically to intercept fog droplets. Wring collected moisture into a container.

## Rationing Strategies

### Desert Rationing

- **No water available:** Minimize all activity. Stay shaded. Do not eat. Move only at night if you must move at all. Your survival window is 1–3 days at 100°F (38°C) without water.
- **Limited water available:** Drink what you have. Do not sip — drink full amounts at intervals. Sipping does not hydrate effectively. Reduce activity to the minimum required for survival tasks.
- **Water sources available but limited yield:** Run all collection methods simultaneously (stills, transpiration bags, dew). Prioritize methods with the best effort-to-yield ratio.

### Sea Rationing

- **Day 1:** Do not drink at all if you are hydrated and not exerting. Your body has reserves.
- **Days 2–4:** Drink 14–18 ounces (400–500 mL) per day if available. This is well below replacement but slows decline.
- **Day 5 onward:** Drink whatever you can produce or collect. Survival beyond 7–10 days without water is unlikely at sea.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Never drink urine as a water substitute. Urine contains dissolved waste salts. As dehydration progresses, urine becomes increasingly concentrated and accelerates kidney failure. Use urine as a moisture source in a solar still only.

## Common Mistakes

- **Drinking seawater in desperation.** Seawater accelerates dehydration and causes kidney failure. There is no safe amount to drink untreated.
- **Sipping water to make it last.** Small sips do not hydrate. Drink full amounts and ration sweat through reduced activity instead.
- **Eating cactus pulp from unknown species.** Most cacti cause nausea and diarrhea. Stick to prickly pear fruit and pads unless you can positively identify another safe species.
- **Building one solar still and expecting enough water.** A single still produces a fraction of daily needs. Build 3–5 and supplement with other methods.
- **Working during peak heat to find water.** You lose more water sweating than most methods produce. Rest in shade from 1000 to 1600. Work at dawn, dusk, and night.
- **Ignoring dew collection.** Dew requires almost zero energy expenditure. Set up collection surfaces every night.
- **Removing clothing in the desert.** Exposed skin increases evaporative water loss. Keep loose clothing on.
- **Assuming clear seawater from distillation is safe.** Taste the distilled water. If it tastes salty, the collection container is contaminated. Clean it and restart.

## Quick Reference

| Method | Yield per Day | Effort | Best Environment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground solar still (x3–5) | 1.5–5 qt / 1.5–5 L total | Medium | Desert with moist soil |
| Inflatable solar still | 1–2.5 qt / 1–2.4 L | Low | Open ocean, sun |
| Transpiration bags (x10–20) | 0.5–2 qt / 0.5–2 L | Low | Desert with vegetation |
| Dew collection | 2–8 oz / 50–250 mL | Very low | Desert, cool nights |
| Prickly pear fruit/pads | Variable | Low | Arid North America |
| Improvised distillation | 1–2 qt / 1–2 L | High | Sea, with fire source |
| Rain catchment at sea | Highly variable | Low | Open ocean, rainy |
| Condensation wipe | 2–8 oz / 50–250 mL | Very low | Sea, cool nights |

**Critical numbers:**

- Minimum water at rest in shade at 90°F (32°C): 1 gallon (3.8 L) per day
- Survival without water at 120°F (49°C): roughly 2 days
- Survival without water at 90°F (32°C) with shade: roughly 5 days
- Seawater salt content: 3.5% — always lethal untreated
