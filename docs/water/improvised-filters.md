# Improvised Water Filters

> Remove sediment, particulates, and some pathogens from wild water using field-constructed filters when commercial options are unavailable.

## At a Glance

- Filtering removes visible debris and reduces turbidity — it does NOT make water safe to drink on its own.
- Always follow filtering with boiling, chemical treatment, or UV purification.
- Charcoal is the single most effective improvised filter medium; make it from hardwood.
- A well-built three-bucket gravity filter reduces turbidity by 80–95%.
- Rebuild or replace filter media every 50–70 gallons (190–265 liters) or when flow rate drops below a trickle.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Filtering is NOT purification. Improvised filters reduce sediment and some bacteria but do not reliably remove viruses, dissolved chemicals, or all pathogens. Always purify filtered water before drinking. See [Water Purification](purification.md).

## How Filtration Works

Filtration forces water through progressively finer media. Each layer traps particles by size. Coarse layers (gravel) catch large debris. Fine layers (sand, charcoal) trap smaller particulates and absorb some dissolved compounds.

Charcoal adsorbs organic chemicals, improves taste, and reduces some heavy metals. Sand provides mechanical filtration down to roughly 50 microns. Neither removes viruses (0.005–0.1 microns) or guarantees removal of bacteria.

## Charcoal Production for Filtering

Quality charcoal is the backbone of every improvised filter. Use hardwood — oak, maple, hickory, or birch. Softwoods (pine, spruce) produce resinous charcoal that imparts taste and filters poorly.

### Making Charcoal

1. Build a hot fire and let it burn down to glowing coals.
2. Remove coals with a stick or makeshift tongs and place them in a metal container or pit.
3. Smother the coals immediately — cover with a lid, sand, or dirt. Cut off all oxygen.
   **Do this outdoors.** Starving coals of oxygen is exactly the condition that produces
   carbon monoxide, which is odourless and kills without warning. Never smother coals
   inside a shelter, tent, vehicle, or snow cave.
4. Let the container cool completely, at least 3–4 hours.
5. Crush the charcoal into pea-sized pieces, roughly 1/4 inch (6 mm). Do not grind to powder — powder clogs filters and passes through.

!!! tip
    Charcoal from a campfire works if you pull coals before they become ash. Black, hard, and glassy chunks are ideal. Grey, crumbly material is ash — discard it.

### Quantity Needed

Prepare at least 2 quarts (1.9 liters) of crushed charcoal per filter. A three-bucket system needs 3–4 quarts (2.8–3.8 liters). Collect more than you think you need — replacement layers extend filter life.

## Three-Bucket Gravity Filter

This is the most effective improvised filter design. It processes water through three stages: coarse sediment removal, fine particulate filtration, and charcoal adsorption.

### Materials

- Three containers of 2–5 gallon (7.5–19 liter) capacity — buckets, large cans, hollowed logs, or bark containers
- Gravel: fist-sized down to marble-sized, roughly 4 inches (10 cm) depth
- Sand: coarse sand, not fine beach sand, roughly 6 inches (15 cm) depth
- Crushed charcoal: 4 inches (10 cm) depth
- Cloth or grass layers to separate media
- A way to punch small drainage holes in the bottom of the upper two containers

### Construction

1. Punch 5–8 small holes (1/8 inch / 3 mm) in the bottom of the top and middle containers.
2. Stack the three containers vertically, top to bottom. Secure with a tripod, forked branches, or stacked rocks.
3. **Top container (pre-filter):** Place a layer of grass or cloth over the holes. Add 4 inches (10 cm) of gravel, coarsest at the bottom, finer toward the top.
4. **Middle container (main filter):** Place a cloth layer over the holes. Add 2 inches (5 cm) of coarse sand. Add 4 inches (10 cm) of crushed charcoal. Add 4 inches (10 cm) of fine sand on top.
5. **Bottom container (collection):** Leave empty. This catches filtered water.

### Operation

1. Pour turbid water slowly into the top container. Do not flood it — add water at a rate the gravel can absorb.
2. Water drips through gravel, removing large sediment and debris.
3. Water drips into the middle container, passing through sand, charcoal, and more sand.
4. Collect filtered water from the bottom container.
5. Run the first 1–2 quarts (1–2 liters) back through the system. The initial output carries loose charcoal dust and fine particles.

### Expected Flow Rate

A properly built three-bucket filter produces 1–2 quarts (1–2 liters) per hour. If flow rate drops below a slow drip, the sand layer is clogged. Remove the top inch (2.5 cm) of sand, rinse it with filtered water, and replace.

## Single-Container Layered Filter

Use when only one container is available. Less effective than a three-bucket system but better than no filtration.

1. Punch 5–8 small holes in the bottom of the container.
2. Place a layer of cloth or green grass over the holes.
3. Add layers from bottom to top: 2 inches (5 cm) gravel, 2 inches (5 cm) charcoal, 3 inches (7.5 cm) sand, 1 inch (2.5 cm) gravel.
4. Top the filter with a cloth to prevent disturbing the sand when pouring water in.
5. Pour water slowly through. Discard the first quart (liter).

!!! note
    Single-container filters clog faster and filter less effectively. Expect roughly 1 quart (1 liter) per hour. Replace media after 20–30 gallons (75–115 liters).

## Tripod Filter

A quick-deploy filter requiring only cloth, a tripod of sticks, and natural filter media. Good for small quantities when constructing a bucket system is impractical.

### Construction

1. Lash three sticks, each 3–4 feet (1–1.2 m) long, into a tripod.
2. Tie three layers of cloth (bandanas, shirt fabric, sock material) at descending heights on the tripod.
3. Place grass or moss on the top cloth layer.
4. Place sand on the middle cloth layer.
5. Place crushed charcoal on the bottom cloth layer.
6. Set a collection container underneath.

### Operation

Pour water onto the top layer. It filters through grass, drips to sand, drips to charcoal, and collects below. This method is slow — roughly 1 pint (0.5 liters) per hour — and best suited for personal use, not group filtration.

## Bark Container Filter

In forested environments, birch bark or thick strips of tulip poplar bark form natural watertight containers suitable for filtering.

1. Peel a sheet of birch bark roughly 12 x 18 inches (30 x 45 cm). Avoid cutting into the cambium — this kills the tree.
2. Fold the bark into a cone or trough shape. Pin seams with wooden pegs or tie with cordage.
3. Punch small holes in the narrow end of the cone.
4. Layer sand and charcoal inside the cone, 2–3 inches (5–7.5 cm) of each.
5. Pour water into the wide end. Collect from the narrow end.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Bark containers are fragile and temporary. Handle gently. Replace after 10–15 gallons (38–57 liters) as the bark softens and seams loosen.

## Millbank Bag and Cloth Filtration

A tightly woven fabric bag — or any dense cotton cloth — acts as a coarse pre-filter. Military-issue Millbank bags reduce turbidity enough for chemical treatment to work effectively.

### Using Cloth as a Filter

1. Fold a cotton shirt or bandana into 4–8 layers.
2. Drape it over a container opening and secure with cordage or a belt.
3. Pour water through slowly. The cloth traps sediment, insects, and large particulates.
4. Wring out the cloth periodically to maintain flow.

Cloth filtration alone removes particles larger than about 20 microns. It does not remove bacteria, viruses, or dissolved contaminants. Use it as a pre-filter before sand/charcoal filtration or purification.

## Soil Filter (Ground Absorption)

Natural soil acts as a slow filter. Use this method when no containers are available.

1. Dig a hole 1–2 feet (0.3–0.6 m) from the edge of a turbid water source — a muddy pond, swamp, or silted stream.
2. Dig 1–2 feet (0.3–0.6 m) deep, below the water table.
3. Let the hole fill with water over 30–60 minutes.
4. The soil between the source and your hole acts as a natural filter, removing sediment and some bacteria.
5. Skim water from the top of the seep hole. Avoid disturbing the bottom.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Soil filtration improves clarity but provides minimal pathogen removal. Always purify seep water before drinking.

## Improvised Ceramic Filter

Clay-rich soil can be formed into a crude ceramic filter if you have the ability to fire it.

1. Collect fine clay soil. Remove rocks and organic matter.
2. Mix clay with sawdust, dried grass, or fine organic material at a 4:1 ratio (clay to organic).
3. Form into a pot or disk shape, 1/2 inch (1.3 cm) thick.
4. Let air-dry for 24–48 hours.
5. Fire in a hot campfire for 3–4 hours. The organic material burns out, leaving microscopic pores in the clay.
6. The resulting ceramic filters water slowly — roughly 1 quart (1 liter) per 2–3 hours —
   down to roughly 1–5 microns.

!!! warning "CAUTION: this catches protozoa, not bacteria"
    At 1–5 microns an improvised ceramic will hold back *Giardia* (8–12 µm) and
    *Cryptosporidium* (4–6 µm), which is genuinely useful — those are the two that
    chlorine and iodine struggle with. It does **not** hold back bacteria, which run
    0.1–10 microns and mostly pass straight through, and it does nothing at all to
    viruses at 0.005–0.1 microns. See the pathogen size table in
    [Water Purification](purification.md#understanding-the-threats).

    A commercial ceramic element is rated 0.2–0.5 microns — roughly ten times finer than
    anything a campfire firing will produce, and the reason commercial ones can claim
    bacteria removal while this cannot.

!!! note
    Improvised ceramics are fragile and inconsistent. Cracks and uneven firing create passages that bypass filtration. Inspect carefully before relying on this method. Always purify the output.

## Filter Maintenance

### When to Rebuild

- Flow rate drops below half of its original output.
- Filtered water remains visibly cloudy after passing through.
- Filter media has processed more than 50–70 gallons (190–265 liters).
- Charcoal layer develops an odor or the water tastes off.

### Maintenance Steps

1. Remove and discard the top inch (2.5 cm) of sand — this layer traps the most sediment.
2. Replace with fresh sand.
3. If charcoal is spent (grey, powdery, or no longer improves taste), replace the entire charcoal layer.
4. Rinse gravel in clean water and reuse.
5. Flush the rebuilt filter with 1–2 quarts (1–2 liters) before collecting water for use.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating filtered water as safe to drink.** Filtration reduces turbidity and some pathogens but does not eliminate viruses or all bacteria. Always purify after filtering.
- **Using ash instead of charcoal.** Ash is powdery calcium carbonate — it dissolves in water and raises alkalinity. Charcoal is carbon that adsorbs contaminants. They are not the same.
- **Grinding charcoal too fine.** Powder clogs sand layers and passes into collected water. Crush to pea-sized pieces, 1/4 inch (6 mm).
- **Pouring water too fast.** Flooding the filter pushes unfiltered water through channels in the media. Pour slowly and steadily.
- **Never replacing filter media.** All filter media has a finite capacity. Sand and charcoal saturate with contaminants. Rebuild on schedule.
- **Skipping the pre-filter step.** Running muddy water directly through a fine filter clogs it within minutes. Always use gravel or cloth as a first stage.
- **Trusting an improvised ceramic against bacteria.** At 1–5 microns it catches protozoa and passes bacteria. Purify the output regardless.
- **Smothering charcoal coals under cover.** Oxygen-starved coals produce carbon monoxide. Make charcoal outdoors, never in a shelter.

## Quick Reference

| Filter Type | Flow Rate | Turbidity Reduction | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three-bucket gravity | 1–2 qt/hr (1–2 L/hr) | 80–95% | Group use, base camp |
| Single-container | 1 qt/hr (1 L/hr) | 60–80% | Solo, short-term |
| Tripod (cloth layers) | 0.5 pt/hr (0.25 L/hr) | 50–70% | Quick personal use |
| Bark container | 0.5 qt/hr (0.5 L/hr) | 60–75% | Forest environments |
| Cloth/Millbank bag | 2–4 qt/hr (2–4 L/hr) | 30–50% | Pre-filter only |
| Soil seep | Variable | 40–60% | No-container situations |
| Improvised ceramic | 1 qt/2–3 hrs | 85–95% | Long-term camp with fire |

**Filter media order (bottom to top):** cloth > gravel > charcoal > sand > gravel > cloth

**Always purify after filtering:** boil, chemically treat, or UV treat.
