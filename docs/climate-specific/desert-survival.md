# Desert Survival

> How to survive in arid environments where heat, dehydration, and exposure are the primary killers.

## At a Glance

- Dehydration kills in 2-3 days without water. In desert heat, you may have less than 24 hours.
- Stay in shade during daylight. Move at night, dawn, or dusk when temperatures drop 30-50°F (17-28°C).
- Keep clothing on. Loose, light-covering clothing blocks solar radiation and holds sweat against the skin where it actually cools you instead of dripping away. You sweat less for the same cooling, so your water lasts longer.
- Stay with your vehicle if stranded. A vehicle is visible from the air, provides shade, and contains salvageable materials.
- Flash floods kill more people in deserts than dehydration. Never camp in dry washes.

## Heat Management

### The Shade Rule

Direct sun exposure in desert environments can produce ground temperatures of 150-170°F (65-77°C) even when air temperature is 110°F (43°C). Seek or create shade during peak hours (10 AM - 4 PM).

- Dig a shallow trench and stretch a tarp, poncho, or space blanket 12 inches (30 cm) above ground level. The air gap is critical — material resting on the ground transfers heat.
- Double-layer shade reduces temperature by an additional 10-15°F (6-8°C). Use a second layer 12 inches (30 cm) above the first.
- Sit on an insulating layer. Ground contact burns exposed skin and transfers heat through clothing.

### Timing Movement

| Time | Temperature | Action |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 10 AM - 4 PM | Peak heat | Rest in shade. Do not move. |
| 4 PM - 10 PM | Cooling | Travel if navigating by landmarks |
| 10 PM - 4 AM | Coolest | Optimal travel time |
| 4 AM - 10 AM | Warming | Travel, then seek shade |

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Night travel requires reliable navigation. Featureless terrain looks identical in every direction under starlight. Carry a compass and take bearings before dark.

## Water Discipline

- Ration sweat, not water. Drink when thirsty. Restricting water intake does not conserve it — it just accelerates dehydration.
- A resting person in shade needs 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per day minimum at 100°F (38°C). Walking in heat requires 1-2 gallons (3.8-7.6 liters) per day.
- Signs of dehydration: dark urine, headache, dizziness, rapid heartbeat, confusion. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already 1-2% dehydrated.
- Never drink urine, blood, or seawater. All accelerate dehydration.

### Finding Water

- Dry riverbeds: dig at the outside bend of dry washes, 1-3 ft (0.3-1 m) down. Water collects at the lowest point of bends.
- Vegetation lines: green vegetation in a dry landscape indicates subsurface water. Dig nearby.
- Animal trails converging on a single point often lead to water.
- Collect dew at dawn using a cloth dragged across vegetation. Wring into a container.
- Solar still: dig a pit 3 ft (1 m) wide, 2 ft (60 cm) deep. Place a container in the center. Cover with clear plastic, seal edges with soil, place a small stone over the container. Yields 0.5-1 quart (0.5-1 L) per day in moist soil with full sun, and close to nothing in dry sand without added vegetation.

!!! note
    Solar stills produce minimal water relative to the energy spent building them. Use them as a supplement, not a primary source.

## Shelter from Sun

=== "Natural Shelter"
    Rock overhangs, caves, and the shaded side of large boulders provide immediate relief. Check for venomous occupants (snakes, scorpions, spiders) before entering. North-facing rock faces (Southern Hemisphere: south-facing) stay shaded longest.

=== "Improvised Shelter"
    Trench shelter: dig a body-length trench 18 inches (45 cm) deep. Cover with a tarp or poncho elevated 12 inches (30 cm) above ground. Pile excavated sand on top for additional insulation. Enter the trench during peak heat.

=== "Vehicle Shelter"
    Open all doors and windows for airflow. Create shade on the sun-facing side with a tarp or blanket draped from the roof. Do not sit inside a sealed vehicle — interior temperatures exceed 140°F (60°C). Stay near the vehicle but in improvised shade.

## Navigation in Featureless Terrain

- Distant landmarks shimmer and distort in heat haze. Take compass bearings, not visual estimates.
- Use back-bearings: look behind you regularly and note landmarks you have passed. If you need to return, the terrain looks completely different from the opposite direction.
- Sand dunes migrate. Do not use dune shapes as fixed landmarks.
- Walk toward large, immovable features: mountain ranges, mesas, rock formations.
- Stars are reliable in clear desert skies. Identify Polaris (Northern Hemisphere) or the Southern Cross before dark.

## Flash Flood Awareness

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Flash floods arrive with zero warning and carry devastating force. A wall of water 6 ft (1.8 m) high can travel at 30 mph (48 km/h) through a narrow canyon.

- Never camp in a dry wash, arroyo, or canyon bottom.
- If rain is visible anywhere on the horizon — even 20 miles (32 km) away — move to high ground immediately.
- Watch for rising water, increasing debris in water, or a distant roar.
- Flash flood risk persists for hours after rain stops upstream.

## Venomous Creatures

### Snakes

- Rattlesnakes, sidewinders, and vipers inhabit most deserts worldwide. Most active at dawn, dusk, and night.
- Watch where you step and reach. Use a stick to probe ahead.
- Shake out boots, clothing, and sleeping bags before use.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Bee, wasp, and scorpion stings can trigger anaphylaxis in a sensitized person,
    which kills faster than the venom itself and is not related to how much venom was
    injected. Watch for spreading hives, throat or facial swelling, wheezing, or
    collapse. Epinephrine is the only first-line treatment — see
    [Bites and Stings](../medical/bites-and-stings.md#anaphylaxis).

### Scorpions

- All scorpions sting. Most are painful but not lethal. The bark scorpion (Centruroides sculpturatus) of the southwestern US can be fatal to children and the elderly.
- Scorpions are nocturnal. They fluoresce under UV light — a UV flashlight reveals them at night.
- Shake out all clothing and footwear. Check under rocks and logs before sitting.

### Spiders

- Black widows and brown recluses inhabit desert regions. Both hide in dark, sheltered areas — rock crevices, woodpiles, abandoned structures.
- Wear gloves when moving debris or collecting firewood.

!!! tip
    Sleep on a raised surface or cot when possible. Elevating your body 6 inches (15 cm) above ground significantly reduces encounters with ground-dwelling venomous creatures.

## Night Travel

- Begin walking at dusk. Stop before dawn when temperatures begin rising.
- Use stars for navigation. Check bearings every 15-30 minutes.
- Walk slowly and deliberately. Ankle injuries on unseen terrain are common and potentially fatal in the desert.
- Carry a flashlight or headlamp but use it sparingly — it destroys night vision for 20-30 minutes.
- Mark your route with cairns (stacked rocks) so you can retrace if needed.

## Vehicle Breakdown Protocol

1. Stay with the vehicle. Searchers find vehicles, not footprints.
2. Deploy all visual signals: hood up, mirrors angled to flash, bright materials on the roof.
3. Inventory water, food, and salvageable materials (mirrors, wiring, seat cushions for insulation, floor mats for shade material).
4. Create shade beside the vehicle using doors, tarps, or blankets.
5. If you must leave the vehicle, leave a note with your direction of travel, time of departure, and number in your party.
6. Conserve battery for emergency signals at night (headlights visible for miles in flat desert).

## Sand and Dust Storms

- Storms arrive quickly. Watch for a dark wall on the horizon.
- Turn your back to the wind. Cover your nose and mouth with a cloth.
- Protect your eyes. Sand abrasion damages corneas and causes temporary blindness.
- If driving, pull over, turn off headlights (other drivers may follow your lights into danger), and wait.
- Mark your position before the storm. Landmarks disappear in zero visibility.

## Temperature Extremes

Desert temperatures can swing 50-60°F (28-33°C) between day and night. A 110°F (43°C) afternoon becomes a 50°F (10°C) night.

- Carry insulation for nighttime. Hypothermia is a real risk in the desert.
- Fire provides warmth, light, and signaling at night. Gather fuel during daylight.
- Dry desert air accelerates heat loss through evaporation. Block wind exposure at night.

## Signaling in Open Terrain

- Flat, open terrain makes ground-to-air signals visible from extreme distances.
- Build signal fires with added rubber, oil, or green vegetation for black smoke (visible against light sand). Rubber and plastic give off genuinely toxic fumes — dioxins and hydrogen chloride among them. Light it, then get upwind and stay out of the plume; do not stand over it feeding the fire.
- Lay out contrasting materials in geometric patterns: **V** (require assistance) or **X** (require medical assistance). See [Signaling for Rescue](../navigation/signaling-for-rescue.md) for the full ICAO set.
- A signal mirror is effective to roughly 10 miles (16 km) in ordinary conditions, and considerably further — 50 miles (80 km) or more — with a proper aimed mirror in clear air. Desert air is unusually clear, so expect the upper end. Aim at aircraft or distant high points.
- Three of anything (fires, rock piles, whistle blasts) is the universal distress signal.

## Common Mistakes

- **Rationing water.** Drink when thirsty. Your body cannot store water by drinking less — you just dehydrate faster.
- **Removing clothing in heat.** Bare skin absorbs far more solar radiation, and sweat runs off rather than evaporating where it cools you. Loose full coverage keeps you cooler and costs less water.
- **Walking during peak heat.** Rest in shade from 10 AM to 4 PM. Movement in peak heat doubles water consumption.
- **Camping in dry washes.** Flash floods arrive without warning and carry lethal force.
- **Leaving a broken-down vehicle.** The vehicle is your best shelter, signal, and supply cache. Stay with it.
- **Assuming desert nights are warm.** Temperature drops of 50°F (28°C) cause hypothermia if you have no insulation.
- **Ignoring venomous creatures.** Always shake out boots and check under rocks. Scorpions and snakes are most active when you are most active — at night.

|----------|--------|
