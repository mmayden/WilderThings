# Arctic and Subarctic Survival

> How to survive in extreme cold environments where hypothermia, frostbite, and whiteout conditions are constant threats.

## At a Glance

- Cold kills faster than hunger or thirst. Shelter and insulation are your first priorities.
- Layer clothing in three systems: base (moisture-wicking), insulation (warmth), shell (wind/water).
- Never eat snow directly. Melt it first — eating snow drops core temperature and costs more energy than it provides.
- Wind chill accelerates heat loss exponentially. A 15 mph (24 km/h) wind at 0°F (-18°C) feels like -19°F (-28°C).
- In polar regions, daylight ranges from 24 hours of darkness to 24 hours of light depending on season. Plan all movement around available light.

## Cold Injury Prevention

### Hypothermia Stages

| Stage | Core Temp | Symptoms |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| Mild | 95-90°F (35-32°C) | Shivering, confusion, poor coordination |
| Moderate | 90-82°F (32-28°C) | Violent shivering stops, slurred speech, irrational behavior |
| Severe | Below 82°F (28°C) | Unconsciousness, weak pulse, cardiac arrest risk |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Paradoxical undressing occurs in moderate-to-severe hypothermia. The victim feels burning hot and removes clothing. If you witness this, the person is in critical danger.

!!! danger "DANGER: a moderately hypothermic person can be killed by rough handling"
    Below about 90°F (32°C) the heart becomes extremely irritable. Jolting, dragging,
    or standing the person up can trigger a fatal arrhythmia. This is a real way that
    rescuers kill people they are trying to save.

    Handle them **gently and horizontally**. Do not let them walk, sit up, or "help".
    Cut wet clothing off rather than pulling it. Rewarm the core, not the limbs —
    warming arms and legs first drives cold blood inward and drops core temperature
    further (afterdrop). Full protocol in
    [Hypothermia and Heatstroke](../medical/hypothermia-and-heatstroke.md).

### If You Fall Through Ice

Immersion is the fastest killer in this environment, and the first minute decides it.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Hitting cold water triggers an involuntary gasp and hyperventilation — **cold shock**.
    If your head is under when it happens you inhale water. Most people who die in cold
    water drown in the first minute, long before hypothermia is a factor.

1. **Do not thrash. Control your breathing first.** The gasping passes in about a minute.
   You cannot do anything useful until it does.
2. **Turn back the way you came.** That ice held your weight seconds ago; ice ahead of
   you has not been tested.
3. **Get horizontal.** Put both arms on the ice, kick your legs up behind you, and swim
   yourself onto the surface like a seal hauling out. Trying to climb out vertically
   just breaks more edge.
4. **Roll away — do not stand.** Keep your weight spread until you are well clear.
5. Then treat as an immediate hypothermia emergency: shelter, dry insulation, fire.

You have roughly 10 minutes of useful hand and arm function before grip fails. Spend
it getting out, not on anything else.

### Trench Foot (Immersion Foot)

Trench foot is a cold-wet injury, not a tropical one — it takes its name from the
trenches of the First World War. Feet that stay wet at temperatures anywhere from
freezing to about 60°F (15°C) develop nerve and tissue damage in **24-48 hours**, and
the damage can be permanent.

- Dry your feet and change socks at every opportunity, even when it feels like a waste
  of time. It is the highest-value thing you will do for yourself all day.
- Dry damp socks against your body overnight.
- Early signs: numbness, tingling, blotchy white or red skin, swelling.
- Unlike frostbite the tissue never freezes, so there is no obvious moment of injury —
  it just quietly happens.

### Frostbite Prevention

- Exposed skin freezes in under 10 minutes at -20°F (-29°C) with wind.
- Protect extremities first: fingers, toes, ears, nose, cheeks.
- Keep hands dry. Wet gloves accelerate frostbite.
- Wiggle fingers and toes regularly. Numbness is the first warning sign.
- Never rub frostbitten skin. Rewarm in water at 99-102°F (37-39°C) if refreezing can be prevented.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not rewarm frostbitten tissue if there is any chance of refreezing. Thaw-refreeze cycles cause far worse tissue damage than sustained freezing.

## The Layering System

=== "Base Layer"
    Wear moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool directly against skin. Cotton kills — it absorbs sweat, loses all insulation value, and accelerates heat loss. Remove or vent layers before you sweat.

=== "Insulation Layer"
    Fleece, down, or synthetic fill. Down provides the best warmth-to-weight ratio but is useless when wet. Synthetic insulation retains warmth when damp. Wear one or two insulation layers depending on activity level.

=== "Shell Layer"
    Windproof and waterproof outer layer. A hard shell blocks wind and precipitation. Pit zips and front ventilation prevent moisture buildup during exertion. Seal all openings — wrists, neck, waist — against wind entry.

!!! tip
    Regulate temperature by venting before you sweat. Open zippers, push back hoods, remove hats. Sweat trapped in clothing freezes when you stop moving.

## Shelter in Snow and Ice

Build shelter immediately when conditions deteriorate. Priorities:

1. Get out of the wind. Even a snow wall reduces wind chill dramatically.
2. Insulate from the ground. Snow itself is a good insulator — that is why snow shelters work — but lying directly on it melts the contact layer, and meltwater soaks your clothing and drains heat fast. Put something between you and the snow.
3. Keep the shelter small. Body heat warms a small space faster.

### Shelter Options

- **Snow trench:** Fastest option. Dig a body-width trench, cover with blocks or a tarp. 30-60 minutes.
- **Quinzhee:** Pile snow, let it sinter 2 hours, hollow it out. Works with any snow type. 3-5 hours.
- **Snow cave:** Requires an existing drift 8+ ft (2.5+ m) deep. 2-4 hours.
- **Wind wall:** Stack snow blocks in a C-shape upwind. Immediate wind reduction for a temporary stop.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Always maintain two ventilation holes in any enclosed snow shelter. Carbon dioxide buildup kills silently.

## Fire in Extreme Cold

- Carry multiple ignition sources in waterproof containers. Butane lighters begin failing near freezing (butane stops vaporizing around 32°F / 0°C) and are useless in real cold — keep one in an inner pocket warmed by body heat, and carry a ferrocerium rod, which works at any temperature.
- Use a platform of green logs or rocks under the fire. Fire built directly on snow melts through and extinguishes.
- Birch bark ignites even when frozen. Collect it as tinder whenever you see it.
- Split wood exposes dry interior — frozen exterior wood resists ignition. Split small.
- Build a reflector wall of stacked logs behind the fire to direct heat toward your shelter.

## Water from Snow and Ice

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Eating snow lowers core body temperature and costs more energy to warm than it provides in hydration. Always melt snow first.

- **Old sea ice** (blue-gray, rounded edges) is nearly salt-free and safe to melt for drinking. New sea ice (milky, sharp edges) retains salt.
- Melt snow in a pot over fire or stove. Pack snow loosely — dense packing scorches the pot and wastes fuel.
- Add a small amount of water to the pot first to prevent scorching.
- Dark-colored containers placed in sunlight melt snow passively.
- You need approximately 10 quarts (9.5 liters) of snow to produce 1 quart (1 liter) of water.

## Navigation in Whiteout

Whiteout conditions eliminate all visual references. Depth perception fails completely.

- Stop moving if you cannot see 30 ft (10 m) ahead. Build shelter and wait.
- If you must move, use a compass bearing. Do not trust your sense of direction.
- Travel roped together in groups. Space members 30 ft (10 m) apart.
- Probe ahead with a ski pole or stick for crevasses, drop-offs, and thin ice.
- Wind direction stays relatively consistent during a storm. Use it as a backup reference if your compass is lost.

## Arctic Food Sources

- **Fishing through ice:** Cut a hole 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) wide. Use improvised hooks, snares, or gill nets. Fish remain active under ice year-round.
- **Trapping:** Snare rabbits and ptarmigan along game trails. Look for tracks, droppings, and browse marks.
- **Lichens:** Rock tripe (Umbilicaria) is edible when boiled — raw lichen causes nausea. Reindeer lichen (Cladonia) is edible after soaking and boiling.
- **Seals and seabirds:** Available in coastal arctic areas. Seal breathing holes on sea ice indicate hunting opportunities.

!!! note
    Caloric needs increase 25-50% in extreme cold. Your body burns significant energy maintaining core temperature. Eat frequently and prioritize fats — fat provides 9 calories per gram versus 4 for protein or carbohydrates.

## Signaling in Snow

- Stamp out large ground signals in snow. Letters should be 10 ft (3 m) tall minimum. Use SOS or a large X.
- Contrast is critical. Lay dark branches, clothing, or gear on white snow.
- Fire and smoke are visible for miles against a white background. Add green boughs for smoke.
- Signal mirrors work exceptionally well on clear arctic days — snow reflects ambient light and increases mirror flash visibility.

## Wind Chill Management

| Air Temp | 10 mph (16 km/h) | 20 mph (32 km/h) | 30 mph (48 km/h) |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 20°F (-7°C) | 9°F (-13°C) | 4°F (-16°C) | 1°F (-17°C) |
| 0°F (-18°C) | -16°F (-27°C) | -22°F (-30°C) | -26°F (-32°C) |
| -20°F (-29°C) | -41°F (-41°C) | -48°F (-44°C) | -53°F (-47°C) |

What the numbers mean for exposed skin (National Weather Service):

| Wind chill | Frostbite in |
|---|---|
| -18°F (-28°C) | 30 minutes |
| -32°F (-36°C) | 10 minutes |
| -48°F (-44°C) | 5 minutes |

At -20°F with a 30 mph wind, exposed skin freezes in about five minutes. Reduce wind exposure at every opportunity. Travel in valleys, behind ridges, and in tree cover when available.

## Polar Bear Awareness

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Polar bears are apex predators that actively hunt humans. They do not bluff charge. Any approach is predatory.

- Travel in groups. Make noise. Polar bears avoid groups of three or more.
- Store food at least 300 ft (90 m) from camp in a bear cache or sealed container.
- Carry bear spray and keep it accessible (not in a buried pack pocket). Effective at ranges under 25 ft (7.5 m).
- Bear bangers and flares may deter bears at longer ranges.
- If charged, do not run. Stand your ground and deploy bear spray. Fight back if contact occurs — target the nose and eyes.

## Daylight Extremes

- **Polar night:** Above 66°N/S latitude, winter brings weeks of continuous darkness. Carry reliable headlamps with lithium batteries (alkaline batteries fail in extreme cold).
- **Midnight sun:** Summer brings 24 hours of light. Set alarms for rest — sleep deprivation impairs judgment as much as cold.
- Schedule travel during the warmest part of the day (typically 10 AM - 4 PM) when possible. Rest during the coldest hours.

## Common Mistakes

- **Wearing cotton.** Cotton absorbs moisture, loses insulation, and freezes. Wear wool or synthetics only.
- **Eating snow without melting it.** Drops core temperature and provides minimal hydration.
- **Sweating into clothing.** Vent layers before exertion. Sweat freezes when activity stops.
- **Ignoring early frostbite signs.** Numbness, white waxy skin, and tingling demand immediate action. Rewarm the area with body heat.
- **Traveling in whiteout conditions.** Getting lost in featureless terrain is nearly certain. Stop, shelter, wait.
- **Skipping food to conserve supplies.** Your body burns massive calories in cold. Eat regularly or your body cannibalizes muscle for heat.
- **Building fire directly on snow.** The fire melts through and extinguishes. Use a platform of green logs.

## Quick Reference

| Priority | Action |
|----------|--------|
| Immediate | Get out of wind, insulate from ground |
| Shelter | Snow trench (30-60 min), quinzhee (3-5 hr), snow cave (2-4 hr) |
| Water | Melt snow — never eat it raw. 10:1 snow-to-water ratio |
| Fire | Platform under fire, keep lighter warm, split frozen wood |
| Clothing | Base (wicking) + insulation + shell. No cotton. Vent before sweating |
| Frostbite | Rewarm at 99-102°F (37-39°C). Never rub. Never refreeze |
| Navigation | Stop in whiteout. Use compass bearings. Rope teams in groups |
| Signaling | Dark materials on white snow. Fire and smoke. Signal mirror |
| Calories | Increase intake 25-50%. Prioritize fats |
