# Mountain Survival

> How to survive in mountainous terrain where altitude, weather, and steep ground create life-threatening hazards.

## At a Glance

- Altitude sickness can kill. Ascend no more than 1,000-1,500 ft (300-450 m) per day above 8,000 ft (2,400 m). Descend immediately if symptoms worsen.
- Mountain weather changes in minutes. Clear skies become violent storms with zero warning. Watch clouds constantly.
- Lightning kills on ridges and summits. Descend below treeline when thunderstorms approach — there is no safe position to adopt outdoors, only safer ground.
- The only cure for serious altitude illness is descent. No drug, rest period, or acclimatization trick substitutes for going lower.
- UV radiation increases 4-5% per 1,000 ft (300 m) of elevation. Sunburn and snow blindness are serious threats above treeline.

## Altitude Sickness

### Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)

Onset: 6-24 hours after arriving above 8,000 ft (2,400 m).

- **Symptoms:** Headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, poor appetite, disturbed sleep.
- **Response:** Stop ascending. Rest at current altitude for 24-48 hours. Hydrate aggressively — 3-4 quarts (3-4 liters) per day. If symptoms do not improve in 24 hours, descend 1,000-3,000 ft (300-900 m).

### High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)

Swelling of the brain. Fatal without treatment.

- **Symptoms:** Severe headache unresponsive to pain medication, confusion, loss of coordination (ataxia), hallucinations, altered consciousness.
- **Test:** Have the person walk heel-to-toe in a straight line. If they cannot maintain balance, suspect HACE.
- **Response:** Descend immediately. Every hour of delay increases mortality. Administer dexamethasone (8 mg initially, then 4 mg every 6 hours) if available.

### High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)

Fluid in the lungs. The most common cause of altitude-related death.

- **Symptoms:** Breathlessness at rest, persistent dry cough progressing to wet cough with pink frothy sputum, extreme fatigue, gurgling or crackling sounds when breathing.
- **Response:** Descend immediately — at least 1,000-3,000 ft (300-900 m). Keep the patient upright. Administer supplemental oxygen if available. Nifedipine (30 mg extended-release) can be used as an adjunct.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    HACE and HAPE can develop simultaneously. Both are fatal without descent. Do not wait for morning, better weather, or group consensus. Descend now.

## Acclimatization

- Ascend no more than 1,000-1,500 ft (300-450 m) of sleeping elevation per day above 8,000 ft (2,400 m).
- "Climb high, sleep low." Hike to a higher point during the day but return to a lower camp to sleep.
- Take a rest day (no altitude gain) every 3,000-4,000 ft (900-1,200 m) of cumulative ascent.
- Stay hydrated. Dehydration worsens altitude sickness. Urine should be clear to light yellow.
- Avoid alcohol and sleeping pills during acclimatization. Both suppress respiratory drive at altitude.

!!! tip
    Acetazolamide (Diamox, 125-250 mg twice daily) accelerates acclimatization. Start 24 hours before ascent. It is a prescription medication — obtain it before your trip.

## Mountain Weather

### Reading the Sky

| Cloud Type | Meaning | Action |
|------------|---------|--------|
| Lenticular (lens-shaped over peaks) | High winds at altitude, storm approaching | Do not ascend exposed ridges |
| Towering cumulus | Thunderstorm developing | Descend below treeline within 30 minutes |
| Lowering ceiling (clouds dropping) | Precipitation imminent | Seek shelter, prepare for reduced visibility |
| Banner clouds (streaming from summit) | Extreme wind at peak | Stay below the summit. Wind speeds may exceed 80 mph (130 km/h) |

### Speed of Change

Mountain weather shifts faster than any other environment. A clear morning can become a whiteout blizzard by noon.

- Check weather before departure and plan turnaround times.
- Storms typically build in the afternoon. Start early, summit by midday, descend before afternoon weather.
- Temperature drops approximately 3.5°F per 1,000 ft (6.5°C per 1,000 m) of elevation gain. A pleasant 70°F (21°C) valley becomes 35°F (2°C) at 10,000 ft (3,000 m) higher.

## Lightning Safety

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Lightning strikes peaks, ridges, and exposed high points first. If you can hear thunder, you are within strike range.

### The 30-30 Rule

- If the time between lightning flash and thunder is 30 seconds or less, the storm is within 6 miles (10 km). Seek shelter immediately.
- Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before leaving shelter.

### If Caught in the Open

!!! danger "The 'lightning crouch' does not work — do not rely on it"

    The National Weather Service **withdrew the lightning crouch in 2008**. It
    provides no meaningful protection: if the lightning channel comes down on
    you, your posture does not change the outcome.

    Worse, teaching it causes harm. People who believe they have a protective
    position delay seeking real shelter. **There is no safe place outdoors in a
    thunderstorm.** Keep moving toward genuine shelter rather than stopping to
    assume a position.

1. **Descend immediately** from ridges, peaks, and exposed high points. Losing elevation is the single most useful thing you can do.
2. Head for a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle if either is reachable. Nothing outdoors is equivalent.
3. Get below treeline. Dense forest of *uniform* height is the best of the bad outdoor options — you are not the tallest object.
4. Avoid tall isolated objects — lone trees, poles, metal structures — and avoid open ground where you are the high point.
5. **Avoid shallow caves, overhangs, and rock crevices.** These do not shelter you from lightning; ground current arcs across the gap and they are a common site of injury.
6. Spread group members 50-100 ft (15-30 m) apart so one strike cannot injure everyone.
7. Discard metal-frame packs and trekking poles and move away from them.
8. Keep moving toward safety. Do not stop and wait it out in the open.

## Rockfall and Avalanche Awareness

### Rockfall

- Most frequent in early morning when overnight frost loosens rock, and in afternoon when solar warming melts ice that holds rock in place.
- **Minimize time spent under the hazard, not distance from it.** Move straight up or straight down through a rockfall zone — the shortest path out. Never traverse horizontally beneath one, which keeps you exposed for far longer.
- Wear a helmet in terrain with overhead rockfall hazard.
- Shout "ROCK!" loudly if you dislodge a stone. Do not look up — protect your head and press against the slope.

### Avalanche

- Avalanche risk is highest on slopes between 25-45 degrees with recent snow loading.
- Convex slopes are more dangerous than concave slopes.
- Avoid slopes with recent wind-deposited snow (pillowed, chalky appearance).
- Cross avalanche terrain one person at a time. The rest of the group watches from a safe zone.
- Carry an avalanche beacon, probe, and shovel in winter mountain terrain. Practice using them before you need them.

!!! note
    A buried avalanche victim has a 90% survival rate if found within 15 minutes. After 30 minutes, survival drops to 30%. Speed of rescue is everything.

## Route Finding on Steep Terrain

- Follow natural ramps: ridgelines, benches (flat steps on a slope), and diagonal weakness in cliff bands.
- Ascend steep grass and scree slopes by zigzagging. Direct ascent wastes energy and loosens rock for anyone below.
- Test every handhold and foothold before committing weight. The phrase is "test before you trust."
- Three points of contact at all times on exposed terrain — two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand.
- Use trekking poles on moderate terrain. Stow them when you need hands for scrambling.

## Water Sources at Altitude

- Snowmelt streams are abundant in spring and summer. Water is typically clean near its source but should still be treated.
- Above treeline, water may be scarce on ridges and summits. Carry at least 2 quarts (2 liters) above your last reliable water source.
- Glacial melt contains rock flour (fine glacial sediment) that gives it a milky appearance. It is safe to drink after settling or filtering but may cause digestive discomfort in quantity.
- Snow can be melted for water. See [Finding Water](../water/finding-water.md) for efficient snow-melting techniques.

## Hypothermia Risk

Mountain hypothermia occurs year-round, not just in winter. Wet clothing combined with wind at altitude is the classic scenario.

- Wind chill on exposed ridges can drop effective temperature 30-40°F (17-22°C) below ambient.
- Carry a wind shell and insulation layer even on day hikes in summer. Weather changes without warning.
- Wet cotton kills. Wear synthetic or wool layers.
- Recognize mild hypothermia early: shivering, confusion, clumsiness. Stop, shelter, add layers, eat calories, drink warm fluids.

## UV Exposure at Altitude

- UV intensity increases approximately 4-5% per 1,000 ft (300 m) of elevation gain.
- Snow reflects 80% of UV radiation, doubling effective exposure.
- Apply SPF 50+ sunscreen to all exposed skin, including under the chin and inside the nostrils. Reapply every 2 hours.
- Wear sunglasses with side shields or wrap-around design. Snow blindness (photokeratitis) causes intense pain and temporary blindness within 6-12 hours of unprotected exposure.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Snow blindness symptoms are delayed. By the time your eyes hurt, the damage is done. Prevention is the only treatment — there is no field cure. Cover the eyes, rest in darkness, and wait 24-48 hours for recovery.

## Emergency Descent Decisions

Descend immediately when any of the following occur:

- Altitude sickness symptoms are worsening (headache worsening, loss of coordination, confusion, breathlessness at rest).
- Thunderstorm approaching with no shelter available.
- Visibility drops below 100 ft (30 m) with no GPS or compass navigation capability.
- Group member is injured and cannot self-evacuate.
- Whiteout or storm makes route finding unsafe.

Do not let summit goals override survival judgment. Most mountain fatalities occur during descent, often because parties summited too late or in deteriorating conditions.

## Signaling from Elevation

- High ground is ideal for signaling. Unobstructed line-of-sight to aircraft and distant points.
- Signal mirrors are exceptionally effective at elevation. Practice aiming before you need it.
- Fires on ridges and open slopes are visible for 20+ miles (32+ km).
- Bright-colored gear laid on rock or snow creates visible patterns from the air.
- Emergency whistles carry farther in thin mountain air. Three blasts = distress.

## Common Mistakes

- **Ascending too fast.** Altitude sickness is the most preventable mountain emergency. Follow the 1,000-1,500 ft (300-450 m) per day rule above 8,000 ft (2,400 m).
- **Ignoring afternoon weather.** Storms build predictably after midday. Start early and descend before afternoon.
- **Staying on ridges in lightning.** Descend immediately when you hear thunder. The 30-30 rule saves lives.
- **Relying on the "lightning crouch."** Withdrawn by the National Weather Service in 2008. It offers no real protection and delays people from reaching actual shelter.
- **Not carrying rain gear in clear weather.** Mountain weather changes without warning. Always carry a shell.
- **Pushing to the summit despite symptoms.** HACE and HAPE kill when victims refuse to descend. The summit is optional; going home is not.
- **Traveling alone without communication.** Carry a satellite communicator or personal locator beacon. Cell phones do not work in most mountain terrain.
- **Skipping sunscreen and eye protection.** Snow blindness and severe sunburn at altitude are debilitating and entirely preventable.

## Quick Reference

| Threat | Response |
|--------|----------|
| AMS (headache, nausea) | Stop ascending, hydrate, rest 24-48 hr, descend if worsening |
| HACE (confusion, ataxia) | Descend immediately. Dexamethasone if available |
| HAPE (breathless at rest, cough) | Descend immediately. Keep upright. Oxygen if available |
| Lightning | Descend below treeline, keep moving to real shelter. The crouch does not work |
| Rockfall | Helmet, fall line travel, shout warning |
| Avalanche | Avoid 25-45° slopes with new snow. Cross one at a time |
| Hypothermia | Add layers, shelter from wind, eat calories, warm fluids |
| Snow blindness | Wear UV-blocking sunglasses. No field cure — wait 24-48 hr |
| Acclimatization | 1,000-1,500 ft (300-450 m)/day above 8,000 ft (2,400 m) |
