# Open Ocean and Coastal Survival

> How to survive after vessel loss at sea, manage life raft situations, and make safe landfall.

## At a Glance

- Never drink seawater. It accelerates dehydration and causes kidney failure. No exceptions.
- The three immediate killers at sea are drowning, hypothermia, and dehydration — in that order of urgency.
- Stay with the vessel as long as possible. Board a life raft only when the vessel is sinking beneath you.
- Signaling is your highest priority after stabilization. Rescue is your best chance of survival.
- Water procurement at sea is possible through rain collection, solar stills, and fish body fluids. Ration intake but never ration water below 1 pint (500 ml) per day.

## Priorities After Vessel Loss

Execute these in order:

1. **Get out of the water.** Board a life raft, cling to floating debris, or climb onto a capsized hull. Immersion in water drains body heat 25 times faster than air.
2. **Account for all people.** Call out, look around, count heads.
3. **Treat injuries.** Stop bleeding, splint fractures, address hypothermia.
4. **Secure the raft.** Deploy the sea anchor (drogue) to reduce drift and prevent capsizing.
5. **Salvage supplies.** Grab any floating gear, water containers, food, signaling devices, and rope within reach.
6. **Activate distress signals.** EPIRB, flares, radio (channel 16 VHF). Do not delay.
7. **Inventory supplies.** Water, food, signaling, first aid, fishing gear. Ration immediately.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never abandon a floating vessel for a life raft. A vessel — even partially swamped — is more visible, more stable, and more durable than any raft. Step UP into the life raft, never down.

## Life Raft Management

### Immediate Actions

- Inflate fully. Check for leaks. Patch with the repair kit.
- Deploy the sea anchor from the bow. It stabilizes the raft, slows drift, and prevents capsizing.
- Bail out water. Keep the floor as dry as possible to reduce hypothermia risk.
- Close the canopy if equipped. It protects from sun, wind, rain, and spray.
- Post a lookout. Assign rotating watches for ships, aircraft, and land.

### Long-Term Maintenance

- Check raft pressure twice daily. Inflate as needed — rafts lose pressure in cold, gain in heat.
- Dry the floor when possible. Wet skin loses heat and develops saltwater sores.
- Inspect the raft for chafing, especially where ropes and hardware contact the tubes.
- Air out the raft interior in calm weather to reduce condensation and mold.

## Water Procurement at Sea

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Seawater contains approximately 3.5% salt. Drinking it forces your kidneys to expel more water than the seawater provides. Seawater accelerates dehydration and causes organ failure. Never drink it.

### Rain Collection

Rain is your primary freshwater source at sea.

1. Rig every available surface to catch rain — tarps, raft canopy, clothing, plastic sheets.
2. Let the first minutes of rain wash salt off collection surfaces before capturing water.
3. Store collected water in every available container — bottles, bags, bailing buckets, boots.
4. In heavy rain, drink your fill first, then store the excess.

### Solar Still

Commercial solar stills (inflatable, cone-shaped) produce 1-3 pints (0.5-1.5 liters) per day in sunny conditions. Improvise one with clear plastic over a container of seawater — evaporation leaves salt behind, and condensation on the plastic is fresh water.

### Fish Body Fluids

- Fish eyes and spinal fluid contain drinkable moisture. Suck the eyes and cut along the spine.
- Do not eat fish without water. Protein digestion requires water and accelerates dehydration.
- Turtle blood is drinkable in survival situations. Sea turtles also provide edible meat and moisture-rich organs.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not drink urine. It contains concentrated waste products and accelerates dehydration just as seawater does.

## Food at Sea

### Fishing

- Improvise hooks from safety pins, wire, or bent metal. Use bright cloth or feathers as lures.
- Fish gather in the shade under a life raft. Dangle lines directly below.
- Small fish attract larger fish. Use small catches as bait.
- Fish are most active at dawn and dusk.
- Gut all fish immediately to prevent spoilage. Eat organs (liver, eyes) for moisture and vitamins.

### Seaweed

- Brown and green seaweed is generally edible. Rinse in fresh water if available to reduce salt.
- Sargassum (floating brown weed common in the Atlantic) is edible but tough and salty. Eat in small quantities.
- Do not eat blue-green algae blooms or any seaweed with a strong chemical odor.

### Seabirds

- Birds may land on the raft. Catch them by hand — they are often unafraid of humans at sea.
- Eat raw if you cannot make fire. Blood and organs provide moisture.
- Use feathers and bones as fishing lures.

!!! note
    Do not eat during the first 24 hours unless you have adequate water. Digestion requires water. Without water, food accelerates dehydration.

## Sun Protection

- Sunburn at sea is intensified by reflected UV from the water surface. Exposure is effectively doubled.
- Cover all exposed skin. Use clothing, rags, or improvised coverings.
- Protect eyes from glare. Improvise sunglasses by cutting narrow slits in cloth, cardboard, or duct tape.
- Lip and nose burns are common and severe. Cover with fabric or sunscreen.
- Saltwater sores develop on skin exposed to constant spray. Keep skin dry when possible and rinse with fresh water if available.

## Hypothermia in Water

| Water Temp | Survival Time |
|------------|---------------|
| 32°F (0°C) | 15-45 min |
| 40°F (4°C) | 30-90 min |
| 50°F (10°C) | 1-3 hours |
| 60°F (16°C) | 2-7 hours |
| 70°F (21°C) | 3-40 hours |

!!! danger "DANGER: the first minute is cold shock, not hypothermia"
    Going into cold water triggers an involuntary gasp and uncontrollable
    hyperventilation. If your head is submerged when it hits, you inhale water. **Most
    cold-water deaths are drownings in the first minute**, not hypothermia hours later.

    Get your head clear and do nothing else until your breathing settles — about a
    minute. Then you have roughly ten minutes of useful hand and arm strength before
    grip fails, and perhaps an hour before hypothermia becomes the threat. Spend the
    ten minutes getting out of the water or securing yourself to something that floats.

### If in the Water

- Adopt the HELP position (Heat Escape Lessening Posture): draw knees to chest, cross arms over chest, keep head above water.
- In a group, huddle together with arms linked and chests facing inward.
- Do not swim unless shore or a raft is within certain reach. Swimming accelerates heat loss and exhausts you.
- Wear a life jacket. It keeps you afloat without effort, preserving energy and heat.

### In the Raft

- Bail out water to keep the floor dry. Wet skin loses heat rapidly.
- Huddle with others for shared body heat.
- Insulate from the raft floor with any available material — life jackets, clothing, rope coils.

## Signaling at Sea

=== "Electronic"
    EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon) transmits your position to search-and-rescue satellites. Activate immediately after abandoning the vessel. VHF radio channel 16 is the international distress frequency.

=== "Visual"
    Flares: fire parachute flares at night for maximum visibility. Handheld flares for close-range signaling when rescue is in sight. Signal mirror: roughly 10 miles (16 km) in ordinary conditions, up to 50 miles (80 km) in clear air with a proper aimed mirror. Aim at aircraft or ships. Sea dye marker: turns surrounding water bright green/orange, visible from aircraft.

=== "Improvised"
    Whistle: carries farther than shouting. Metal objects: flash reflected sunlight. Dark objects waved against sky: visible against the horizon. Fire in a metal container on the raft: visible at night (use extreme caution with fire on inflatable rafts).

## Shark Management

- Do not trail hands or feet in the water.
- Remove all fish blood and guts from the raft immediately. Dump waste well away from the raft.
- If a shark bumps the raft, strike it firmly on the nose or gills with a paddle, oar, or any hard object.
- Dark-colored raft undersides attract fewer sharks than bright-colored ones.
- Sharks are most active at dawn, dusk, and night. Increase vigilance during these times.

!!! note
    Shark attacks on life rafts are rare. Most shark interactions are investigatory bumps. Firm deterrence drives them away.

## Navigation by Stars and Currents

- **Polaris (North Star):** Locate the Big Dipper, follow the pointer stars to Polaris. It marks true north within 1 degree.
- **Southern Cross:** Extend the long axis 4.5 times to find the south celestial pole.
- **Sun:** Rises approximately east, sets approximately west. Track the arc for orientation.
- **Ocean currents:** Major currents follow predictable patterns. The Gulf Stream flows northeast, the California Current flows south. Drift with favorable currents if heading toward shipping lanes or land.
- **Swells:** Open ocean swells are consistent in direction. Use them as a reference to maintain course.

## Making Landfall Safely

!!! danger "WARNING"
    More survivors die making landfall than at any other phase of ocean survival. Surf, rocks, and coral reefs are lethal obstacles.

1. Approach land during daylight. Do not attempt a night landing on an unknown coast.
2. Look for sandy beaches with gentle surf. Avoid cliffs, rocky shores, and reef breaks.
3. If approaching a reef, look for channels (dark blue water between lighter reef areas).
4. Deploy the sea anchor to slow your approach and maintain control.
5. Wear all available clothing and footwear for protection against rocks and coral.
6. Time the waves. Paddle hard during a lull between sets.
7. If capsized in surf, stay on the seaward side of the raft to avoid being pinned between the raft and rocks.

## Coastal Foraging

Once ashore:

- **Shellfish:** Mussels, clams, oysters, and limpets are found in tidal zones. Cook them — this kills bacteria and parasites — but understand that **cooking does not make toxic shellfish safe** (see the warning below).
- **Seaweed:** Edible varieties (kelp, dulse, sea lettuce) grow in intertidal areas. Rinse and eat raw or cooked.
- **Tidal pools:** Contain small fish, crabs, sea urchins, and snails. All are edible when cooked.
- **Coconuts:** Available on tropical coasts. Green coconuts contain the most liquid. Mature coconuts provide calorie-dense meat.

!!! danger "DANGER: cooking does NOT prevent paralytic shellfish poisoning"

    Saxitoxin, the toxin behind PSP, is **heat and acid stable**. Boiling,
    frying, steaming, canning, and freezing all leave it intact. Acid plus heat
    can even convert some forms into *more* toxic ones. The toxin is tasteless
    and odorless, and the shellfish look and smell completely normal.

    PSP causes tingling of the lips and face, then progressive paralysis and
    respiratory failure. There is no antidote — treatment is breathing support
    until the toxin clears. In the field that is usually not survivable.

    **The only defense is not eating contaminated shellfish:**

    - Avoid any area with an algal bloom — water discolored red, brown, or with
      unusual foam. Blooms are not always visible, so absence of discoloration
      is not proof of safety.
    - Avoid filter feeders (mussels, clams, oysters, scallops) where blooms are
      possible. Toxin concentrates in them.
    - Where local closure information exists, follow it. Coastal PSP closures
      are seasonal and exist precisely because you cannot judge this yourself.
    - If someone develops facial tingling or numbness after eating shellfish,
      treat it as an emergency and evacuate. Be ready to support breathing.

## Common Mistakes

- **Drinking seawater.** Fatal mistake. No amount of willpower overcomes the physiological harm. Never drink seawater.
- **Abandoning a floating vessel too early.** The vessel is more visible and more stable than a life raft. Step up into the raft, not down.
- **Eating without water.** Protein digestion requires water. Without adequate water, food accelerates dehydration.
- **Wasting flares.** Fire flares only when a ship or aircraft is visible. Ration signaling devices.
- **Swimming for shore.** Distances at sea are deceptive. What appears to be 1 mile (1.6 km) may be 5 miles (8 km). Swimming exhausts you and accelerates hypothermia.
- **Attempting landfall at night.** Unseen rocks, reef, and surf kill. Wait for daylight.
- **Ignoring saltwater sores.** Small wounds become infected rapidly in saltwater. Keep wounds clean and covered.
