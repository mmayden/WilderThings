# Venomous Snake Identification and Bite Protocol

> Identify the region, respect the distance, treat the bite — not the snake.

## At a Glance

- Approximately 7,000–8,000 venomous snakebites occur in the US annually; fewer than 5 are fatal.
- Do NOT attempt to catch or kill the snake for identification. Photograph it from a safe distance if possible.
- The only effective treatment for a significant envenomation is antivenom administered in a medical facility.
- Prevention is simple: watch where you step, where you reach, and where you sit.
- "Dry bites" (no venom injected) occur in 20–50% of venomous snake strikes.

## Identification by Region

### North America

#### Rattlesnakes (Crotalus and Sistrurus)

- **Range:** Every contiguous US state except Maine (also absent from Alaska and Hawaii; extirpated from Rhode Island and Delaware). Southern Canada. Mexico. Most diverse in the southwestern US.
- **Identification:** Segmented rattle on the tail (may be missing in juveniles or if broken off). Broad, triangular head. Vertical pupils. Heat-sensing pits between the eye and nostril.
- **Size:** Varies by species. Timber rattlesnake: 36–60 in (91–152 cm). Western diamondback: 48–72 in (122–183 cm). Eastern diamondback: up to 96 in (244 cm) — the largest venomous snake in North America.
- **Behavior:** Generally rattle before striking. May NOT rattle if startled or if they have learned that rattling attracts predators. Strike range is approximately one-half to two-thirds body length.
- **Venom type:** Primarily hemotoxic. Destroys tissue, disrupts clotting, causes massive local swelling.

#### Copperhead (Agkistrodon contortrix)

- **Range:** Eastern and central US from Massachusetts to Nebraska to Texas.
- **Identification:** Hourglass-shaped crossbands (wider on sides, narrow on spine). Copper-colored head. Heat pits. Vertical pupils. Stocky body.
- **Size:** 24–36 in (61–91 cm).
- **Behavior:** Freeze-and-rely-on-camouflage. Most bites occur when the snake is stepped on. Often does NOT rattle, flee, or warn.
- **Venom type:** Mildly hemotoxic. Least dangerous of North American pit vipers. Rarely fatal but causes significant pain and swelling.

#### Cottonmouth / Water Moccasin (Agkistrodon piscivorus)

- **Range:** Southeastern US. Aquatic and semi-aquatic habitats — swamps, rivers, lakes, drainage ditches.
- **Identification:** Heavy-bodied. Dark (often black) in adults. Juveniles have distinct crossbands. When threatened, opens mouth wide to display white interior ("cottonmouth"). Heat pits. Vertical pupils.
- **Size:** 30–48 in (76–122 cm). Up to 72 in (183 cm).
- **Behavior:** Stands its ground rather than fleeing. Swims with body on the surface (nonvenomous water snakes swim with body submerged and head up). Can bite underwater.
- **Venom type:** Hemotoxic. More potent than copperhead. Can cause tissue necrosis and systemic effects.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Many nonvenomous water snakes are killed because they are mistaken for cottonmouths. If you are unsure, leave the snake alone and give it a wide berth.

#### Coral Snake (Micrurus fulvius and relatives)

- **Range:** Southeastern US (eastern coral snake), Arizona and New Mexico (Arizona coral snake). Also Mexico and Central/South America.
- **Identification:** Bright bands of red, yellow/white, and black encircling the body. Small head, not triangular. Round pupils. No heat pits. This snake is an elapid, not a pit viper.
- **Size:** 20–30 in (51–76 cm). Slender.
- **The rhyme:** "Red touches yellow, kill a fellow. Red touches black, friend of Jack."
- **Limitations of the rhyme:** Works ONLY for North American species. Central and South American coral snakes, and Old World species, vary enormously. Some have aberrant color patterns. Some nonvenomous mimics break the pattern rule. Do NOT rely on the rhyme outside the US, and even within the US, treat any brightly banded red/black/yellow snake with extreme caution.
- **Behavior:** Reclusive, fossorial (burrowing). Rarely encountered. Must chew to envenomate effectively — the fangs are small and fixed.
- **Venom type:** Neurotoxic. Causes progressive paralysis, respiratory failure. Symptoms may be delayed 6–12 hours. This delay is dangerous — victims feel fine and delay seeking treatment.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Coral snake bites may initially cause minimal pain and swelling. Do NOT mistake the lack of immediate symptoms for a dry bite. Seek emergency medical care for any confirmed or suspected coral snake bite regardless of symptoms.

### General Venomous Indicators (and Why They Are Unreliable)

Common field marks taught to identify venomous snakes:

| Feature | "Rule" | Why It Is Unreliable |
|---|---|---|
| Triangular head | Venomous snakes have triangular heads | Many nonvenomous snakes flatten their heads when threatened, appearing triangular. Coral snakes have small, rounded heads. |
| Vertical pupils | Venomous = vertical; nonvenomous = round | Requires getting dangerously close to the snake's face. Also, coral snakes have round pupils. |
| Heat pits | Pit between eye and nostril = venomous | Only applies to pit vipers. Coral snakes lack pits. Requires close observation. |
| Single anal plate | Venomous snakes have a single row of subcaudal scales | Requires handling the snake. Absolutely never do this. |

**The practical rule:** If you cannot positively identify a snake as nonvenomous, treat it as venomous. Maintain a distance of at least 6 ft (1.8 m) and leave it alone.

## Bite Protocol

### What TO Do

1. **Move away** from the snake's strike range — at least 6 ft (1.8 m). A snake can strike again.
2. **Stay calm.** Increased heart rate accelerates venom distribution. Panic causes more harm than venom in most cases.
3. **Remove jewelry and constrictive clothing** from the bitten extremity immediately, before swelling begins. Rings, watches, bracelets, tight sleeves, boots.
4. **Immobilize the bitten limb.** Splint it if possible. Keep it at or slightly below heart level.
5. **Mark the edge of swelling** with a pen or marker and note the time. Repeat every 15–30 minutes. This information helps the medical team assess progression.
6. **Photograph the snake** from a safe distance if possible. Do NOT pursue, capture, or kill it.
7. **Evacuate to a medical facility.** Walk the victim out if possible. If the bite is on a leg, carry or improvise transport. Call 911 or activate an emergency beacon.
8. **Monitor for anaphylaxis.** Some people have allergic reactions to venom independent of the envenomation itself. Watch for hives, facial swelling, difficulty breathing. See [Insect Threats](insect-threats.md) for anaphylaxis management.
9. **Note the time of the bite** and any symptoms as they develop.

### What NOT to Do

- **Do NOT apply suction.** Extractor devices and mouth suction do not remove clinically significant venom and cause additional tissue damage.
- **Do NOT apply a tourniquet.** Trapping venom in a limb concentrates tissue destruction and increases the risk of amputation.
- **Do NOT apply ice.** Cold vasoconstriction concentrates venom locally and increases tissue necrosis.
- **Do NOT cut the wound.** Incision increases bleeding, infection risk, and tissue damage without removing venom.
- **Do NOT apply electric shock.** This myth persists. It causes burns and does nothing to venom.
- **Do NOT drink alcohol.** Alcohol vasodilates and accelerates venom absorption.
- **Do NOT attempt to capture or kill the snake.** This is the most common cause of second bites and third-party bites.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Every minute spent applying folk remedies is a minute not spent evacuating. The only definitive treatment is hospital-administered antivenom.

### Pressure Immobilization Technique (Elapids Only)

For **coral snake** bites (and overseas elapids like cobras, mambas, taipans), apply a pressure immobilization bandage:

1. Wrap a broad elastic bandage (ACE bandage, torn clothing strip) starting at the bite site and wrapping toward the heart.
2. Wrap firmly — as tight as for a sprained ankle. You should be able to slide a finger under the bandage.
3. Splint the entire limb to prevent movement.
4. Do NOT remove the bandage until the victim reaches a medical facility with antivenom available.

Do NOT use pressure immobilization for pit viper bites (rattlesnake, copperhead, cottonmouth). Their hemotoxic venom causes local tissue destruction, and trapping it increases necrosis.

## Dry Bites

A "dry bite" occurs when a venomous snake strikes but injects no venom or only a trivial amount.

- Occurs in an estimated 20–50% of pit viper bites and up to 50% of coral snake bites.
- The snake controls venom delivery. Defensive strikes are more likely to be dry.
- **Treat every bite as envenomated until proven otherwise.** Monitor for at least 8 hours (24 hours for coral snake) before concluding a dry bite.
- Even dry bites carry infection risk from the snake's oral bacteria. Clean the wound and seek medical evaluation.

## Antivenom

- CroFab (crotalidae polyvalent immune fab) is the standard antivenom for North American pit viper bites. Anavip is an alternative.
- Antivenom is most effective when administered within 4–6 hours of the bite.
- Delayed administration (up to 24 hours) can still be beneficial for coagulopathy.
- Antivenom is expensive ($20,000–$100,000+ per treatment course) and not stocked at every hospital. Confirm availability with the receiving facility.
- Allergic reactions to antivenom (serum sickness) occur in approximately 5–10% of cases. The medical team manages this.
- There is currently limited availability of coral snake antivenom (Pfizer's product was discontinued; remaining stock exists but is scarce). This makes prevention even more critical.

## Prevention

### Watch Where You Step

- Never step over a log without looking on the other side first. Step onto the log, then down.
- Avoid placing your hands on ledges, in rock crevices, or into holes you cannot see into.
- Use a trekking pole to probe ahead in dense vegetation, tall grass, or rocky terrain.
- Stay on established trails when possible. Avoid bushwhacking through snake habitat.

### Footwear and Clothing

- Wear boots that cover the ankle. Leather or heavy synthetic.
- Wear loose-fitting long pants. Many bites do not penetrate two layers of fabric.
- Consider snake gaiters in areas with high rattlesnake density. Gaiters cover from ankle to knee and resist fang penetration.
- Wear gloves when collecting firewood, moving rocks, or handling debris.

### Camp

- Shake out boots, clothing, and sleeping bags before use.
- Zip your tent fully. Snakes seek warmth and shelter.
- Do NOT place your sleeping bag directly on the ground in open-air bivy setups in snake country.
- Use a headlamp when moving around camp at night. Many snakes are nocturnal.
- Keep the area around your tent clear of debris, woodpiles, and rock piles that attract rodents (which attract snakes).

### Awareness by Season and Time

- Snakes are most active in spring (emergence from brumation) and fall (returning to dens).
- Peak activity times: dusk and dawn in warm months; midday in spring and fall.
- In extreme heat, snakes become nocturnal. Increase night vigilance.
- Cool nights (below 50 F / 10 C) reduce snake activity significantly.

## Common Mistakes

- **Attempting to catch or kill the snake.** Causes the majority of second bites. Leave the snake alone.
- **Applying tourniquets, suction, or ice.** Outdated first aid that worsens outcomes.
- **Assuming a lack of immediate symptoms means a dry bite.** Coral snake envenomation can be asymptomatic for hours before respiratory failure.
- **Relying on the triangular head rule.** Misidentifies many nonvenomous snakes and misses coral snakes entirely.
- **Wearing sandals or going barefoot in snake habitat.** Ankle-covering boots prevent the majority of fang-to-skin bites.
- **Reaching into blind spaces.** Never reach where you cannot see. Use a stick.
- **Walking at night without a light.** Many venomous snakes are nocturnal, especially in warm weather.

## Quick Reference

| Action | Correct | Incorrect |
|---|---|---|
| First response | Back away, stay calm | Chase or kill the snake |
| Bitten limb | Immobilize, keep at heart level | Tourniquet, ice, elevation |
| Wound care | Mark swelling, note time | Cut, suck, apply suction device |
| Jewelry | Remove immediately | Leave on |
| Transport | Evacuate to hospital ASAP | Wait and see |
| Coral snake | Pressure immobilization bandage | Same as pit viper treatment |
| Any bite | Treat as envenomated | Assume dry bite without monitoring |
