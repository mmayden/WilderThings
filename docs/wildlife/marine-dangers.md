# Marine Dangers

> The ocean contains more animals that can hurt you than any terrestrial environment — know the threats before you enter the water.

## At a Glance

- Sharks are statistically a low risk; rip currents and hypothermia kill far more swimmers
- Jellyfish stings range from painful to fatal — box jellyfish are the deadliest marine animal on Earth
- Blue-ringed octopus and cone snails deliver venom with no effective antivenom in the field
- Treat all coral cuts aggressively to prevent infection
- Know how to escape a rip current: swim parallel to shore, never fight it head-on

## Sharks

### Risk in Perspective

Unprovoked shark attacks average 70–80 per year worldwide, with 5–10 fatalities. You are more likely to die from a lightning strike or a bee sting.

### Species Risk Levels

| Species | Risk | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Great white | High (most fatal attacks) | Temperate coasts worldwide |
| Tiger shark | High | Tropical/subtropical waters |
| Bull shark | High (enters freshwater) | Warm coastal, rivers, estuaries |
| Oceanic whitetip | Moderate (open water) | Deep tropical oceans |
| Most other species | Low | Varies |

### Encounter Protocol

1. Stay calm. Do not thrash or splash.
2. Maintain eye contact with the shark. Face it at all times.
3. Back toward shore, a boat, or a reef wall slowly.
4. If the shark circles closer, prepare to defend — strike the nose, eyes, or gills with a fist, camera, or any hard object.
5. If bitten, fight aggressively. Sharks often release after a strong defensive response.
6. Exit the water immediately after any contact. Apply direct pressure to wounds.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Avoid swimming at dawn, dusk, or night — peak shark feeding times. Do not swim near fishing activity, seal colonies, or murky river mouths.

### Reducing Risk

- Swim in groups. Solitary swimmers are at higher risk.
- Avoid wearing shiny jewelry — it resembles fish scales.
- Do not enter the water with open wounds.
- Stay out of the water if sharks have been sighted recently.

!!! danger "Any sting can cause anaphylaxis — that kills faster than the venom"
    Everything on this page describes what a venom does. A severe *allergic* reaction
    to it is a separate danger, it is not dose-dependent, and it can kill within
    minutes — far faster than any sting here.

    After any sting, watch for: hives spreading beyond the sting site, swelling of the
    lips, tongue or throat, wheezing, or sudden dizziness and collapse. **Two or more
    body systems involved means anaphylaxis.**

    Epinephrine is the only first-line treatment; antihistamines do not stop it. Full
    protocol in [Bites and Stings](../medical/bites-and-stings.md#anaphylaxis).

## Jellyfish

### Box Jellyfish

=== "Indo-Pacific Box Jellyfish (Chironex fleckeri)"
    - **Deadliest marine animal.** Can kill a human in 2–5 minutes.
    - Found in northern Australia, Southeast Asia, and the Philippines from October to May.
    - Tentacles reach 10 feet (3 m) with millions of nematocysts.
    - Treatment: douse with vinegar immediately to deactivate unfired nematocysts. Call emergency services. Begin CPR if the victim stops breathing.

=== "Irukandji Jellyfish"
    - Tiny (0.4 in / 1 cm bell), nearly invisible.
    - Causes Irukandji syndrome: severe pain, nausea, hypertension, heart failure.
    - Found in Australian tropical waters and possibly wider Indo-Pacific.
    - Treatment: vinegar on the sting site, hospital care urgently needed.

### Portuguese Man-of-War

- Not a true jellyfish — a colonial organism (siphonophore).
- Tentacles trail up to 100 feet (30 m) below the surface. Sting even when washed up dead on the beach.
- Treatment: remove tentacles with a stick or gloved hand (not bare skin). Rinse with seawater. Do not use vinegar — it may worsen man-of-war stings.
- Immerse the affected area in hot water at 113°F (45°C) for 20–45 minutes.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Do not urinate on jellyfish stings. This is a myth and can worsen the sting. Use vinegar for box jellyfish or hot water for most other species.

## Sea Snakes

- Found in tropical waters of the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Not found in the Atlantic.
- Highly venomous but rarely bite. Fangs are small and most bites do not envenomate.
- Curious — they may approach divers but are not aggressive.
- If bitten: apply a pressure immobilization bandage (same as for terrestrial snake bite). Keep the victim still. Evacuate to a hospital immediately.

!!! note
    Sea snake venom is among the most toxic of any snake, but envenomation from bites is uncommon due to small fang size and reluctant biting behavior.

## Stingrays

- Buried in sand in shallow water along warm coastlines worldwide.
- Shuffle your feet when wading (the "stingray shuffle") to give them warning.
- Stings cause intense, immediate pain. The barbed spine may remain in the wound.
- Treatment: immerse in hot water at 113°F (45°C) for 30–90 minutes. Remove the spine only if it comes out easily. Seek medical care for deep punctures or spines in the chest or abdomen.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    A stingray spine to the chest or abdomen can be fatal. Do not remove the spine — stabilize it in place and evacuate immediately.

## Cone Snails

- Tropical and subtropical marine snails found on coral reefs.
- Fire a harpoon-like tooth that delivers fast-acting venom. No antivenom exists.
- Geographic cone snail (*Conus geographus*) has caused human fatalities.
- Never pick up cone snails. If stung: apply pressure immobilization bandage, keep still, evacuate to a hospital. Be prepared to provide rescue breathing — venom can cause respiratory paralysis.

## Blue-Ringed Octopus

- Tiny — body 2–3 inches (5–8 cm). Found in tide pools and shallow reefs in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, especially Australia.
- Bright blue rings flash as a warning when agitated.
- Venom (tetrodotoxin) causes paralysis and respiratory failure. No antivenom exists.
- Treatment: call emergency services immediately. Apply pressure immobilization bandage. Be prepared to perform rescue breathing for hours — the victim may be paralyzed but conscious. Full recovery is possible with respiratory support.

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Never pick up a blue-ringed octopus. A single bite can kill within minutes if breathing support is not provided.

## Coral Cuts

- Coral is a living animal with a calcium carbonate skeleton. Cuts become infected easily.
- Clean cuts immediately with fresh water and mild soap.
- Remove all coral fragments with tweezers — embedded pieces cause chronic infection.
- Apply antibiotic ointment and cover with a sterile dressing.
- Monitor for signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus.

## Rip Currents

Rip currents kill more beachgoers annually than sharks, jellyfish, and all other marine animals combined.

### How to Identify

- A channel of choppy, discolored water moving away from shore.
- A gap in the breaking waves.
- Foam, seaweed, or debris moving steadily seaward.

### How to Escape

1. Do not panic. Do not fight the current by swimming directly toward shore.
2. Swim parallel to the shoreline until you are out of the current — typically 50–100 feet (15–30 m) to one side.
3. Once free of the pull, swim at an angle back toward shore.
4. If you cannot swim out, float or tread water and signal for help. Rip currents do not pull you under — they pull you out.

!!! tip
    If caught in a rip current and too tired to swim parallel, float on your back and wave one arm to signal for help. The current will weaken beyond the breakers.

## Hypothermia in Water

Water conducts heat away from the body 25 times faster than air.

| Water Temperature | Estimated Survival Time |
|---|---|
| 32°F (0°C) | 15–45 minutes |
| 40°F (4°C) | 30–90 minutes |
| 50°F (10°C) | 1–3 hours |
| 60°F (15°C) | 2–7 hours |
| 70°F (21°C) | 3–40 hours |

- Adopt the HELP position (Heat Escape Lessening Posture): draw knees to chest, cross arms, keep head above water.
- In a group, huddle together.
- Do not swim unless shore or rescue is very close. Swimming increases heat loss.

## Common Mistakes

- **Panicking in a rip current.** Fighting the current exhausts you. Swim sideways, not toward shore.
- **Ignoring coral cuts.** Minor cuts become serious infections in tropical water within days.
- **Touching unfamiliar marine life.** Cone snails, blue-ringed octopuses, and many other small, beautiful creatures can kill.
- **Using freshwater or vinegar on the wrong jellyfish species.** Vinegar for box jellyfish. Hot water for most others. Research your destination.
- **Swimming alone in unfamiliar waters.** Every marine danger is worse when no one is there to help.
- **Underestimating cold water.** Hypothermia can incapacitate you in minutes, not hours.

## Quick Reference

| Threat | Immediate Action |
|---|---|
| Shark approach | Stay calm, maintain eye contact, back toward shore |
| Box jellyfish sting | Vinegar, call emergency services, CPR if needed |
| Man-of-war sting | Remove tentacles (not bare hands), hot water 113°F (45°C) |
| Stingray sting | Hot water 113°F (45°C) for 30–90 minutes |
| Blue-ringed octopus bite | Pressure bandage, rescue breathing, evacuate |
| Cone snail sting | Pressure bandage, rescue breathing, evacuate |
| Rip current | Swim parallel to shore, then angle in |
| Cold water immersion | HELP position, huddle, do not swim unless rescue is close |

## Fish That Poison You When Eaten

Ciguatera, scombroid, and pufferfish tetrodotoxin are hazards of *eating* marine fish
rather than being injured by them, and none of the three is destroyed by cooking. They
are covered where the reader is deciding whether to eat a catch:
[Fish That Poison You](../food/fishing-improvised.md#fish-that-poison-you).
