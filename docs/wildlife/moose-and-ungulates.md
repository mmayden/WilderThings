# Moose and Large Ungulate Safety

> Moose injure more people in North America than bears and wolves combined — size, speed, and unpredictability make large ungulates serious threats.

## At a Glance

- Moose are the most dangerous large animal in North America by encounter rate — aggressive, fast, and territorial
- A moose encounter demands the opposite of bear advice: run, and get behind a solid object like a tree
- Elk and bison are most dangerous during rut (fall) and calving season (spring)
- Wild boar charge without warning and can inflict fatal injuries with their tusks
- Vehicle collisions with large ungulates kill hundreds of people annually

## Moose

### Why Moose Are Dangerous

- Weigh 800–1,500 lbs (360–680 kg). Stand 6–7 feet (1.8–2.1 m) at the shoulder.
- Run up to 35 mph (56 km/h). Faster than you over any distance.
- Kick in all directions — front and rear hooves are lethal weapons.
- Cows with calves are extremely aggressive from May through July.
- Bulls during rut (September–October) are unpredictable and combative.
- Urban and suburban moose habituated to humans are the most frequent attackers.

### Signs of Agitation

Recognize these warnings and back away immediately:

- **Ears pinned back.** A calm moose holds its ears forward. Ears laid flat signal aggression.
- **Raised hackles.** Hair on the neck and shoulders stands up.
- **Lip licking.** The moose is stressed and preparing to act.
- **Head lowered.** Charging posture. Leave now.
- **Stomping or pawing the ground.** Imminent charge.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A moose that lowers its head, pins its ears, and advances toward you is about to charge. Do not stand your ground. Run.

### Encounter Protocol

1. Give moose a wide berth — at least 75 feet (23 m). More if a cow has a calf.
2. If a moose notices you and its body language is calm (ears up, watching), detour widely around it.
3. If a moose shows signs of agitation, back away immediately. Talk calmly but move away with purpose.
4. **If a moose charges — run.** This is the opposite of bear advice. Moose rarely pursue a sustained chase.
5. **Run toward cover, not just away.** Put a large tree, boulder, car, or building between you and the moose. Moose struggle to change direction around solid objects, so weaving among trees breaks their pursuit far better than sprinting across open ground.
6. If knocked down, curl into a ball, protect your head and neck with your arms, and stay down until the moose moves away. Getting up too soon often triggers a second attack.

!!! note
    Unlike bears, moose are not testing you with bluff charges. If a moose charges, it intends to make contact. Run and get behind cover.

### Moose and Dogs

- Moose view dogs as wolves. A dog near a moose will trigger aggression directed at both the dog and the owner.
- Keep dogs leashed in moose country.
- If your dog provokes a moose, release the leash. The moose will typically chase the dog, not you.

## Elk

### Seasonal Dangers

=== "Rut (September–October)"
    - Bulls weigh 700–1,100 lbs (320–500 kg) and are flooded with testosterone.
    - They charge people, vehicles, and other elk with little provocation.
    - Bugling signals the rut is active. Give bugling bulls extreme distance.
    - Stay at least 75 feet (23 m) away. Use telephoto lenses, not your legs.

=== "Calving Season (May–June)"
    - Cow elk are aggressive defenders. They kick with devastating force.
    - Never approach a calf, even if it appears abandoned. The mother is nearby.
    - If a cow charges, get behind a barrier and do not run in a straight line.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Elk in national parks (Yellowstone, Rocky Mountain, Banff) are habituated to humans but not tame. Habituation makes them more dangerous, not less — they charge at closer range with less warning.

## Bison

- Weigh 1,000–2,000 lbs (450–900 kg). Top speed 35 mph (56 km/h). Can change direction instantly.
- The most dangerous large animal in US national parks by number of injuries.
- Appear docile but charge with no warning.
- Maintain at least 75 feet (23 m) distance. The National Park Service recommends 25 yards (23 m) minimum.

### If a Bison Charges

1. Run. Bison usually stop after a short charge if you create distance.
2. Get behind a substantial barrier — a car, a large tree, a building.
3. Do not attempt to climb a standard fence. Bison can crash through most fences.
4. If knocked down, protect your head and stay still.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Bison gore and toss victims. Injuries include puncture wounds, broken bones, and internal organ damage. Treat any bison encounter at close range as a medical emergency waiting to happen.

## Wild Boar

Wild boar (feral hogs, feral swine) are found across the southern US, Europe, Asia, and Australia.

- Males have razor-sharp tusks up to 5 inches (12 cm) long.
- Charge low and fast, slashing upward with their tusks.
- Most aggressive when wounded, cornered, or with piglets.
- Primarily nocturnal. Most encounters occur at dawn, dusk, or during nighttime hiking.

### If a Boar Charges

1. Climb a tree, vehicle, or any elevated surface. Boar cannot climb.
2. If you cannot climb, dodge to the side at the last moment. Boar have poor turning ability at speed.
3. If knocked down, protect your femoral arteries (inner thighs) and abdomen.

!!! tip
    Boar have poor eyesight but excellent hearing and smell. If you detect boar rooting or grunting ahead, make noise and detour. Most will flee from human sounds.

## Vehicle Collisions

Ungulate-vehicle collisions cause over 200 deaths and 26,000 injuries annually in the US alone.

### Prevention

- Peak collision times: dusk, dawn, and the first hours after dark.
- Peak collision months: October–December (rut and migration) and May–June (calving, dispersal).
- If you see one deer or moose near the road, expect more. They travel in groups.
- Use high beams when no oncoming traffic is present. Scan the road edges, not just the center.

### If a Collision Is Unavoidable

1. Brake firmly but do not swerve. Swerving into oncoming traffic or off the road causes more fatalities than hitting the animal.
2. Release the brake just before impact to raise the front of the vehicle — this angles the animal over the hood rather than through the windshield.
3. After the collision, pull off the road, turn on hazard lights, and call emergency services.
4. Do not approach the injured animal. A wounded ungulate kicks and thrashes.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    A moose stands tall enough that a vehicle strike often takes out the legs, sending the full body mass — 800 to 1,500 lbs (360–680 kg) — through the windshield. Moose-vehicle collisions have a significantly higher fatality rate than deer collisions.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating moose like bears.** Standing your ground against a charging moose gets you stomped. Run and get behind cover.
- **Approaching "cute" bison or elk for photos.** These animals injure more park visitors than any predator.
- **Getting between a cow and her calf.** Any ungulate species. Always scan for calves before approaching.
- **Assuming slow movement means the animal is docile.** Bison and moose go from standing still to full charge in seconds.
- **Running across open ground from a moose.** You cannot outrun one at 35 mph. Run *to cover* — weave among trees and obstacles so it cannot follow your line. (Note this is the opposite of crocodilian advice, where zigzagging is a myth and a straight line is correct.)
- **Ignoring the dog factor.** Dogs trigger aggressive responses from moose and elk far more often than humans alone.
