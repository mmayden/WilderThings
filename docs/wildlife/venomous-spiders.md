# Venomous Spider Identification and Bite Protocol

> Identify the spider if safe, treat symptoms — most "spider bites" are something else entirely.

## At a Glance

- Only two spiders in North America pose significant medical risk: the black widow and the brown recluse.
- Most suspected spider bites are actually MRSA (staph) infections, insect bites, or other skin conditions.
- No spider actively hunts humans. Bites are almost always defensive (trapped against skin).
- Prevention is straightforward: shake out boots, check bedding, wear gloves when moving debris.
- Deaths from spider bites in the US are extremely rare — fewer than 7 per year, and most of those involve the very young, the very old, or those with compromised health.

## Black Widow (Latrodectus spp.)

### Identification

- **Size:** Female body 0.5 in (13 mm). With legs, approximately 1.5 in (38 mm) across. Males are much smaller and rarely bite.
- **Color:** Shiny jet black (southern and western black widows). Northern black widow may have red/white dorsal markings.
- **Field mark:** Red hourglass marking on the ventral (underside) abdomen. This is the defining feature. In some species, the hourglass is broken into two separate triangles or is orange rather than red.
- **Web:** Irregular, tangled, strong. No organized pattern. Found low to the ground.

### Range

All 48 contiguous US states, southern Canada, Mexico, and much of the temperate world.
Five species of *Latrodectus* occur in North America. Most common in the South and West.

The genus is global and goes by different names, which matters if you are reading this
outside North America. All of them cause the same syndrome and take the same treatment:

| Region | Common name | Species |
|--------|------------|---------|
| North America | Black widow | *L. mactans*, *L. hesperus*, *L. variolus* |
| Australia, New Zealand | Redback, katipō | *L. hasselti*, *L. katipo* |
| Southern Africa | Button spider | *L. indistinctus* and others |
| Mediterranean, Central Asia | Malmignatte, karakurt | *L. tredecimguttatus* |
| South America | Viuda negra | *L. curacaviensis* and others |

The redback is the most common significant spider envenomation in Australia — far more
frequent than funnel-web bites. Unlike the funnel-web, **a redback bite does not take a
pressure immobilization bandage.** The venom acts slowly and binding the limb only
concentrates the pain.

### Habitat

- Dark, undisturbed spaces: woodpiles, stone walls, sheds, garages, outhouses, crawl spaces.
- Under rocks, logs, and debris.
- In boots, gloves, and clothing left outdoors or in storage.
- Outdoor furniture, mailboxes, meter boxes.
- Rarely found in occupied, well-lit, frequently-used indoor spaces.

### Bite and Symptoms (Latrodectism)

The bite itself may feel like a pinprick or go unnoticed.

**Timeline:**

- **0–30 minutes:** Small red mark at bite site. Minor local pain.
- **30–60 minutes:** Pain intensifies and begins spreading. Muscle cramping starts, typically in the large muscle groups nearest the bite.
- **1–6 hours:** Full-body muscle cramping, rigidity, and pain (especially abdomen, back, and chest). Abdominal rigidity can mimic appendicitis or peritonitis. Sweating, nausea, vomiting, elevated blood pressure, headache.
- **6–24 hours:** Symptoms peak. Severe cases include hypertension, tachycardia, respiratory difficulty.
- **24–72 hours:** Gradual resolution in most cases. Residual muscle pain and weakness can persist for weeks.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If the victim develops severe abdominal rigidity, chest tightness, or difficulty breathing, evacuate immediately. These symptoms indicate significant envenomation requiring medical intervention.

### Treatment

- Clean the bite with soap and water.
- Apply ice wrapped in cloth for 10–15 minutes to manage pain.
- Over-the-counter pain relief (ibuprofen, acetaminophen). Opioids may be needed for severe pain.
- Seek medical care for moderate to severe symptoms.
- Hospital treatment is opioids for pain and benzodiazepines for muscle spasm. Antivenom
  (Antivenin *Latrodectus mactans*) is held back for severe envenomation — seizures,
  hypertensive crisis, respiratory compromise, or pain that opioids will not touch —
  because of the risk of anaphylaxis to the horse serum it is made from.

!!! note "IV calcium gluconate is no longer recommended"
    It was standard for decades and appears in older references. Trials found it does not
    work: 96% of moderate-to-severe cases started on calcium gluconate needed opioids
    added anyway. Methocarbamol was dropped for the same reason. Listed here because a
    reader may find the old advice elsewhere and wonder why nobody is using it.
- Most healthy adults recover fully within 1–3 days with supportive care.

## Brown Recluse (Loxosceles reclusa)

### Identification

- **Size:** Body 0.25–0.5 in (6–13 mm). Leg span approximately 1 in (25 mm).
- **Color:** Tan to dark brown, uniform. Legs are slightly lighter, no banding.
- **Field mark:** Dark violin-shaped marking on the cephalothorax (fused head-chest segment), with the neck of the violin pointing toward the abdomen. This mark is subtle and requires close inspection.
- **Eyes:** Six eyes arranged in three pairs (dyads). Most spiders have eight eyes. This is the most reliable identification feature but requires magnification.
- **Web:** Small, irregular, loosely woven. Not used for prey capture — the recluse hunts actively.

### Range

Central and south-central US: a band from Nebraska to Ohio, south to Texas and Georgia. This range is frequently overstated. Brown recluses are NOT established in California, the Pacific Northwest, New England, or most of the western US.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If you are outside the established range of the brown recluse (roughly the central US), a suspected "recluse bite" is far more likely to be a MRSA skin infection. See a physician for proper diagnosis. Do NOT self-diagnose or self-treat for a recluse bite.

### Habitat

- True to its name: reclusive. Found in dark, undisturbed areas.
- Closets, attics, basements, crawl spaces, storage boxes.
- Behind furniture, inside wall voids, under baseboards.
- In clothing, shoes, and bedding that has been undisturbed for weeks.
- Outdoors: under bark, rocks, and debris in protected locations.
- Can establish large populations in a single building (hundreds to thousands) without any bites occurring, because they avoid contact with humans.

### Bite and Progression

The bite is often painless at first.

**Timeline:**

- **0–2 hours:** Mild stinging or nothing at all. Small red area.
- **2–8 hours:** Pain increases. Red area develops a white center (vasoconstriction) surrounded by a red ring — the "red, white, and blue" or "bull's-eye" pattern.
- **8–24 hours:** A blister or vesicle may form at the bite center.
- **24–72 hours:** In moderate to severe cases, the central tissue begins to darken (necrosis). The area becomes purple or black.
- **3–14 days:** The necrotic area may enlarge to an ulcer. Size varies from a dime to 6+ in (15+ cm) across in severe cases. This is dermonecrosis.
- **2–8 weeks:** The ulcer slowly heals by secondary intention (from the edges inward). Scarring is common.
- **Systemic symptoms (rare, <1% of bites):** Fever, chills, nausea, hemolytic anemia, kidney failure (loxoscelism). Most common in children and debilitated individuals. Can be fatal.

### Treatment

- Clean the bite with soap and water.
- Apply ice wrapped in cloth for 10–15 minutes at a time to slow tissue destruction.
- Immobilize the bite area and keep it elevated.
- Seek medical evaluation within 24 hours for any suspected recluse bite with progressive symptoms.
- **No FDA-approved antivenom exists for brown recluse bites in the US.** Treatment is supportive.
- Wound care: keep the ulcer clean, apply antibiotic ointment, cover with a sterile bandage. Change dressings daily.
- Surgical debridement may be needed for large necrotic wounds, but early surgery is generally avoided — it often worsens outcomes. Allow the wound to declare itself over 1–2 weeks before surgical intervention.
- Dapsone and other systemic therapies are sometimes used but remain controversial.

## Hobo Spider (*Eratigena agrestis*, formerly *Tegenaria agrestis*)

- **Range:** Pacific Northwest US and southern British Columbia.
- **Previously considered:** A significant medical threat causing necrotic bites similar to brown recluse.
- **Current understanding:** The CDC removed the hobo spider from its list of medically significant spiders. Evidence for necrotic bites is weak and largely based on flawed studies. Most "hobo spider bites" are likely misdiagnosed MRSA infections.
- **Identification:** Brown, funnel-web builder, 12–18 mm body, herringbone pattern on abdomen. Difficult to distinguish from many similar species.
- **Practical approach:** If bitten by any spider in the Pacific Northwest and necrotic symptoms develop, seek medical care. Treat the wound, not the species identification.

## Funnel-Web Spider (Atrax and Hadronyche spp.)

- **Range:** Eastern Australia, particularly within 100 mi (160 km) of Sydney.
- **Significance:** The Sydney funnel-web (Atrax robustus) is arguably the most dangerous spider in the world.
- **Size:** Body up to 1.4 in (35 mm). Robust, glossy black.
- **Behavior:** Aggressive when cornered. Rears up and strikes downward with large fangs. Can bite through fingernails and soft shoes.
- **Venom:** Neurotoxic (atracotoxin). Causes excitatory neurotransmitter release. Symptoms include profuse sweating, muscle fasciculations, salivation, tearing, tachycardia, hypertension, pulmonary edema. Can kill within 15 minutes in severe cases (children).
- **Treatment:** Apply a pressure immobilization bandage immediately (same technique as for elapid snakebites — see [Venomous Snakes](venomous-snakes.md)). Evacuate to a hospital with antivenom. Since the introduction of antivenom in 1981, no deaths have occurred from confirmed funnel-web bites in Australia.
- **Prevention:** Shake out shoes and clothing. Check under toilet seats. Do not leave laundry on the ground. Pool fences and water features attract wandering males.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If bitten by a funnel-web spider, apply a pressure immobilization bandage to the entire limb immediately and do NOT remove it until you reach a hospital. This is a life-threatening emergency.

## Bite First Aid (General)

1. **Remain calm.** Spider bites are rarely life-threatening.
2. **Identify the spider** if safe to do so. Trap it under a glass or photograph it. Do NOT handle it.
3. **Clean the bite** with soap and water.
4. **Apply ice** wrapped in cloth, 10–15 minutes on, 10–15 minutes off.
5. **Monitor for symptom progression.** Mark the border of any redness with a pen and note the time.
6. **Seek medical care** if: severe pain develops, muscle cramping begins, the wound develops a bull's-eye pattern, necrosis appears, or systemic symptoms (fever, nausea, difficulty breathing) occur.
7. **Update tetanus vaccination** if not current (within the past 10 years).

## When to Evacuate

Evacuate immediately (call emergency services or activate a beacon) if:

- The victim is a child under 5, elderly, or immunocompromised.
- Severe muscle cramping, rigidity, or abdominal pain develops (likely black widow).
- Difficulty breathing, chest tightness, or cardiovascular symptoms appear.
- Signs of anaphylaxis: hives, facial swelling, wheezing. See [Insect Threats](insect-threats.md) for anaphylaxis management.
- Rapidly spreading redness, streaking (red lines extending from the bite), or fever — these suggest secondary bacterial infection, not spider venom, and require antibiotics.
- In Australia: any suspected funnel-web bite.

## Prevention

### Shake and Check

- Shake out boots, shoes, and clothing before putting them on, especially if stored outdoors or in undisturbed areas.
- Check sleeping bags and bedding before entering.
- Inspect gloves before putting them on.
- Check under toilet seats (particularly relevant for funnel-webs in Australia and black widows in outhouses).

### Camp and Shelter

- Keep your tent zipped at all times. Even a small gap admits spiders.
- Do not leave clothing or gear on the ground overnight.
- Store boots inside your tent or stuff sack, upside down.
- Shake out any gear that sat on the ground before packing it.

### Handling Debris

- Wear leather gloves when moving firewood, rocks, lumber, or stored equipment.
- Break apart woodpiles from the outside. Do not reach into the center.
- Use a stick to flip rocks and logs rather than your hands.
- Tap or shake items before picking them up. Vibration causes most spiders to flee.

### Around Structures

- Seal cracks and gaps in building foundations, window frames, and door frames.
- Remove woodpiles, rock piles, and debris from directly against structures.
- Use sticky traps in garages, basements, and storage areas to monitor spider populations.
- Reduce clutter in storage spaces. Cardboard boxes are preferred habitat for recluses; switch to sealed plastic bins.

## Myth-Busting

### "Most spider bites are from spiders"

They are not. Multiple studies show that 60–80% of lesions diagnosed as "spider bites" by patients and even by some physicians are actually:

- **MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) skin infections** — by far the most common misdiagnosis.
- Other bacterial infections (cellulitis, abscesses).
- Insect bites (mosquitoes, fleas, bedbugs, mites — see [Insect Threats](insect-threats.md)).
- Herpes simplex, diabetic ulcers, Lyme disease, fungal infections.
- Chemical burns, vasculitis, and other dermatological conditions.

A confirmed spider bite requires either witnessing the spider biting you or capturing the spider on your body immediately after feeling the bite. Waking up with a lesion is not a spider bite until proven otherwise.

### "Brown recluse bites always cause necrosis"

They do not. Most confirmed recluse bites heal without significant necrosis. Only approximately 10% of bites develop moderate to severe dermonecrosis.

### "All brown spiders in the US are brown recluses"

False. Hundreds of brown spider species exist in North America. The brown recluse has a specific, limited range. Outside that range, a brown spider is almost certainly not a recluse.

### "Daddy longlegs are the most venomous spider but their fangs are too small to bite"

False on every count. "Daddy longlegs" refers to harvestmen (not spiders, no venom glands), cellar spiders (minimal venom, can bite, no medical significance), or crane flies (not arachnids). This myth has no factual basis.

## Common Mistakes

- **Self-diagnosing a skin lesion as a spider bite.** Seek medical evaluation. MRSA requires antibiotics, not wound care for envenomation.
- **Applying a tourniquet to a spider bite.** Never appropriate for spider envenomation.
- **Cutting, sucking, or applying suction to a spider bite.** Ineffective and harmful.
- **Early surgical excision of a brown recluse bite.** Usually worsens the wound. Allow the lesion to fully declare itself first.
- **Ignoring camp hygiene.** Spiders enter open tents, get into boots, and nest in piled clothing. Prevention takes 30 seconds per item.
- **Panicking over a spider sighting.** Most spiders are harmless and provide pest control. Identify, avoid, and move on.
