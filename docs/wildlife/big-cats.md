# Big Cat Encounters

> Face the cat, stand tall, fight back — never run, never crouch, never look like prey.

## At a Glance

- Mountain lions are ambush predators. If you can see one, you have already disrupted its attack pattern.
- Running triggers pursuit. Stand your ground every time.
- Children are disproportionately targeted. Keep them close and pick them up immediately.
- Fatal mountain lion attacks average 1 per year in North America. Lightning kills 20.
- Most big cat encounters end without contact if you deny the cat an easy target.

## Mountain Lion / Cougar

### Range and Habitat

Mountain lions (*Puma concolor*) occupy the largest range of any terrestrial mammal in the Western Hemisphere: from British Columbia to Patagonia. In North America, established populations exist throughout the western states and provinces, Florida (the Florida panther subspecies), and expanding territory in the Midwest and East.

Preferred habitat includes:
- Rugged terrain with dense cover (canyons, rock outcrops, thick brush).
- Transition zones between forest and open areas.
- Proximity to deer populations (their primary prey).
- Elevations from sea level to 10,000 ft (3,000 m).

### Behavior

- Primarily crepuscular and nocturnal. Most active at dawn and dusk.
- Solitary except during mating or when a female has kittens.
- Territorial. Males maintain ranges of 50–150 sq mi (130–390 sq km).
- Kill by ambush: stalk from behind, leap onto the back, deliver a bite to the base of the skull or throat.
- Almost never attack from the front when the target is facing them and alert.
- Cache large kills by covering them with leaves, dirt, and debris. Return to feed over several days.

### Signs of Presence

#### Tracks
- Round, 3–3.5 in (7.5–9 cm) wide. Wider than long.
- Four toes, no claw marks (retractable claws).
- Leading edge of the heel pad has two lobes; trailing edge has three lobes.
- Distinguish from canine tracks: canines show claws, are longer than wide, and have a single-lobed heel pad leading edge.

#### Scat
- Segmented, blunt-ended.
- Contains hair and bone fragments.
- Often partially buried or covered with debris (scraping behavior).

#### Scrapes
- Mounds of dirt, leaves, or pine needles 6–12 in (15–30 cm) long, often with urine.
- Found along ridgelines, trail junctions, and territory boundaries.
- Indicate an active territorial cat. Increase your alertness.

#### Cached Kills
- Deer or other large prey partially covered with debris.
- Strong odor of decomposition.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If you find a cached kill, leave the area immediately. The cat is nearby and will defend this food source aggressively. Do NOT investigate. See also [Bear Safety](bear-safety.md) — bears also cache kills and react similarly.

## Encounter Protocol

### When You See a Mountain Lion

1. **Stop.** Do NOT run. Do NOT turn your back.
2. **Face the cat** directly. Maintain eye contact.
3. **Appear large.** Raise your arms overhead. Open your jacket wide. Stand on a rock if available.
4. **Speak firmly.** Use a loud, authoritative voice. Do NOT scream — high-pitched sounds can mimic prey.
5. **Do NOT crouch or bend down.** This makes you look like a four-legged animal. If you need to pick something up, squat while maintaining eye contact.
6. **Back away slowly** if possible. Give the cat a clear escape route. Never corner a mountain lion.
7. **Throw objects** if the cat holds its ground or approaches. Rocks, sticks, water bottles, trekking poles. Aim for the body.

### If the Cat Charges

- Hold your ground. Many charges are bluff charges — the cat pulls up short.
- Deploy bear spray if you carry it. Effective at 15–20 ft (4.5–6 m). See [Bear Safety](bear-safety.md) for spray technique.
- Use trekking poles offensively: thrust at the face.

### If the Cat Makes Contact

- **Fight back aggressively.** There is no "play dead" protocol for big cats.
- Target the **eyes** and **nose**. These are the most sensitive areas.
- Use any available weapon: knife, rock, trekking pole, bare hands.
- Protect your neck and throat — cover them with one arm while striking with the other.
- Gouge the eyes. This is not the time for restraint.
- Mountain lions often release and retreat once prey fights back effectively.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never play dead with a mountain lion. Every mountain lion attack is predatory. Playing dead confirms you as food.

## Children and Group Safety

### Children

- Mountain lions preferentially target small, fast-moving beings that appear prey-sized.
- Keep children within arm's reach in cat country. Do NOT let them run ahead on the trail.
- If a cat appears, **pick children up immediately** — even if you must crouch briefly to do so. Put them on your shoulders.
- Instruct children to face the cat, raise their arms, and shout if they are old enough to follow directions.
- Never let children play alone in areas with known mountain lion presence.

### Group Dynamics

- Travel in groups. Mountain lions almost never attack groups of three or more.
- If a cat appears, group together. Stand shoulder to shoulder. Raise arms collectively.
- Place children and smaller individuals in the center.
- Present a unified, large, loud front.
- Do NOT scatter. A fleeing individual becomes an irresistible target.

## Night Hiking in Cat Country

Night hiking in active mountain lion territory substantially increases your risk. Mountain lions rely on stealth and darkness.

- Carry a powerful headlamp (300+ lumens) and sweep it regularly.
- Keep the beam slightly above eye level to catch eyeshine. Cat eyes reflect brightly — usually green or amber.
- Make more noise than usual: talk, sing, strike trekking poles together.
- Keep your group tight. No stragglers.
- If you detect eyeshine that does not retreat, stop, face the source, and make yourself large.
- Carry bear spray in a hip holster with the safety removed while actively hiking.
- Avoid wearing earbuds or headphones. Your ears are your best early warning system.

## Statistics

### North America (Mountain Lion)

- Fatal attacks: approximately 27 confirmed deaths from 1890 to 2025.
- Average: roughly 1 fatality per 5 years historically, increasing to approximately 1 per year in recent decades as habitat overlap grows.
- Non-fatal attacks: approximately 200 confirmed from 1890 to 2025.
- Most victims: solo adults or unaccompanied children. Children under 16 are overrepresented.
- Most attacks: dawn, dusk, or full darkness.
- Most common regions: British Columbia, California, Colorado.

### Context

- Deer kill approximately 200 people per year in the US (vehicle collisions).
- Domestic dogs kill approximately 30–50 people per year in the US.
- Lightning kills approximately 20 people per year in the US.
- Mountain lions kill approximately 1 person per year in the US and Canada combined.

Your risk is real but statistically low. Proper behavior reduces it to near zero.

## Jaguar

The jaguar (*Panthera onca*) ranges from the southwestern US (rare, limited sightings in Arizona and New Mexico) through Central and South America.

- Significantly more powerful than a mountain lion. Males reach 200–250 lb (90–113 kg).
- Kills by biting through the skull (unique among big cats).
- Attacks on humans are extremely rare. Jaguars prefer to avoid people entirely.
- If encountered: follow the same protocol as for mountain lions. Face the cat, appear large, fight back if attacked.
- Jaguars are strong swimmers and excellent climbers. Climbing a tree is not a reliable escape.

## Leopard

The leopard (*Panthera pardus*) ranges across sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.

- Most adaptable of the big cats. Thrives near human settlements.
- Significantly more likely to attack humans than mountain lions, particularly in India.
- Man-eating leopards are a documented phenomenon, historically and currently.
- Encounter protocol: identical to mountain lion. Face it, stand tall, fight back.
- Leopards are nocturnal and excel at ambush. Avoid walking alone at night in leopard territory.
- In camp, keep fires burning and sleep inside a tent or enclosed structure.

## Common Mistakes

- **Running.** The single most dangerous thing you can do. A mountain lion runs at 50 mph (80 km/h) in short bursts. Running triggers hard-wired pursuit behavior.
- **Crouching or bending down.** This transforms your silhouette from an upright human (not typical prey) to a four-legged animal shape (typical prey).
- **Turning your back.** Mountain lions attack from behind. Face the cat at all times.
- **Playing dead.** No big cat respects this. You become an easy meal.
- **Ignoring signs of presence.** Fresh tracks, scrapes, and especially cached kills mean a cat is actively using the area. Increase your vigilance or reroute.
- **Letting children run ahead.** A small, fast-moving figure at a distance from the group is the ideal target.
- **Wearing earbuds on the trail.** You lose your most important sense for detecting approach.
- **Approaching a sighting for photos.** Maintain at least 100 yd (91 m) distance and leave the area.

## Quick Reference

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Cat spotted at distance | Stop, face it, appear large, speak firmly, back away slowly |
| Cat approaching | Stand ground, shout, throw objects, deploy spray |
| Cat charges | Hold ground (likely bluff), spray, thrust with poles |
| Contact made | Fight aggressively — eyes, nose, throat |
| Children present | Pick them up, place in center of group |
| Night eyeshine detected | Stop, face source, light it up, appear large |
| Cached kill found | Leave the area immediately |
| Fresh tracks/scrapes | Increase alertness, make noise, tighten group |
