# Shelter Principles

> Understand the priorities, site selection, and physics of field shelter before you build anything.

## At a Glance

- Shelter is typically the first survival priority — exposure kills faster than dehydration or starvation.
- The five shelter priorities in order: location, insulation, wind protection, waterproofing, signaling visibility.
- Ground insulation matters more than walls or a roof. Still air is a good insulator; cold ground is not, and lying on it conducts heat out of you continuously all night.
- Smaller shelters retain more body heat. Build only as large as necessary.
- Know when NOT to build — if rescue is imminent, conserve energy and stay visible.

## The Survival Priority Hierarchy

Before building shelter, understand where it falls in the overall survival priority list:

1. **Immediate safety** — escape danger (fire, flood, avalanche, hostile terrain).
2. **Shelter** — protect against exposure (hypothermia, hyperthermia, wind, rain).
3. **Water** — prevent dehydration.
4. **Fire** — warmth, water purification, signaling, morale.
5. **Food** — last priority; the body survives weeks without food.

Shelter and fire often compete for the second slot depending on environment. In cold, wet, or windy conditions, shelter comes first. In hot environments, shade IS shelter.

## The 5 Priorities of Shelter

### 1. Location

Choosing where to build matters more than what you build. A perfect structure in a bad location fails. See the full site selection criteria below.

### 2. Insulation

Dead air space trapped in fibrous material is the primary insulator. The ground, not the air, is the greatest heat thief. Prioritize ground insulation above all else.

### 3. Wind Protection

Wind strips heat from your body through convection. A 10 mph (16 km/h) wind makes 40°F
(4°C) feel like 34°F (1°C) — and the effect grows sharply as the air gets colder: the
same wind at 20°F (-7°C) feels like 9°F (-13°C). Orient the shelter opening away from
prevailing wind. Use natural windbreaks (fallen trees, rock walls, dense vegetation).

### 4. Waterproofing

Wet clothing and wet insulation lose up to 90% of their insulating value. Keep precipitation off your body and out of your shelter. Layer roofing materials like shingles — overlap from bottom to top so water runs off.

### 5. Signaling Visibility

Balance concealment (from wind and weather) with visibility (for rescue). If rescue is expected, position the shelter near an open area where you can be seen from the air. Place signal markers near the shelter. Do not bury yourself so deep in the woods that searchers cannot find you.

## Site Selection Criteria

### Drainage

- Build on a slight slope or elevated ground — never in a depression, ditch, or dry wash.
- Test the ground: push a stick into the soil. If it goes in easily and the soil is saturated, move uphill.
- Look for high-water marks on trees and banks — flash floods follow rain miles upstream.
- Avoid dry riverbeds and canyon bottoms entirely.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Flash floods kill in minutes with no warning. Never shelter in a dry streambed, arroyo, or canyon bottom, regardless of current weather.

### Deadfall Danger

- Look up. Dead standing trees ("widow makers") and hanging branches kill without warning.
- Check all trees within a 50-foot (15 m) radius of your site for dead limbs, cracked trunks, and leaning deadwood.
- In high wind, this radius must increase — large trees throw debris well beyond 50 feet (15 m).
- Avoid building directly under coconut palms, large dead snags, or trees with visible rot.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    "Widow makers" — dead branches lodged in living trees — drop without wind or warning. Inspect the canopy above any shelter site.

### Wind Direction

- Determine prevailing wind by observing tree lean, flag-shaped growth patterns, and feeling the breeze.
- Orient the shelter opening **90 degrees to prevailing wind** (perpendicular) or away from it entirely.
- Use natural windbreaks: rock faces, dense vegetation hedges, fallen logs, terrain features.
- In winter, avoid exposed ridgelines and hilltops — wind chill is extreme at elevation.
- In summer in hot climates, position for cross-ventilation to prevent heat buildup.

### Water Proximity

- Build close enough to a water source for convenient access — within 200 yards (180 m).
- Build far enough to avoid flood risk, insect swarms, and damp ground — at least 50 feet (15 m) above and away from the waterline.
- Consider the terrain between your shelter and the water source — a steep, slippery descent wastes energy and creates injury risk.

### Material Availability

- Survey the area before committing to a site. You need abundant natural materials within easy carrying distance.
- For a debris hut, you need hundreds of pounds of dead leaves, grass, or forest litter within 100 yards (90 m). See [Debris Hut Construction](./debris-hut.md).
- For any shelter, you need structural materials: ridgepoles, ribbing sticks, large branches.
- If materials are scarce, move to a better site — hauling materials long distances wastes critical energy.

### Other Site Factors

- **Flat sleeping area** — a slight slope for drainage is fine, but avoid slopes steep enough to make sleeping uncomfortable. Sleep with your head uphill.
- **Sun exposure** — in cold weather, choose a south-facing slope (Northern Hemisphere) for maximum solar warming. In hot weather, seek north-facing shade.
- **Animal activity** — avoid game trails, animal dens, wasp nests, and ant mounds. Check the ground and nearby trees for signs of habitation.
- **Insect density** — standing water, dense brush, and shaded swampy areas breed mosquitoes. Move to higher, breezier ground when possible.

## Insulation Principles

### Dead Air Space

Insulation works by trapping pockets of still air. Moving air (convection) carries heat away; still air does not. Every insulation method — natural or synthetic — relies on this principle.

- **Loose, fluffy materials** trap more dead air: dry leaves, grass, pine needles, cattail fluff.
- **Compressed materials** lose insulating value — do not pack insulation tight.
- **Wet materials** lose insulating value because water conducts heat 25 times faster than air. Keep insulation dry.

### Ground Insulation Is Priority One

Still air is one of the better insulators available to you — it is why loft matters in
clothing and debris. Cold ground is the opposite: it is in direct contact, it conducts
continuously, and it never warms up the way a pocket of trapped air does. Body heat also
melts a damp patch underneath you, which makes the conduction worse as the night goes on.

Without ground insulation, your shelter's roof and walls are close to worthless.

**Minimum ground insulation:** 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) of compressed material between your body and the earth.

**Materials for ground insulation (best to worst):**

1. Closed-cell foam pad — best artificial option.
2. Dry evergreen boughs (spruce, fir, pine) — layer 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) deep; they compress to 3–4 inches (8–10 cm).
3. Dry leaves, grass, or straw — pile 12 inches (30 cm) deep; they compress significantly.
4. Dry bark slabs — lay flat as a base, then add softer material on top.
5. Logs laid side by side — crude but better than bare ground.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Sleeping directly on the ground without insulation causes rapid heat loss even in moderate temperatures. Ground insulation is not optional — it is the most critical component of any shelter.

### Wall and Roof Insulation

Apply the same principle: trap dead air space.

- Minimum roof insulation for a debris shelter: 3 feet (1 m) of loose debris. More is better; less is the commonest reason a shelter fails.
- Test with the "arm test" — push your arm into the debris layer from outside. If you can see daylight through the inside, add more material.
- In rain environments, the outer layer must shed water — shingle bark, layers of broad leaves, or tightly packed fine debris acts as a thatch.

## Body Heat Retention

Your body is the primary heat source inside a shelter. All shelter design revolves around retaining that heat.

### Heat Loss Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Description | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Conduction** | Heat transfers through direct contact (ground) | Ground insulation |
| **Convection** | Moving air carries heat away (wind) | Windproofing, small openings |
| **Radiation** | Body radiates heat to cold surroundings | Reflective surfaces, small shelter volume |
| **Evaporation** | Moisture on skin or clothing evaporates, cooling you | Stay dry, ventilate moisture |
| **Respiration** | Exhaling warm, moist air | Unavoidable; minimize with shelter warmth |

### Practical Measures

- **Wear a hat** — the head is only about 10% of your body surface and loses heat
  roughly in proportion to that, not the 40–45% older manuals claim. Cover it anyway:
  it is usually the one part left bare, and the body defends the scalp's blood supply
  rather than shutting it down the way it does the hands and feet.
- **Insulate extremities** — stuff dry grass or leaves into boots and gloves.
- **Stay dry** — remove wet clothing before entering the shelter. Hang wet items to dry if possible.
- **Eat before sleeping** — digestion generates heat. Fats and proteins produce more sustained warmth than carbohydrates.
- **Exercise before entering shelter** — do light exercise (squats, push-ups) to generate heat, then enter the shelter immediately while warm. Do not exercise to the point of sweating.

## Shelter Size: Smaller Is Warmer

A shelter needs to hold your body and nothing more. Every cubic foot / cubic meter of extra space is air your body must heat.

### Sizing Guidelines

- **Emergency body-heat shelter** — just large enough to lie down with 6 inches (15 cm) of clearance on each side and above.
- **Overnight shelter** — room to sit up, store gear at your feet, and shift sleeping positions.
- **Long-term shelter** — room to sit, store materials, and perform basic tasks during bad weather. Larger but still compact.

### The Coffin Rule

If your shelter is only slightly larger than a coffin, your body heat keeps it warm. This is the target for emergency shelters. Comfort takes second place to warmth.

## Shelter Categories

### Emergency Shelter (0–4 Hours to Build)

- Purpose: survive the immediate night.
- Examples: leaf pile, snow trench, poncho lean-to, natural rock overhang, fallen tree cavity.
- Priority: speed. Imperfect shelter now beats perfect shelter at midnight.
- Build with whatever is immediately available. Do not waste daylight searching for ideal materials.

### Overnight Shelter (4–8 Hours to Build)

- Purpose: reliable protection for one or more nights while planning next steps.
- Examples: debris hut, lean-to with fire reflector, tarp shelter, quinzhee.
- Priority: balance of warmth, weather protection, and reasonable comfort.
- See [Debris Hut Construction](./debris-hut.md) for the most effective single-person overnight shelter.

### Long-Term Shelter (8+ Hours to Build)

- Purpose: sustained living in a survival or wilderness situation.
- Examples: log cabin, wickiup, wattle-and-daub structure, snow cave (in persistent cold).
- Priority: durability, space for gear and activities, fire integration, ventilation.
- Invest time in improving drainage, adding a fire pit or reflector wall, and upgrading insulation over successive days.

## Hot Weather Shelters

In hot environments, shelter serves the opposite function: blocking heat and providing shade.

### Principles

- **Shade is shelter.** Block direct solar radiation as the first priority.
- **Elevate your shelter** — the ground surface in desert sun can exceed 150°F (65°C). Raise your sleeping/resting surface at least 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) off the ground.
- **Ventilate** — allow air to flow through. Open-sided shelters work better than enclosed ones in heat.
- **Double-layer roof** — two layers of material with an air gap between them reduce heat transfer significantly compared to a single layer.
- **Avoid activity during midday** — rest in shade from 10 AM to 4 PM. Work and travel during cooler hours.

### Desert Shelter Construction

1. Find or create shade — use a tarp, poncho, or improvised cover.
2. If digging is possible, excavate a shallow trench (body-width, 18 inches / 45 cm deep) and cover it with a raised shade layer. The trench benefits from cooler subsurface temperature.
3. Anchor the shade layer 12–24 inches (30–60 cm) above the trench.
4. If using a vehicle, use the vehicle's shadow during the appropriate time of day, or suspend a reflective blanket from the vehicle.

## Cold Weather Considerations

- Insulate aggressively — minimum 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) ground insulation, 3 feet (1 m) roof debris.
- Seal all gaps against wind infiltration.
- Keep the entrance small and consider a door plug of stuffed debris.
- Build a fire reflector wall from green logs or stacked rocks to redirect radiant heat toward the shelter opening.
- If using fire near a shelter, maintain adequate clearance — at least 6 feet (1.8 m) from combustible materials.
- In snow, a quinzhee or snow cave offers excellent insulation — settled snow is mostly
  trapped air. Build either one only after reading [Snow Shelters](./snow-shelters.md):
  they need ventilation holes maintained at all times, and an unsettled quinzhee
  collapses while you are inside hollowing it.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Fire near a debris shelter is extremely dangerous. Dry leaves and grass ignite instantly. Maintain strict fire discipline and keep flames well clear of shelter materials.

## When NOT to Build

Not every situation calls for shelter construction. Conserve energy when:

- **Rescue is imminent** — stay visible, stay put, signal instead.
- **You have existing shelter** — a vehicle, aircraft wreckage, or standing structure provides better shelter than anything you can build. Improve it rather than starting from scratch.
- **Conditions are mild** — if the night temperature stays above 60°F (15°C) with no rain or wind, your energy may be better spent on other priorities.
- **You lack daylight** — building in darkness is inefficient and dangerous. In that case, make an emergency leaf pile or use what is immediately available rather than attempting a full build.
- **You are injured** — a complex shelter build with a broken limb or sprained joint may worsen your injury. Choose the simplest adequate option.

## Common Mistakes

- **Building too large** — the most common error. A debris hut for one person needs to be coffin-sized, not room-sized.
- **Neglecting ground insulation** — the most consequential error. Without ground insulation, all other efforts are wasted.
- **Building in a hazardous location** — flood zones, beneath widow makers, on exposed ridges.
- **Prioritizing aesthetics over function** — a messy pile of leaves that retains heat is superior to a neat structure that leaks wind.
- **Wasting daylight on a perfect shelter** — start early. A functional shelter at dusk beats a half-finished masterpiece at dark.
- **Ignoring wind direction** — an opening facing the wind funnels cold air directly onto you.
- **Forgetting a door plug** — an open entrance in a debris hut loses most of the shelter's heat-retaining benefit.
- **Building too close to fire** — debris shelters are tinder. One spark destroys hours of work and your shelter.
- **Sweating during construction** — wet clothing in a cold shelter is a hypothermia recipe. Pace yourself and remove layers while working.
- **Not testing insulation depth** — use the arm test. If your arm pushes through the debris and you see light inside, add more.
