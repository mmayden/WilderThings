# Long-Term Survival Structures

> Transition from emergency shelter to semi-permanent structures that sustain life for weeks or months.

## At a Glance

- Transition to long-term shelter after immediate survival needs (water, fire, food procurement) are stable — typically day 3–7.
- Log cabin, A-frame, and raised platform designs each suit different terrain and climate conditions.
- Invest heavily in roofing and drainage — a leaking roof negates weeks of construction effort.
- Build smaller than you think. A 6 × 8 foot (1.8 × 2.4 m) interior handles sleeping, fire tending, and gear storage.
- Plan ventilation from the start. Smoke inhalation in an enclosed shelter is lethal.

## When to Transition from Emergency Shelter

Emergency shelters (debris huts, tarps, snow caves) keep you alive short-term. They are cramped, poorly ventilated, and degrade quickly. Transition to a long-term structure when:

1. You have a reliable water source within 200 yards (180 m).
2. Fire-making is consistent and fuel supply is sustainable.
3. Food procurement (trapping, foraging, fishing) is underway.
4. Rescue is unlikely within the next week.
5. You have a cutting tool — a knife at minimum, an axe or hatchet ideally.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not abandon a functional emergency shelter to start building. Maintain your current shelter while constructing the new one. Sleep in the old shelter until the new roof is weatherproof.

## Site Selection for Semi-Permanent Camps

All criteria from [Shelter Principles](./shelter-principles.md) apply, plus:

- **Proximity to resources.** Within 200 yards (180 m) of water. Near abundant firewood and building materials.
- **Elevated ground.** At least 10 feet (3 m) above the nearest waterway's high-water mark.
- **Sun exposure.** Southern exposure (Northern Hemisphere) provides passive solar warmth in cold climates. In hot climates, seek shade from the west.
- **Natural windbreak.** Use terrain features — ridgelines block wind; hillsides reflect heat from a fire.
- **Flat building surface.** Level a 10 × 12 foot (3 × 3.7 m) area minimum before construction.

### Drainage and Water Management

Poor drainage destroys shelters. Address it before laying the first log.

1. Dig a **perimeter trench** 6 inches (15 cm) wide and 6 inches (15 cm) deep around the shelter footprint.
2. Route the trench downhill away from the entrance.
3. Crown the interior floor — build the center 2–3 inches (5–7.5 cm) higher than the edges so water flows outward.
4. Never build on clay soil without trenching. Clay holds water and turns the floor to mud.

## Structure Types

=== "Log Cabin"

    Best for: cold climates, forested areas, stays of 1 month or longer.

    ### Foundation

    1. Lay two parallel **sill logs** on level ground, 6–8 feet (1.8–2.4 m) apart.
    2. Use the straightest, most rot-resistant logs available. Diameter: 6–10 inches (15–25 cm).
    3. Flatten the top of each sill log with an axe or knife to create a stable bearing surface.

    ### Wall Construction

    1. Notch each log near the ends using a **saddle notch** — a rounded scoop that cups over the log beneath it.
    2. Alternate butt ends and tip ends at each corner to keep the walls level.
    3. Stack walls to at least 4 feet (1.2 m). Taller walls waste effort unless you need standing room.
    4. Fill gaps between logs with **chinking** — a mix of mud, moss, and dry grass pressed firmly into every crack.

    !!! tip
        Score the inside of each notch with shallow cuts before fitting. This prevents the log from rolling out of position.

    ### Gable and Roof

    1. Build triangular gable ends by stacking progressively shorter logs.
    2. Lay a **ridgepole** across the gable peaks.
    3. Lean roof poles from ridgepole to wall tops, spaced 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) apart.
    4. Apply roofing material (see Roofing section below).

=== "A-Frame Permanent"

    Best for: moderate climates, solo shelters, faster construction.

    1. Set two sturdy uprights into the ground 6–8 feet (1.8–2.4 m) apart, each 5–6 feet (1.5–1.8 m) tall.
    2. Lash a **ridgepole** across the tops. Use square lashing for maximum strength. See [Knots and Lashing](../tools-and-craft/knots-and-lashing.md).
    3. Lean long poles from the ridgepole to the ground on both sides at 50–60 degree angles.
    4. Lash cross-members horizontally across the leaning poles at 12 inch (30 cm) intervals.
    5. Apply roofing layers from the bottom up.

    !!! note
        An A-frame sheds rain well but offers limited interior space. Extend the length rather than the width to add room.

=== "Raised Platform"

    Best for: tropical environments, flood-prone areas, insect and predator protection.

    1. Set four corner posts into the ground 18 inches (45 cm) deep. Use hardwood. Char the buried ends with fire to resist rot.
    2. Platform height: 3–4 feet (0.9–1.2 m) above ground.
    3. Lash horizontal beams between the corner posts at platform height.
    4. Lay cross-poles tightly across the beams to form the floor. Lash each one.
    5. Build a simple A-frame or lean-to roof structure on top of the platform.
    6. Notch a log for a ladder or lash together a simple step.

    !!! danger "WARNING"
        Test every lashing point under load before sleeping on the platform. A collapse from 4 feet (1.2 m) onto uneven ground causes serious injury.

## Wattle-and-Daub Walls

Wattle-and-daub creates solid, windproof, insulating walls for any frame structure.

### Wattle (Framework)

1. Drive **stakes** vertically into the ground at 6 inch (15 cm) intervals along the wall line.
2. Weave flexible branches (willow, hazel, green saplings) horizontally between the stakes, alternating in front and behind each stake.
3. Pack the weave tight. Push each new branch down firmly against the one below.

### Daub (Plaster)

1. Mix **clay-rich soil** with water, dry grass or straw, and animal dung if available.
2. Aim for a consistency like thick oatmeal — it should hold its shape when squeezed.
3. Press the daub into the wattle from both sides simultaneously. Fill every gap.
4. Apply in layers no thicker than 1 inch (2.5 cm). Let each layer dry before adding the next.
5. Total wall thickness: 3–4 inches (7.5–10 cm) minimum.

!!! tip
    Daub cracks as it dries. Mix in more fibrous material (grass, hair, shredded bark) to reduce cracking. Repair cracks immediately with fresh daub.

## Roofing Materials

| Material | Lifespan | Waterproofing | Best Climate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bark sheets | 6–12 months | Excellent | Temperate forest |
| Grass thatch | 3–6 months | Good (if 8+ in / 20+ cm thick) | Temperate, tropical |
| Sod (grass-side up) | 1–2 years | Excellent | Cold, northern |
| Layered leaves | 1–3 months | Fair | Emergency only |

### Application Rules

- **Overlap from bottom to top.** Each layer overlaps the one below by at least one-third. Water runs down, never under.
- **Minimum roof pitch:** 45 degrees. Steeper is better for shedding rain and snow.
- **Bark roofing:** Harvest bark in spring when the sap runs. Flatten sheets under heavy rocks for 2–3 days before installing. Weight them down with cross-poles.
- **Thatch:** Bundle grass into tight handfuls. Tie bundles to roof poles with cordage. Layer 8–12 inches (20–30 cm) thick.

## Insulation for Extended Stays

Ground insulation remains the top priority. For long-term use, improve beyond the emergency minimum.

1. **Raised sleeping platform.** Build a simple frame 6–12 inches (15–30 cm) off the floor. Fill underneath with dry debris. The air gap adds insulation.
2. **Wall insulation.** Pack dry leaves, grass, or moss between inner and outer wall layers. Minimum depth: 4 inches (10 cm).
3. **Banked earth.** Pile soil against the lower 12–18 inches (30–45 cm) of exterior walls. This blocks drafts and adds thermal mass.
4. **Door plug.** Build a frame stuffed with insulation that fits tightly in the entrance. Pull it in behind you at night.

## Ventilation and Smoke Management

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Carbon monoxide from an interior fire is odorless and lethal. Never seal a shelter completely. Always maintain at least two ventilation openings.

### Smoke Hole Design

1. Leave a **gap at the roof peak** — 6–8 inches (15–20 cm) wide — directly above the fire pit.
2. Build a small raised collar of stacked wood or wattle around the smoke hole to prevent rain from dripping in.
3. Create a **removable cover** (bark slab or hide) to close the hole during heavy rain when no fire is burning.

### Cross-Ventilation

- Place a **low vent** near the floor on the wall opposite the entrance — 4 × 4 inches (10 × 10 cm) minimum.
- The entrance acts as the primary intake. The smoke hole and low vent create airflow that pulls smoke upward and out.
- In extreme cold, reduce the low vent size but never block it entirely.

## Door and Entrance Design

- **Face the entrance away from prevailing wind.** This applies to every shelter type.
- **Size:** 2 feet (60 cm) wide, 3 feet (90 cm) tall. Smaller retains more heat. Larger is more convenient.
- **Threshold:** Raise the entrance 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) above exterior ground level to block water runoff.
- **Door construction:** Lash a frame of straight sticks. Fill with woven branches or bark. Hinge with cordage loops at the top, or lean it in place.

## Interior Layout

Organize the interior for efficiency and safety.

1. **Fire pit** — centered or slightly toward the entrance, away from walls. Line with rocks. See [Fire Types](../fire/fire-types.md) for appropriate indoor fire configurations.
2. **Sleeping area** — opposite the entrance, farthest from drafts. Elevate on a platform if possible.
3. **Gear storage** — along one wall on a raised shelf or hung from pegs driven into wall logs.
4. **Drying rack** — near the fire but not over it. Hang wet clothing and gear to dry between uses.

## Common Mistakes

- **Building too large.** Bigger means colder, harder to heat, and slower to build. Keep it small.
- **Ignoring drainage.** Water pooling inside destroys insulation and morale within hours.
- **Sealing the shelter airtight.** Ventilation is not optional. Two openings minimum when burning any fire.
- **Using green logs for the frame.** Green wood warps, shrinks, and opens gaps as it dries. Use standing dead hardwood.
- **Skipping ground insulation.** Even in a well-built cabin, sleeping directly on the ground drains body heat rapidly.
- **Placing the fire pit against a wall.** Minimum clearance: 12 inches (30 cm) from any wall surface. Wattle-and-daub walls are fire-resistant but not fireproof.
- **Neglecting roof maintenance.** Inspect roofing weekly. Patch gaps immediately. Small leaks become big problems fast.
