# Desert Shelter

> How to build shelters in arid environments where shade, heat management, and water conservation are the primary survival concerns.

## At a Glance

- Shade is the single highest priority in desert shelter — direct sun exposure accelerates dehydration and heat illness.
- Build or improve shelter during cool hours only (before 10 AM, after 4 PM). Resting in shade during peak heat conserves up to 1 quart (1 liter) of water per hour compared to working in direct sun.
- Desert temperatures drop 40-60°F (22-33°C) at night. Plan for both extreme heat and near-freezing cold.
- The double-roof principle (two layers with an air gap) reduces radiant heat transfer by up to 40%.
- Dig below grade whenever possible — ground temperature 12 inches (30 cm) down is 20-30°F (11-17°C) cooler than the surface.

## Shelter Priorities in Desert Environments

Rank shelter needs in this order:

1. **Shade** — blocks direct solar radiation, the primary heat source
2. **Insulation from ground** — sand and rock surfaces reach 150-170°F (65-77°C) at midday
3. **Wind protection** — useful during sandstorms, counterproductive during calm heat (you want airflow)
4. **Rain protection** — rare but flash floods occur; avoid washes and low ground

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not build in a dry wash (arroyo/wadi). Flash floods arrive with little warning from storms miles away. Build on elevated ground at least 3 ft (1 m) above the wash floor.

## Timing

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Physical labor in direct desert sun between 10 AM and 4 PM causes rapid fluid loss — 1-2 quarts (1-2 liters) per hour. Build shelter during cool hours or at night. If caught without shade at midday, sit on elevated insulation, cover exposed skin, and minimize movement until temperatures drop.

Work schedule:

- **Dawn to 10 AM:** Primary construction window.
- **10 AM to 4 PM:** Rest in shade. Minimal activity.
- **4 PM to dark:** Secondary construction or improvement window.
- **Night:** Travel, signal, or continue building if moonlight or headlamp allows.

## Below-Grade Shelters

Digging below ground level exploits cooler subsurface temperatures. Ground temperature stabilizes approximately 20-30°F (11-17°C) below surface temperature at a depth of 12-18 inches (30-45 cm).

### Desert Trench Shelter

1. Select a site with compacted sand or soil (loose dune sand collapses). Avoid rocky ground that cannot be excavated.
2. Dig a trench 3 ft (1 m) wide, 7-8 ft (2-2.5 m) long, and 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) deep.
3. Pile excavated sand along the north side (Northern Hemisphere) to create additional shade during midday.
4. Span the trench with a tarp, poncho, space blanket, or layered vegetation. Elevate the covering 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) above the trench floor.
5. If a second layer of material is available, add it 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) above the first layer to create a double-roof air gap.
6. Anchor the covering edges with sand, rocks, or buried stakes.
7. Leave both narrow ends partially open for ventilation. Seal one end during sandstorms.

### Pit Shelter

For longer stays, dig deeper.

1. Excavate a pit 4 ft (1.2 m) deep, 4 ft (1.2 m) wide, and 7-8 ft (2-2.5 m) long.
2. Shore the walls with rocks, branches, or packs if the soil is loose.
3. Cover with a double-layer roof (see double-roof principle below).
4. Cut a ramp or steps on one short end for entry.
5. Line the floor with a ground cloth, vegetation, or clothing to insulate from ground contact.

!!! tip
    If you are digging anyway and conditions justify a still, put its pit next to the
    trench so the two share one excavation effort. Read the yield warning below first —
    a still is rarely worth digging for on its own.

## Above-Grade Shade Structures

When digging is impractical (rocky terrain, tool shortage), build shade above ground.

### Open Desert Shade

1. Find or place two vertical supports 6-7 ft (1.8-2.1 m) apart. Use trekking poles, branches, vehicle parts, or rock stacks.
2. Stretch a tarp, blanket, or space blanket between the supports at 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) height.
3. Orient the long axis east-west so the shade covers the largest area during midday.
4. Angle the covering to allow airflow underneath. Do not create an enclosed box — trapped air overheats.
5. If material allows, add a second layer 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) above the first.

### Rock Overhang Evaluation

Natural rock overhangs provide immediate shade. Evaluate before using:

- Check for loose rock, cracks, or signs of recent rockfall. Do not shelter under unstable formations.
- Confirm the overhang faces away from prevailing afternoon sun (north-facing in the Northern Hemisphere is ideal).
- Check for animal sign — scorpions, snakes, and rodents shelter under overhangs. Clear the area before settling in.
- Deepen the shade by hanging a tarp or space blanket from the overhang lip.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Rock surfaces store heat and radiate it for hours after sunset. A south-facing rock wall at dusk can radiate 100-120°F (38-49°C). Position your sleeping area away from sun-heated rock faces.

## The Double-Roof Principle

Two layers of material separated by an air gap of 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) reduce radiant heat transfer significantly. The outer layer absorbs solar radiation and heats up. The air gap acts as insulation, preventing that heat from conducting to the inner layer.

- **Outer layer:** Any opaque material — tarp, poncho, space blanket (shiny side up), brush, sand-covered fabric.
- **Air gap:** 4-6 inches (10-15 cm). Maintain with sticks, rocks, or tied separators.
- **Inner layer:** Second tarp, blanket, or vegetation.

A double-roof shelter is 20-40°F (11-22°C) cooler underneath than a single-layer shelter.

!!! tip
    A space blanket with the reflective side facing up as the outer layer reflects up to 90% of radiant heat. Use a second tarp below it as the inner layer.

## Sand Anchoring Techniques

Desert soil and sand do not hold conventional stakes. Use these methods:

- **Deadman anchor:** Tie the guy line to the center of a stick, rock, or stuff sack filled with sand. Bury it 12 inches (30 cm) deep. Pack sand firmly on top.
- **Sand stakes:** Fill stuff sacks, bags, or tied-off pant legs with sand. Bury them as anchors.
- **Rock piles:** Stack rocks over the guy line or tarp edge. Use 15-20 lb (7-9 kg) minimum per anchor point.
- **Trench-and-bury:** Dig a narrow trench, lay the tarp edge in it, and bury with 6 inches (15 cm) of packed sand.

## Sandstorm Windbreak

During a sandstorm (haboob), shelter priorities shift to wind protection and breathing safety.

1. Orient the shelter opening away from the wind direction (downwind).
2. Seal the windward side completely with a tarp, vehicle panel, or banked sand.
3. Leave the leeward side partially open to prevent the shelter from pressurizing and collapsing.
4. Cover your nose and mouth with a damp cloth to filter sand particles.
5. Protect eyes with goggles, sunglasses, or a wrapped cloth with a narrow slit.
6. Seal gear in bags or bury under clothing — wind-driven sand penetrates zippers and pockets.

## Night Cold Management

Desert air temperatures regularly drop to 35-50°F (2-10°C) at night, and below freezing at higher elevations. Sand and rock lose heat rapidly after sunset.

- Close open sides of the shelter after sunset while maintaining ventilation.
- Insulate the floor. Bare sand conducts heat away from the body. Use a pad, vegetation, spare clothing, or a layer of dry brush.
- Heat rocks in a fire during evening hours. Wrap heated rocks in cloth and place them in the shelter as radiant warmers. Avoid rocks from riverbeds — trapped moisture can cause them to explode when heated.
- Wear all available layers. Cover the head — 10% of heat loss occurs from an uncovered head.
- If available, use a space blanket with the reflective side facing inward at night to retain body heat.

!!! note
    The same shelter that keeps you cool during the day must keep you warm at night. Plan for both extremes during construction.

## Water Conservation and Shelter

Shelter choice directly affects water requirements.

| Activity | Water Loss |
|----------|------------|
| Resting in shade | 0.5 qt/hr (0.5 L/hr) |
| Resting in sun | 1 qt/hr (1 L/hr) |
| Working in sun | 1-2 qt/hr (1-2 L/hr) |
| Working in shade | 0.5-1 qt/hr (0.5-1 L/hr) |

Good shelter reduces water needs by 50% or more. Every hour spent in direct sun instead of shade costs an additional 0.5-1 quart (0.5-1 liter) of sweat.

## Solar Still Shelter Combination

A below-grade trench shelter and a solar still share the same construction principles. Build them adjacent to each other to minimize digging effort.

1. Dig the shelter trench first (see Desert Trench Shelter above).
2. Dig a second pit 3 ft (1 m) in diameter and 2 ft (60 cm) deep immediately adjacent to the shelter.
3. Place a collection container at the center bottom of the still pit.
4. Cover the still pit with clear plastic sheeting. Seal edges with sand.
5. Place a small rock at the center of the plastic so it sags into a cone shape directly over the container.
6. Condensation forms on the underside of the plastic and drips into the container.

Adding green vegetation or urine to the still pit increases output substantially — it is
the moisture in the pit, not the sand, that the still is distilling.

!!! danger "DANGER: the still can easily cost more water than it produces"
    A ground still yields **0.5-1 quart (0.5-1 L) per day in genuinely moist soil**, and
    close to nothing in dry sand with nothing added. Compare that against the table in
    this guide: digging in the sun costs **1-2 quarts (1-2 L) per hour**. An hour of
    excavation can cost several times what the still returns in a day.

    Build one only when all three hold: the soil is damp or you have vegetation to add,
    you can dig during cool hours, and you have already set up rain and dew collection.
    **A single tarp in one inch of rain yields more than a week of solar stills.**

    Never drink urine directly — it is a moisture source for the still, nothing else. As
    dehydration progresses urine concentrates and accelerates kidney failure.

[Desert and Sea Water](../water/desert-and-sea-water.md) for how the methods compare.

## Site Selection Criteria

Evaluate potential shelter sites against these factors:

- **Elevation:** Build at least 3 ft (1 m) above any wash or drainage. Higher ground catches more breeze.
- **Substrate:** Compacted sand or hardpan digs easier than rocky ground. Loose dune sand collapses and requires shoring.
- **Existing shade:** A rock overhang, vehicle, or large boulder reduces construction effort. Build adjacent to existing shade sources.
- **Wind exposure:** Moderate breeze aids cooling during the day. Excessive wind (sandstorm conditions) requires windbreak construction.
- **Visibility:** If rescue is expected, build where the shelter or signal devices are visible from the air and from likely approach routes.

## Common Mistakes

- **Building during peak heat.** Construction labor in midday sun causes dangerous fluid loss. Build during cool hours only.
- **Single-layer roof.** A single tarp in direct sun provides inadequate heat reduction. Use the double-roof principle.
- **Sealing the shelter completely.** Trapped air overheats. Maintain airflow on calm days.
- **Sheltering in a dry wash.** Flash floods arrive from distant storms without warning. Build on high ground.
- **Ignoring night cold.** Planning only for daytime heat. Desert nights are cold enough for hypothermia, especially with dehydration and fatigue.
- **Sleeping on bare ground.** Sand and rock conduct heat away from the body at night and burn during the day. Always insulate the floor.
- **Using a dark-colored tarp shiny-side down.** Orient reflective surfaces outward during the day to reject solar radiation.
- **Digging a solar still in dry sand.** It returns almost nothing, and the digging costs 1-2 quarts (1-2 L) of sweat per hour. Set up rain and dew collection first.
