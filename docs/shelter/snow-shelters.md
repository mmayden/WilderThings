# Snow Shelters

> How to build emergency and semi-permanent shelters from snow in winter and alpine environments.

## At a Glance

- Snow is an excellent insulator — interior temperatures hold near 32°F (0°C) even when outside air drops to -40°F (-40°C).
- Ventilation holes prevent carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide buildup, which kills silently.
- Insulate the floor with boughs, a foam pad, or a pack — never sleep directly on snow.
- The entrance must sit below the sleeping platform so cold air sinks away from you.
- Choose your shelter type based on snow conditions, available time, and group size.

## When to Choose Which Type

| Shelter | Snow Needed | Build Time | Best For |
|---------|-------------|------------|----------|
| Snow trench | Any packable snow, 3 ft (1 m) deep | 30-60 min | Solo, fastest emergency option |
| Quinzhee | Any snow, even powder | 3-5 hours (includes settling) | 1-3 people, most snow types |
| Snow cave | Deep consolidated snowbank or drift, 8+ ft (2.5+ m) | 2-4 hours | 1-4 people, existing drifts |
| Igloo | Wind-packed slab snow (sintered) | 2-4 hours (experienced builder) | Groups, long-term camps |

## Assessing Snow Conditions

Before choosing a shelter type, evaluate the snow:

- **Grab test:** Squeeze a handful. If it holds shape, it is packable and suitable for a quinzhee or trench. If it crumbles to powder, pile and wait longer for sintering.
- **Step test:** Step firmly onto the snow surface. If your boot sinks less than 1 inch (2.5 cm), the snow is wind-packed slab — suitable for igloo blocks. If you sink to mid-shin or deeper, the snow is unconsolidated.
- **Probe test:** Push a ski pole or long stick straight down. Measure total depth. You need at least 8 ft (2.5 m) of consolidated snow for a snow cave.
- **Temperature check:** Snow shelters work best below 25°F (-4°C). Near or above freezing, melt-refreeze cycles weaken walls and cause dripping.

## Snow Trench

The fastest emergency snow shelter. Build this when time is critical and hypothermia is approaching.

### Construction

1. Stomp out a trench 3 ft (1 m) wide, 8 ft (2.5 m) long, and 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) deep.
2. Cut snow blocks from the trench floor or nearby snowpack. Each block should be roughly 18 x 12 x 6 inches (45 x 30 x 15 cm).
3. Lean blocks against each other across the top of the trench in an A-frame pattern. Alternatively, lay branches, a tarp, or a poncho across the top and pile snow over it.
4. Leave a gap at one end for entry. Plug it with a pack or snow block after entering.
5. Poke a ventilation hole through the roof with a ski pole, stick, or fist. Diameter: 2 inches (5 cm) minimum.
6. Line the floor with boughs, a sleeping pad, or extra clothing.

!!! tip
    A snow trench takes 30-60 minutes to build. Prioritize this shelter when you have less than two hours of daylight remaining.

## Quinzhee

A pile-and-hollow shelter that works with any type of snow, including powder. This is the most versatile snow shelter.

### Construction

1. Pile snow into a mound 7-8 ft (2-2.5 m) high and 10-12 ft (3-3.5 m) in diameter at the base. Use a shovel, snowshoes, a pot, or your hands.
2. Pack the outside surface lightly by patting with the flat of a shovel or gloved hands.
3. Insert 15-20 sticks or poles into the mound, each 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) long. Push them perpendicular to the surface at even spacing. These are thickness gauges.
4. **Wait 1.5-2 hours** for the snow to sinter (recrystallize and bond). This step is not optional — skip it and the roof collapses.
5. Dig an entrance tunnel low on the downwind side. Angle it downward, then up into the mound. The tunnel floor must sit below the interior sleeping platform.
6. Hollow out the interior. When you hit the inner end of a guide stick, stop digging in that direction. Maintain 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) of wall thickness everywhere.
7. Carve a flat sleeping platform at least 12 inches (30 cm) above the entrance tunnel floor.
8. Smooth the interior ceiling into a dome shape. Eliminate flat spots and protrusions — these create drip points.
9. Poke two ventilation holes through the upper walls at 45-degree angles. Diameter: 2 inches (5 cm) each. Place one on each side.
10. Glaze the interior walls by lighting a candle or small fire briefly. The slight melt refreezes into an ice layer that strengthens the structure and reduces dripping.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never seal the entrance completely. Carbon dioxide accumulates fast in an enclosed snow shelter. Maintain ventilation holes and a partially open entrance at all times.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    If you skip the 1.5-2 hour settling time, the mound will not bond internally. The roof will collapse during hollowing or while you sleep.

### Interior Dimensions

- Ceiling height at center: 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) sitting height
- Floor area for two people: roughly 6 x 7 ft (1.8 x 2.1 m)
- Wall thickness: 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) minimum everywhere

## Snow Cave

Requires an existing deep snowbank or wind-deposited drift at least 8 ft (2.5 m) deep. Commonly found on leeward slopes, road cuts, and below ridgelines.

### Site Selection

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Evaluate avalanche risk before digging into any slope. Avoid slopes between 25-45 degrees with recent snow loading. If the slope has avalanche debris at the base, do not dig there.

Choose a snowbank on a slope of less than 25 degrees or a flat drift. Probe with a pole or ski to confirm depth of at least 8 ft (2.5 m).

### Construction

1. Dig a horizontal entrance tunnel into the snowbank at the lowest practical point. Make the tunnel 3 ft (1 m) wide and 3 ft (1 m) tall — just large enough to crawl through.
2. Angle the tunnel slightly upward after the first 3 ft (1 m).
3. Excavate the main chamber above and behind the tunnel entrance. Carve upward so the sleeping platform sits 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) above the top of the tunnel opening.
4. Shape the ceiling into a smooth dome. Remove any flat spots or lumps that cause drip points.
5. Maintain wall and ceiling thickness of at least 12 inches (30 cm). Push a pole through from outside to check.
6. Carve a shelf for a candle at head height. A single candle raises interior temperature by 2-4°F (1-2°C).
7. Poke two ventilation holes at 45-degree angles through the roof using a ski pole or long stick.
8. Smooth and glaze the interior ceiling to prevent dripping.
9. Place a pack or snow block at the entrance as a partial windbreak. Do not seal it completely.

### Cold Sink Design

The entrance tunnel and sleeping platform create a cold sink. Cold air is denser and pools at the lowest point. Position the sleeping platform above the tunnel so warm air from body heat stays at your level and cold air drains downward into the tunnel.

A well-built snow cave with one or two occupants holds 28-32°F (-2 to 0°C) inside regardless of outside temperature.

## Igloo

Requires wind-packed slab snow (sintered snow that holds its shape when cut). Test by stepping on the snow surface — your boot should leave a print less than 1 inch (2.5 cm) deep.

!!! note
    Igloo construction requires practice. Build at least two practice igloos in non-emergency conditions before relying on this technique in a survival situation.

### Block Cutting

1. Identify a slab snow area. Probe to confirm depth of at least 18 inches (45 cm).
2. Cut blocks using a snow saw, machete, or long-bladed knife. Standard block size: 24 x 16 x 8 inches (60 x 40 x 20 cm).
3. Cut blocks from within the planned floor area — this simultaneously creates the sunken interior.
4. Handle blocks by supporting the bottom. Lift from underneath, not by gripping the sides.

### Spiral Construction

1. Mark a circle on the snow surface: 9 ft (2.7 m) diameter for two people, 12 ft (3.6 m) for four.
2. Place the first row of blocks around the circle perimeter, angling each block inward 10-15 degrees.
3. Cut a spiral ramp into the top of the first row. Start at one block and cut diagonally upward across three to four blocks. This ramp allows continuous spiral placement.
4. Place subsequent blocks along the spiral, increasing the inward lean with each row. Each block leans on the one below it and the one beside it.
5. Trim the inner face of each block to maintain the dome curve. The angle increases as you build higher.
6. Place the capstone (keystone block) at the top. Cut it slightly larger than the opening and lower it in from outside, then trim to fit.
7. Fill all gaps between blocks with loose snow packed firmly.
8. Cut a low entrance below floor level. Dig a short tunnel or simply cut an arched opening.
9. Poke two ventilation holes near the top.

### Build Time

An experienced two-person team completes an igloo in 1.5-2 hours. A first-time builder should expect 3-5 hours. Practice is essential.

## Critical Details for All Snow Shelters

### Ventilation

!!! danger "DANGER"
    Carbon monoxide from stoves, candles, or body metabolism accumulates in sealed snow shelters. Maintain at least two ventilation holes at all times. If you feel drowsy, headachy, or nauseous, open the entrance immediately and get fresh air.

Poke ventilation holes at a 45-degree upward angle to prevent rain or meltwater from dripping in. Check holes every few hours — snowfall and frost can seal them. Keep a stick inside the shelter to re-open blocked holes.

### Keep the Shovel Inside With You

!!! danger "DANGER: you cannot dig out of a snow shelter from the inside without a tool"
    Two things seal people in. A wall or roof collapses onto the occupant, or a storm
    drifts the entrance shut overnight while they sleep. Both happen, and both leave
    someone in the dark, in a confined space, with snow that has set around them.

    Collapsed snow is not loose powder. It packs and locks, and a buried person often
    cannot move their arms enough to dig, let alone push a roof off. Digging out
    barehanded through a drifted entrance takes a long time you may not have if the
    ventilation is also blocked.

    - **Bring the shovel inside every night.** Not left at the entrance, not outside —
      inside, within reach of where you sleep.
    - Keep the stick for clearing vent holes inside too, and check the holes before
      sleeping and whenever you wake.
    - **Do not dig a cave or hollow a quinzhee alone if anyone else is available.**
      Someone outside who can dig is the difference between a collapse being an
      inconvenience and being fatal.
    - If you must work alone, dig with your feet toward the entrance so a collapse leaves
      you facing the way out, and stop to clear the tunnel as you go.

!!! warning "CAUTION: glazing the walls stops them breathing"
    Snow is porous, and an unglazed shelter exchanges a small amount of air through the
    walls themselves. Glazing the interior with a candle — which this guide recommends,
    because it strengthens the structure and stops dripping — seals that path. It makes
    the ventilation holes the only air exchange you have.

    Glaze the ceiling if you want, then treat the vent holes as the thing keeping you
    alive rather than a comfort measure.

### Floor Insulation

Never sleep directly on snow. Body heat melts the contact surface, soaking clothing and accelerating heat loss. Layer the floor in this order:

1. Boughs (spruce, fir, pine) — 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) thick
2. Sleeping pad or pack frame
3. Sleeping bag or emergency blanket

### Interior Temperature Management

- A candle raises interior air temperature by 2-4°F (1-2°C).
- Body heat from two people raises it by 10-15°F (5-8°C).
- If the interior gets above 32°F (0°C), the ceiling begins to melt and drip. Open a ventilation hole wider or extinguish the candle.
- Glaze the ceiling by briefly warming it with a candle after construction. The melted surface refreezes into a smooth ice layer that sheds water to the walls instead of dripping.

### Entrance Design

- Orient the entrance away from prevailing wind (downwind side).
- The entrance floor must be lower than the sleeping platform by at least 12 inches (30 cm).
- Use a pack, snow block, or jacket as a partial door. Never seal it fully.
- A right-angle turn in the entrance tunnel blocks wind more effectively than a straight tunnel.

### Marking Your Shelter

Mark the shelter exterior with a bright-colored item — a stuff sack, flagging tape, or ski pole. Snow shelters are invisible from a distance and easy to miss, including by search-and-rescue teams.

### Wet Clothing Management

!!! warning "CAUTION: you will get wet building this"
    Digging snow is hard work in contact with snow, and it soaks you from both sides —
    sweat inside, melt outside. Finishing a good shelter in wet clothing produces the
    hypothermia the shelter was built to prevent.

    Strip to a light layer plus a shell before you start, accept being cold while
    working, and put dry layers on the moment you stop. If you have a spare dry base
    layer, keep it dry for sleeping and change into it inside.

Remove wet outer layers before entering the shelter. Brush off all snow from clothing and boots at the entrance — snow tracked inside melts and soaks gear. Hang damp items from sticks pushed into the walls near the ceiling where warm air collects. Do not sleep in wet clothing; strip to dry base layers inside the sleeping bag and use body heat to dry damp socks and gloves overnight.

## Common Mistakes

- **Skipping the settling time on a quinzhee.** Unsettled snow collapses during hollowing or overnight. Wait the full 1.5-2 hours.
- **Sealing the entrance for warmth.** Blocking airflow causes CO2 buildup. Leave ventilation open even if it feels cold.
- **Sleeping on bare snow.** Direct contact with snow drains body heat faster than cold air. Always insulate the floor.
- **Making walls too thin.** Walls under 12 inches (30 cm) collapse under their own weight or from external loading. Use guide sticks.
- **Building on an avalanche-prone slope.** Check slope angle and recent snow conditions before digging into any hillside.
- **Flat ceiling surfaces.** Flat spots collect meltwater and drip. Dome the ceiling so meltwater runs down the walls.
- **Entrance at the same level as the sleeping platform.** Eliminates the cold sink effect. Dig the entrance lower.
- **Leaving the shovel outside.** A collapse or an overnight drift seals you in, and packed snow cannot be dug barehanded. Sleep with it.
- **Hollowing a quinzhee or cave alone when someone could be spotting.** A collapse with nobody outside is how snow shelter deaths happen.
- **Sweating through the build.** Wet clothing in a snow shelter defeats the entire point of building it.
