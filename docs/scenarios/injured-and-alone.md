# Injured and Alone
> Self-assessment, self-treatment, and self-rescue protocols when you are hurt in the wilderness with no one to help.

## At a Glance
- Run the ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) on yourself before doing anything else.
- Stop catastrophic bleeding first — everything else is secondary.
- A one-handed tourniquet can save your life; practice the technique before you need it.
- The decision to self-rescue vs stay and signal depends on injury severity, mobility, and rescue probability.
- Your greatest enemy is not the injury. It is the psychological spiral that follows it.

---

## Self-Assessment: The First 60 Seconds

Adrenaline masks pain. You may not realize the extent of your injuries immediately. Force yourself through this checklist before acting.

### The ABCs

**A — Airway**
- Can you breathe freely through your nose and mouth?
- Is there blood, vomit, or debris in your mouth? Clear it.
- If your jaw is broken, prop yourself on your side so fluids drain out.

**B — Breathing**
- Take a deep breath. Is it painful? Pain on one side may indicate broken ribs or a pneumothorax (collapsed lung).
- Count your breaths for 15 seconds and multiply by four. Normal: 12-20 per minute. Above 24 suggests shock, pain, or chest injury.
- If you hear a sucking sound from a chest wound, seal it immediately with any airtight material (plastic wrapper, duct tape, a gloved hand). Tape three sides so trapped air can escape, or all four if you have a vented commercial seal.
- **If breathing gets worse after sealing it, lift one corner of the seal to let air out, then reseal.** Air can build up in the chest and crush the lung — this "burping" is the only thing you can do about it alone, and improvised tape seals fail often enough that you should expect to need it.

**C — Circulation**
- Check for bleeding. Run your hands over your entire body — head, neck, armpits, chest, abdomen, groin, thighs, back. Look at your hands after each area.
- Life-threatening bleeds feel warm, wet, and fast. Arterial blood is bright red and spurts.
- Check your pulse at the wrist. If you cannot find it, check the neck (carotid). A rapid, weak pulse indicates blood loss or shock.

### Full Body Survey
After ABCs, systematically check:
- **Head:** Feel the skull for depressions, swelling, or boggy spots. Check ears and nose for clear fluid (cerebrospinal fluid leak = skull fracture).
- **Neck and spine:** Can you move your head without electric/shooting pain down your arms or legs? If not, minimize spinal movement.
- **Shoulders and arms:** Squeeze each arm, check range of motion.
- **Chest and ribs:** Press gently. Instability or grinding (crepitus) = fracture.
- **Abdomen:** Press gently in all four quadrants. Rigidity or guarding = internal injury. You cannot treat this in the field — prioritize evacuation.
- **Pelvis:** Press inward on both hip bones. Instability = pelvic fracture. Do not repeat this test — you can worsen bleeding.
- **Legs:** Squeeze each leg from hip to ankle. Check ankles and feet for sensation and movement.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Abdominal rigidity, a pelvic fracture, or signs of significant internal bleeding (rapid pulse, cold skin, confusion without external blood loss) mean you must signal for rescue immediately. These injuries cannot be self-treated.

## Stopping Catastrophic Bleeding

### Direct Pressure
1. Place the cleanest cloth available directly on the wound.
2. Press hard — harder than feels comfortable. Use your body weight.
3. Hold for a minimum of 10 minutes without checking. Lifting to look resets clotting.
4. If blood soaks through, add more material on top. Do not remove the first layer.

### Wound Packing
For deep wounds (stab, gunshot, gaping laceration):
1. Push clean cloth directly into the wound cavity with your fingers.
2. Pack tightly, filling the entire space.
3. Apply direct pressure on top of the packing.

### One-Handed Tourniquet Application
If you are bleeding heavily from a limb and your other arm is functional:

**With a manufactured tourniquet (CAT, SOFTT-W):**
1. Slide the tourniquet onto the injured limb, 2-3 in (5-8 cm) above the wound. Never place over a joint.
2. Pull the free end through the buckle with your teeth and free hand.
3. Twist the windlass until bleeding stops.
4. Secure the windlass in the clip.
5. Note the time. Write it on the tourniquet or on your skin with any marking implement.

**Improvised one-handed tourniquet:**
1. Use a belt, strip of fabric (at least 1.5 in / 4 cm wide), or a [triangular bandage folded](../tools-and-craft/cordage.md).
2. Wrap it around the limb above the wound.
3. Tie a half knot. Place a stick, pen, or sturdy twig on the knot. Tie a full knot over the stick.
4. Twist the stick until bleeding stops. Secure the stick against the limb by tying or taping it in place.

!!! danger "DANGER: never loosen a tourniquet that has been on for hours"
    A tourniquet causes severe pain. That is expected and is not a reason to release it.

    Releasing a tourniquet that has been on for hours dumps the breakdown products of
    dying muscle into your circulation at once. That reperfusion injury can stop your
    heart — people die that way *after* surviving the bleed. Past roughly six hours,
    releasing it is more dangerous than leaving it on. Timing detail in
    [First Aid Basics](../medical/first-aid-basics.md#how-long-a-tourniquet-can-stay-on).

    Alone, you cannot manage any of that safely. **Write the time on your skin, leave
    it alone, and tell whoever reaches you exactly when it went on.** Losing a limb is
    survivable; bleeding out and reperfusion arrest are not.

## Self-Splinting

### General Principles
- Immobilize the joint above and below the fracture.
- Pad between the splint and skin to prevent pressure sores.
- Check circulation below the splint after application: feel for a pulse, check skin color (pink = good, blue/white = too tight), and test sensation.

### Arm or Wrist Fracture
1. Find two rigid objects (sticks at least 12 in / 30 cm long, trekking pole sections, rolled magazine or bark).
2. Place one on each side of the break.
3. Pad with cloth, moss, or clothing.
4. Lash in place above and below the fracture using strips of cloth, paracord, or [natural cordage](../tools-and-craft/cordage.md). Use a [clove hitch](../tools-and-craft/knots-and-lashing.md#clove-hitch-quick-attach) to start each lashing.
5. Fashion a sling from a shirt, bandana, or belt to support the arm against your chest.

### Lower Leg Fracture
1. Straighten the leg gently by pulling traction from the ankle (pull along the length of the leg, not upward).
2. Place a rigid splint on the outside of the leg from hip to past the foot.
3. Place a second splint on the inside from groin to past the foot if material is available.
4. Pad the ankle, knee, and groin.
5. Lash at the ankle, below the knee, above the knee, and at the hip.

### Improvised Splint Materials
- Trekking poles (adjustable length, rigid)
- Branches stripped of nubs (at least thumb-thickness)
- SAM splint if in your kit (moldable aluminum)
- Rolled bark stripped from a dead tree
- Foam sleeping pad rolled and taped
- Your own body: splint a broken finger to the adjacent finger, a broken leg to the other leg

### Ankle Sprain or Fracture
1. Do not remove the boot if the ankle is swelling — the boot acts as a compression splint.
2. Lash a figure-eight pattern of cloth or cordage around the boot and ankle for additional stability.
3. If the boot is already off, wrap the ankle in cloth and splint with sticks on both sides.

## Treating Your Own Wounds

### Wound Irrigation
- Use the cleanest water available in the highest volume you can manage.
- Punch a small hole in a water bottle cap and squeeze — this creates adequate irrigation pressure (approximately 8 psi).
- Flush the wound from the cleanest edge outward, washing debris away.
- Use at least 500 ml (1 pint) per wound.

### Wound Closure
- Close clean, straight cuts with butterfly closures (adhesive strips) or improvised tape strips.
- Do **not** close dirty wounds, bite wounds, or punctures — these must drain to prevent infection.
- Steri-strips: apply perpendicular to the wound edges, pulling the skin together. Alternate sides for even tension.

### Burns
- Cool with clean water for 10 minutes.
- Do not pop blisters — intact skin is a sterile barrier.
- Cover with a clean, non-adhesive dressing.
- Burns larger than your palm (approximately 1% body surface) or on the face, hands, feet, or genitals require evacuation.

### Infection Signs (Monitor Continuously)
- Increasing pain after the first 24 hours (pain should be decreasing, not increasing).
- Spreading redness beyond the wound edge.
- Red streaks extending from the wound toward the heart (lymphangitis — this is urgent).
- Pus (yellow/green discharge).
- Fever, chills, or malaise.
- Foul smell.

## Pain Management Without Medication

### Breathing Techniques
- **Box breathing:** Inhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds. Repeat for 5 minutes. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system and measurably reduces pain perception.
- **Slow exhale:** Inhale for 4 seconds, exhale for 8 seconds. The extended exhale triggers a relaxation response.

### Cold Application
- Apply cold water, snow wrapped in cloth, or a cool mud pack to injuries with swelling. Apply for 15-20 minutes, then leave it off for at least 40 minutes before repeating.
- Do not apply ice or snow directly to skin — frostbite compounds the injury.

### Positioning
- Elevate injured limbs above heart level to reduce swelling and throbbing pain.
- For rib fractures, sit upright leaning slightly toward the injured side. This limits chest wall movement on the broken side.

### Distraction and Mental Techniques
- Count objects in your environment. Name every tree species you can see. Recite song lyrics. Mental engagement reduces pain signal processing.
- Visualize the pain as a color or shape. Mentally shrink it. This technique has clinical support in pain management literature.
- Set small, timed goals: "I will manage this for the next 10 minutes." Repeat.

### Natural Analgesics (Use with Caution)
- Willow bark contains salicin (related to aspirin). Chew the inner bark of willow species. Provides mild anti-inflammatory and analgesic effect. Do not use if allergic to aspirin or if you have a bleeding wound (salicin has a mild blood-thinning effect).
- Do not eat unknown plants in hopes of pain relief.

## Mobility with Injuries

### Improvised Crutch
1. Find a Y-shaped branch (forked at the top) approximately armpit height.
2. Pad the fork with cloth or moss to prevent armpit nerve damage.
3. Trim nubs and rough bark from the shaft.
4. Test weight-bearing before trusting it fully.
5. Walk with the crutch on the opposite side of the injury: if your right leg is hurt, the crutch goes under your left arm.

### Walking with a Leg Splint
- Use the crutch or two trekking poles.
- Take short steps. Place the crutch/poles first, then swing the injured leg through, then step with the good leg.
- On downhill terrain, lower the crutch/poles first, then step down with the good leg, then ease the injured leg down.

### Crawling Techniques
When you cannot walk at all:
- **Army crawl:** Lie prone, pull with forearms, push with the good leg. Slow but effective over flat or slightly downhill terrain.
- **Seated scoot:** Sit on your backside, push with arms and the good leg. Works on moderate terrain.
- **Log roll:** For short, flat distances or to reach a specific point. Protect the injured limb by holding it against your body.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Crawling is exhausting. If you must crawl, do so toward a signaling position (clearing, ridgetop, road) rather than attempting to crawl out of the wilderness. A 1 mile (1.6 km) crawl on rough terrain can take 8-12 hours.

## Self-Rescue vs Stay-and-Signal Decision

### Stay and Signal if:
- Someone knows your location and expected return.
- You cannot walk or can only crawl.
- You have shelter, water access, and signaling capability at your current position.
- The terrain between you and help is technical, steep, or hazardous.
- The weather is deteriorating.

### Attempt Self-Rescue if:
- No one knows where you are.
- You can walk (even slowly) and know the direction to a road or trailhead.
- Your current position is exposed to elements with no way to improve it.
- You are confident the route is manageable with your injuries.
- Remaining in place will worsen your condition (e.g., rising water, extreme cold with no shelter materials).

### If You Move
- Mark your starting position with a note, ground-to-air symbol, or trail marker.
- Travel during the safest hours (daylight, moderate temperatures).
- Rest frequently — your calorie burn is massively elevated when injured.
- Reassess at every rest stop: is continuing smarter than stopping here?
- See [Lost in the Woods — Navigation](lost-in-woods.md#navigation-if-you-decide-to-move) for route-finding techniques.

## Psychological Challenge of Solo Injury

Being injured alone is the most psychologically demanding survival scenario. The combination of pain, helplessness, and fear of death creates a powerful downward spiral.

### Immediate Psychological First Aid
1. **Breathe.** Pain and fear make you hyperventilate. Force slow, deep breaths.
2. **Talk out loud.** Narrate your actions: "I am checking my bleeding. I am applying pressure." Verbal processing keeps your prefrontal cortex (logical brain) engaged over your amygdala (panic center).
3. **Triage your emotions.** Acknowledge fear. Say it: "I am scared." Then set it aside and focus on the next physical task.

### Ongoing Mental Discipline
- Assign yourself a routine: check wound, gather firewood, improve shelter, signal. Repeat. Routine creates normalcy.
- Set micro-goals measured in minutes, not miles or days.
- Do not make permanent decisions based on temporary despair. "I can't do this" is always temporary if you are still breathing.
- Anger is a useful emotion in survival. Channel it toward survival tasks, not toward self-blame.
- If you feel yourself giving up, focus on one person you refuse to let down.

## Prioritization When You Cannot Do Everything

If your injuries limit you to only a few actions, prioritize in this order:

1. **Stop life-threatening bleeding.** Nothing else matters if you bleed out.
2. **Protect your airway and breathing.** Position yourself so you will not choke if you lose consciousness.
3. **Get out of immediate environmental danger** (water, lightning, rockfall).
4. **Signal for rescue** — whistle, mirror, fire, ground-to-air symbol.
5. **Insulate from the ground.** Pull leaves, branches, or gear under you.
6. **Water** — only after the above are handled.
7. **Splint and stabilize injuries** to prevent further damage.
8. **Everything else** (shelter improvement, food) is bonus.

## Common Mistakes
- Failing to check for bleeding due to adrenaline masking the wound.
- Removing a boot from a swelling ankle (the boot is your best splint).
- Loosening a tourniquet because of pain.
- Attempting to relocate (reduce) a dislocation without training.
- Moving with a suspected spinal injury unnecessarily.
- Closing a dirty wound (traps bacteria, causes abscess).
- Refusing to rest ("I'll push through it") and collapsing in a worse location.
- Crawling for miles when staying put and signaling would bring faster rescue.
- Waiting too long to apply a tourniquet during arterial bleeding.

## Quick Reference

| Injury Type             | Immediate Action                        | Key Rule                              |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Arterial bleeding       | Tourniquet or direct pressure           | Tourniquet pain is expected; do not release |
| Broken limb             | Splint in place, immobilize two joints  | Check circulation below the splint    |
| Chest wound (sucking)   | Seal it; leave one side open            | If breathing worsens, burp the seal    |
| Snake bite              | Immobilize, below heart level           | Do not cut, suck, or tourniquet       |
| Spinal injury suspected | Minimize movement, signal for rescue    | Do not move unless immediate danger   |
| Burn                    | Cool water 10 min, do not pop blisters  | Cover with non-adhesive dressing      |
| Head injury             | Monitor consciousness level, rest       | Evacuate if confused, vomiting, unequal pupils |
