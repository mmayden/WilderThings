# Water Crossing Safety

> How to read a river, choose a crossing site, wade safely, and manage the risks of moving through water in a survival situation.

## At a Glance
- Most water crossing deaths result from underestimating current speed and depth. If in doubt, do not cross.
- Cross at the widest, shallowest point — not the narrowest. Narrow sections are deep and fast.
- Face upstream and side-step with a sturdy pole for balance. Never cross facing downstream.
- Unbuckle your pack's waist and chest straps before entering water so you can ditch it if you fall.
- Cold water kills in minutes through cold shock and incapacitation, long before hypothermia sets in.

---

## Reading the River

Before approaching the water, observe from a high point.

### Current Speed

- **Slow:** Surface is smooth with minimal ripples. Usually safe to wade if depth allows.
- **Moderate:** Visible ripples and small waves. Crossable with caution and a pole.
- **Fast:** Whitewater, standing waves, visible turbulence. Do not attempt to wade. Find another site.

!!! tip
    Throw a stick into the current. If it moves faster than a walking pace, the water is too fast to wade safely.

### Depth Indicators

- Dark water is deep. Light-colored, visible-bottom water is shallow.
- A smooth V shape pointing downstream indicates a deeper channel between rocks.
- A smooth V shape pointing upstream indicates a rock just below the surface — shallow water.
- Ripples over a wide area often indicate a shallow riffle — a good crossing candidate.

### Hazards

| Hazard | Visual Indicator | Risk |
|--------|-----------------|------|
| Strainers (fallen trees) | Branches in current | Pins you underwater |
| Hydraulics (holes) | Foamy recirculation below drops | Traps and drowns |
| Undercut banks | Dark shadows at water's edge | Traps under rock |
| Sweepers | Low branches over water | Knocks you off feet |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Strainers (submerged trees and debris) are the deadliest river hazard. Water flows through them but your body does not. Avoid any crossing route that passes near strainers.

## Crossing Site Selection

### Ideal Characteristics

1. Wide and shallow — water at knee depth or less, spread across a broad channel.
2. Visible bottom (gravel or sand, not mud).
3. No strainers, fallen trees, or logjams downstream of the crossing.
4. Gradual entry and exit banks (not steep or undercut).
5. A safe "runout" downstream — if you fall, the current carries you to calm, shallow water rather than into rapids, strainers, or cliffs.

### Where NOT to Cross

- Above waterfalls or drops of any size.
- Where the river narrows and deepens.
- Near logjams, bridge debris, or strainers.
- At bends — the outside of a bend is deep and fast with undercut banks.
- During or immediately after heavy rain (water levels rise rapidly and unpredictably).

---

## When NOT to Cross

Do not cross if any of these conditions apply:

- Water is above your thighs.
- Current is fast enough that you cannot maintain footing on the bank.
- Water temperature is near freezing and you have no way to warm up on the far side.
- Downstream hazards (falls, strainers, rapids) exist within 100 yards (90 m).
- You are alone and injured.
- A safer crossing exists within reasonable walking distance upstream or downstream.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    The cost of a failed crossing is enormous: hypothermia, lost gear, drowning. The cost of walking an extra mile to a safer crossing is only time and calories. Choose the longer, safer route.

## Solo Wading Technique

### Preparation

1. **Remove pants and socks** (or wear only quick-dry clothing). Wet heavy clothing increases drag and chills you. Boots stay on for traction.
2. **Unbuckle your pack's waist strap and chest strap.** If you fall, you need to shed the pack instantly. A waterlogged pack pins you face-down.
3. **Secure critical items** (fire kit, knife, phone in waterproof bag) in your pockets or tied to your body — not in the pack.
4. **Find a sturdy pole** — a green branch 6-8 feet (1.8-2.4 m) long, wrist-thick. This is your third point of contact.

### Crossing

1. Face upstream. Plant the pole upstream of your body.
2. Side-step across. Move one foot, then bring the other to meet it. Do not cross your legs.
3. Lean slightly into the current, using the pole for support.
4. Move the pole, then move your feet. Always maintain two points of contact with the bottom.
5. Shuffle feet along the bottom — do not lift them high. Feel for stability before committing weight.
6. Angle your crossing slightly downstream (ferry angle) to reduce the force of current against your body.

!!! note
    If the water reaches above your knees and the current is moderate, do not proceed. Knee-deep fast water exerts approximately 33 lb (15 kg) of force per leg. Thigh-deep water doubles that.

## Group Crossing Methods

### Line Abreast

1. Three or more people link arms or grip each other's pack straps, standing side by side.
2. The strongest member stands upstream, breaking the current for others.
3. Move together, stepping in unison. Communicate verbally: "Step. Step. Step."
4. This method provides the most stability for groups.

### Pivot Method (Two People)

1. Two people face each other, gripping each other's forearms or shoulders.
2. The downstream person braces while the upstream person steps forward.
3. Then the upstream person braces while the downstream person pivots around.
4. Continue alternating, always maintaining mutual support.

### Rope-Assisted Crossing

1. The strongest swimmer crosses first with one end of the rope, using the solo wading technique.
2. Anchor the rope to a tree or solid rock on both banks, angled slightly downstream.
3. Remaining members cross one at a time, holding the rope on the upstream side with both hands.
4. Do not tie yourself to the rope. If you fall, you need to let go and swim — being tied to a fixed rope in current pins you underwater.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never tie a rope around your waist or clip into a fixed line when crossing moving water. If you are swept off your feet, the rope holds you underwater. Hold the rope — do not attach to it.

## Pack Management

- **Unbuckle all straps** before entering water. Waist strap, chest strap, and any compression straps that could snag.
- **Wear the pack high on your shoulders** to keep it above water as long as possible.
- **If you fall,** ditch the pack immediately. Shed it and swim to safety. You can retrieve it downstream or survive without it.
- **Waterproof critical items** in plastic bags or dry sacks inside the pack. Assume the pack will get submerged.

## Cold Water Dangers

Cold water (below 60 degF / 15 degC) is far more dangerous than most people realize. Drowning from cold water incapacitation kills faster than hypothermia.

### Cold Water Immersion Timeline

| Phase | Time | Effect |
|-------|------|--------|
| Cold shock | 0-3 min | Gasping reflex, hyperventilation, cardiac stress. Drowning risk is highest here. |
| Swim failure | 3-30 min | Muscles lose strength and coordination. You cannot swim or grip. |
| Hypothermia | 30+ min | Core temperature drops. Confusion, unconsciousness, cardiac arrest. |

### Mitigation

- Keep your head above water. Cold shock causes involuntary gasping — inhaling water is the primary killer.
- If you fall in, do not try to swim immediately. Focus on controlling your breathing for the first 60 seconds.
- Get out of the water as fast as possible. Even sitting on a rock in cold air is better than remaining in cold water.
- On the far bank, change into dry clothing immediately. Build a fire. Warm your core first (see [Hypothermia and Heatstroke](../medical/hypothermia-and-heatstroke.md)).

## Improvised Flotation

If you must cross deep water, improvise flotation:

- **Pants float:** Tie the legs shut, swing the waist opening through the air to fill with trapped air, and hold the waist underwater. The inflated legs provide buoyancy for 5-10 minutes.
- **Dry bag or stuff sack:** Seal air inside a waterproof bag. Hug it to your chest.
- **Closed-cell foam pad:** Sleeping pad provides significant flotation. Hold it under your chest.
- **Sealed water bottles:** Two 1-liter bottles provide noticeable buoyancy. Tuck them inside your shirt.
- **Log:** A dry log 6 inches (15 cm) in diameter and 4 feet (1.2 m) long supports approximately 50 lb (23 kg).

!!! note
    Improvised flotation aids balance and buoyancy but does not make you safe in fast current. Use flotation only in slow-moving, deep water where wading is impossible.

## Downstream Hazards Assessment

Before crossing, walk downstream and assess what happens if you fall.

- **Good runout:** The current eases into a calm pool or wide, shallow area within 50-100 yards (45-90 m). Falling here is recoverable.
- **Bad runout:** Rapids, strainers, waterfalls, or gorge walls downstream. Falling here may be fatal. Do not cross.

If the downstream assessment is unfavorable, walk upstream until you find a crossing with a safe runout. No crossing is worth a lethal downstream hazard.

## Common Mistakes
- Crossing at the narrowest point (narrow = deep and fast).
- Facing downstream while wading (current pushes your knees from behind, folding you forward).
- Keeping the pack waist strap buckled (traps you face-down if you fall).
- Tying into a fixed rope across the current (holds you underwater if you lose footing).
- Underestimating cold water (cold shock drowns in minutes, not hours).
- Crossing above downstream hazards without checking (falls, strainers, rapids).
- Attempting to cross in water above the thighs in moderate current (force on legs exceeds your ability to resist).
- Crossing during or immediately after rain (water levels rise rapidly and without warning).

## Quick Reference

| Factor | Safe | Dangerous |
|--------|------|-----------|
| Depth | Below knee | Above thigh |
| Current | Walking pace or slower | Faster than walking |
| Bottom | Visible, gravel/sand | Invisible, muddy |
| Downstream | Calm pool | Rapids, strainers, falls |
| Temperature | Above 60 degF (15 degC) | Below 50 degF (10 degC) |
| Width | Wide, spread channel | Narrow, constricted |

**Crossing checklist:**
1. Observe from high ground.
2. Check downstream hazards.
3. Unbuckle all pack straps.
4. Secure critical items on body.
5. Get a sturdy pole.
6. Face upstream, side-step, ferry angle.
