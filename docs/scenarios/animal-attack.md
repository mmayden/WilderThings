# Animal Attack
> Decision flowcharts and survival protocols for encounters with dangerous wildlife, from prevention through post-attack wound care.

## At a Glance
- Identify the animal before reacting — wrong responses get you killed.
- Bear attacks require opposite responses depending on species and motivation.
- Fight back against mountain lions, predatory black bears, and wild boar. Never play dead with these animals.
- Snake bites: immobilize, keep below heart level, evacuate. Do not cut, suck, or tourniquet.
- After any attack, infection is the primary ongoing threat.

## Bear Attack

### Step 1: Identify the Bear

| Feature             | Black Bear                      | Grizzly / Brown Bear                  |
|---------------------|---------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Shoulder hump       | No                              | Yes — prominent muscular hump         |
| Face profile        | Straight from forehead to nose  | Concave (dished)                      |
| Ears                | Tall, pointed                   | Short, rounded                        |
| Claws               | Short, dark, curved (1.5 in / 4 cm) | Long, light, straighter (2-4 in / 5-10 cm) |
| Size                | 200-400 lb (90-180 kg)          | 400-800 lb (180-360 kg)              |

### Step 2: Determine Motivation

**Defensive attack** (most common for grizzlies):
- The bear charges because you surprised it, threatened cubs, or approached a food cache.
- Signs: woofing, jaw-popping, bluff charges, ears back.

**Predatory attack** (rare but lethal):
- The bear stalks you, circles, approaches silently, or follows you.
- The bear is calm, focused, and deliberate.
- More common with black bears than grizzlies.

### Step 3: Respond

**Grizzly — Defensive Attack:**
1. Do not run. Bears sprint at 35 mph (56 km/h). You cannot outrun one.
2. Stand your ground during bluff charges.
3. Deploy bear spray at 20-30 ft (6-9 m) in a slight downward angle.
4. If contact occurs: play dead. Lie face down, hands clasped behind your neck, legs spread to resist being flipped. Keep your pack on for spine protection.
5. Remain motionless until the bear leaves the area. Grizzlies often return to check.

**Black Bear — Defensive Attack:**
1. Stand tall and make yourself look large. Speak in a firm, loud voice.
2. Back away slowly. Do not turn your back.
3. Use bear spray at 20-30 ft (6-9 m).
4. If it bluff-charges, hold your ground and keep shouting.
5. **If it makes contact, fight back — target the nose and eyes.** Never play dead with a black bear regardless of why it attacked. This is the rule that separates black bears from grizzlies, and getting it backwards is fatal.

**Any Bear — Predatory Attack:**
1. Do not play dead. This bear wants to eat you.
2. Face the bear. Make yourself as large as possible.
3. Fight with everything available: rocks, sticks, knife, fists.
4. Target the nose, eyes, and face.
5. Shout aggressively. Do not stop fighting.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If a bear enters your tent at night, this is virtually always predatory. Fight immediately with maximum aggression.

## Mountain Lion

Mountain lions (cougars, pumas) are ambush predators. If one is facing you and has not attacked, you have likely already deterred it by detecting it.

### Response — Always Fight
1. Face the animal. Never turn your back or run — running triggers a chase response.
2. Make yourself appear as large as possible. Raise your arms, open your jacket wide, stand on a rock or log.
3. Shout, throw rocks, use sticks. Be aggressive.
4. If it attacks: fight back with everything. Target eyes, nose, and throat.
5. Use a knife if available (see [Knife Use and Care](../tools-and-craft/knife-use-and-care.md)). Stab the throat, eyes, or base of the skull.
6. Do not play dead. Mountain lions will continue feeding on a passive target.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Children are at highest risk. In mountain lion territory, keep children between adults, not trailing behind.

## Wolf Pack

Unprovoked wolf attacks on humans are extremely rare. Most documented attacks involve habituated wolves or rabid individuals.

### Response
1. Do not run. Wolves are pursuit predators.
2. Make yourself appear large. Group together if with others.
3. Back away slowly while facing the wolves.
4. Maintain aggressive posture: shout, wave arms, throw objects.
5. If attacked, fight back targeting the nose and eyes.
6. Climb a tree if possible — wolves cannot climb. Get at least 12 ft (3.7 m) up.
7. Build or maintain a fire — wolves generally avoid fire.

## Moose

Moose injure more people than bears and wolves combined in North America. A bull moose weighs up to 1,500 lb (680 kg) and can kick in any direction.

### Signs of Aggression
- Ears pinned back.
- Hair raised on the hump (hackles).
- Licking lips.
- Lowering the head.

### Response
1. Give moose at least 75 ft (23 m) of space — more if a cow has a calf. They have a larger personal-space threshold than bears.
2. If it charges: **run.** Unlike bears and cats, moose often do not pursue far. They want you away from them.
3. Put a large object between you and the moose: a tree, a car, a boulder.
4. If knocked down, curl into a ball, protect your head and neck with your arms, and stay down until the moose leaves. Getting up triggers repeated stomping.
5. Do not approach a cow with calves — this is the most dangerous moose encounter.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Moose are most aggressive during rut (September-October) and calving season (May-June). Give extra distance during these periods.

## Wild Boar

Wild boar (feral hogs) weigh 100-400 lb (45-180 kg) and have razor-sharp tusks. They are fast, aggressive when cornered, and will charge without warning.

### Response
1. Climb. Get off the ground onto a tree, boulder, vehicle, or any elevated surface. Boar cannot climb.
2. If you cannot climb, sidestep the charge — boar run in straight lines and turn poorly.
3. If knocked down, protect your femoral arteries (inner thighs) and abdomen. Curl on your side with knees drawn up.
4. Fight back with a knife, spear, or heavy stick targeting the head and eyes.
5. A boar will usually disengage once it perceives the threat is neutralized. Do not chase it.

## Snake Bite Protocol

### Immediate Actions
1. Move away from the snake to avoid a second bite.
2. Note the snake's appearance (color, pattern, head shape) for identification, but do not attempt to catch or kill it.
3. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing near the bite — swelling begins rapidly.
4. Immobilize the bitten limb. Splint it in a neutral position if possible (see [Injured and Alone](injured-and-alone.md)).
5. Keep the bite at or below heart level.
6. Mark the edge of swelling with a pen or charcoal and note the time. This tracks progression for medical responders.
7. Evacuate to medical care immediately. Antivenom is the only effective treatment for envenomation.

### Do NOT
- Cut the wound.
- Suck the venom (mouth or extractor pump — neither works).
- Apply a tourniquet (traps venom in the limb, causing tissue death).
- Apply ice (increases tissue damage).
- Drink alcohol (accelerates venom absorption).
- Apply electric shock (myth with no medical basis).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    An estimated 20-50% of venomous snake bites are "dry bites" with no venom injected. Evacuate regardless — you cannot determine envenomation status in the field, and coral snake envenomation in particular can be symptom-free for hours.

### Pit Viper vs Coral Snake (North America)
- **Pit vipers** (rattlesnake, copperhead, cottonmouth): triangular head, vertical pupils, heat-sensing pits between eye and nostril. Cause immediate local pain and swelling.
- **Coral snakes:** red-yellow-black banding ("red touches yellow, kills a fellow"). **That rhyme works only for North American species** — coral snakes elsewhere, and some harmless mimics, break the pattern entirely. Neurotoxic venom may produce delayed symptoms; seek medical care even if you feel fine. See [Venomous Snakes](../wildlife/venomous-snakes.md).

## Alligator and Crocodile

### Myth Busted: Zigzag Running
The old advice to run in a zigzag pattern is wrong. Alligators and crocodiles are fast in short bursts on land — around 11 mph (18 km/h) — but cannot sustain it. **Run straight and fast away from the water.** Zigzagging slows you down for no benefit — these animals do not chase prey on land for extended distances.

### If Grabbed
1. Target the eyes — gouge, poke, stab. The eyes are their most sensitive point.
2. Hit the snout and palatal valve (the flap at the back of the throat that prevents water intake). If you can wedge something into the valve, the animal will open its jaws.
3. Do not try to pry the jaws open — the closing force exceeds 2,000 psi, but the opening muscles are weak. A strong person can hold a gator's mouth shut with bare hands, but cannot force it open.
4. If dragged underwater, fight. These animals drown prey. Resistance to the death roll is your only option.

### Prevention
- Never swim, wade, or stand at the water's edge in known alligator/crocodile habitat, especially at dawn, dusk, or night.
- Stay at least 15 ft (4.5 m) from the water's edge.
- Never feed alligators — this eliminates their fear of humans.

## Dog Attack

Domestic and feral dogs account for far more attacks than all wild animals combined.

### Prevention
- Do not run from an aggressive dog.
- Avoid direct eye contact (perceived as a challenge).
- Stand sideways to reduce your profile.
- Speak in a calm, firm voice.

### During an Attack
1. Wrap your non-dominant forearm in a jacket, shirt, or pack strap and offer it as a sacrifice target.
2. When the dog latches on, strike the back of the skull, the nose, or the ribs with your free hand, a rock, or a stick.
3. If knocked down, curl into a ball protecting your neck and face.
4. If you have a knife, target the throat or the base of the skull.
5. A rear chokehold around the dog's neck can cause it to release.

## After the Attack: Wound Care

### Immediate Wound Management
1. Control bleeding with direct pressure. Apply a tourniquet only for life-threatening limb hemorrhage (see [Injured and Alone](injured-and-alone.md)).
2. Irrigate the wound with the cleanest water available — high volume, moderate pressure. Use a water bottle with a small puncture in the cap to create a jet.
3. Remove visible debris (dirt, teeth, quills) with clean fingers or improvised tweezers (two small sticks).
4. Do not close bite wounds with tape or sutures in the field — bite wounds are contaminated and must drain.
5. Cover loosely with the cleanest material available.

### Infection Prevention
- Change dressings at least twice daily.
- Monitor for signs of infection: increasing redness, swelling, warmth, red streaks from the wound, fever, or foul-smelling discharge.
- If you have a first aid kit, apply antibiotic ointment.
- Animal bites carry high infection risk: bacteria from the animal's mouth, soil, and your skin all enter the wound.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    All mammal bites carry rabies risk. Any bite from a bat, raccoon, skunk, fox, or feral dog requires post-exposure prophylaxis as soon as possible. Rabies is 100% fatal once symptoms appear.

    Being late is not a reason to skip it — there is no cutoff after which the treatment
    stops being given. Full detail in
    [Rabies Risk](../wildlife/wolves-and-canids.md#rabies-risk).

### Evacuation Decision
Evacuate immediately if:
- Bleeding cannot be controlled.
- The wound is on the face, neck, or over a joint.
- Signs of envenomation develop (snake, spider).
- Any puncture wound is deeper than 0.5 in (1.3 cm).
- Bone, tendon, or muscle is visible.
- The victim shows signs of shock (pale, cold, rapid pulse, confusion).
- You suspect rabies exposure.

## Common Mistakes
- Running from predators that have chase instincts (bears, cats, wolves, dogs).
- Playing dead with the wrong species (never play dead with a black bear in predatory mode or a mountain lion).
- Attempting to suck venom from a snake bite.
- Running zigzag from alligators/crocodiles.
- Neglecting wound irrigation after the immediate danger has passed.
- Closing bite wounds in the field (traps bacteria).
- Failing to monitor for infection in the hours and days after the attack.
- Approaching a "dead" or injured animal.
