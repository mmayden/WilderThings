# Improvised Tools from Natural Materials

> How to make cutting, scraping, drilling, and digging tools from stone, bone, wood, shell, and antler when manufactured tools are unavailable.

## At a Glance
- A sharp stone flake takes minutes to produce and can cut hide, wood, and cordage.
- Bone awls and needles enable sewing, basket-making, and fish hook construction.
- Hardwood digging sticks and clubs require no tools other than fire and abrasion to shape.
- Hafting (attaching a stone or bone head to a wooden handle) multiplies force and reach.
- Select materials based on your region — not all stone, wood, or bone is equal.

---

## Stone Tools

### Material Selection

Not all stone works for tool-making. Seek fine-grained, glassy, or crypto-crystalline rock that fractures predictably.

| Stone Type | Region | Quality |
|------------|--------|---------|
| Flint/chert | Eastern NA, Europe | Excellent |
| Obsidian | Western NA, volcanic regions | Excellent (extremely sharp, brittle) |
| Quartzite | Widespread | Good (harder to control) |
| Jasper | Widespread | Good |
| Slate | Northern regions | Good for grinding, poor for flaking |

!!! tip
    Test unknown stone by striking two pieces together. Good tool stone produces sharp flakes and a ringing sound. Dull thuds indicate unsuitable material.

### Hammer Stones

1. Find a dense, round or oval river cobble that fits comfortably in your palm — approximately 1-2 lb (0.5-1 kg).
2. Avoid stones with cracks or fracture lines. These shatter under repeated impact.
3. Use the hammer stone to strike flakes from your tool stone, to crack bones for marrow, and to drive wedges.

### Producing a Cutting Edge (Basic Flaking)

1. Select a piece of flint or chert the size of your fist or larger.
2. Hold the core stone firmly in your non-dominant hand, padded with a folded piece of leather, bark, or thick cloth to prevent cuts.
3. Strike the edge of the core at a 45-degree angle with your hammer stone. Aim for a platform (a flat spot near the edge) approximately 0.5 inches (1.3 cm) wide.
4. A sharp flake detaches from the opposite side. This flake is your cutting tool.
5. Use the flake as-is for cutting hide, plant fibers, or meat. The edge is sharper than steel when freshly struck.

!!! danger "DANGER: protect your eyes before you strike the first flake"
    Percussion flaking throws razor-sharp fragments at speed, and they travel toward your
    face. Eye injury is the characteristic knapping injury, and losing an eye in the field
    is not survivable in any practical sense — you lose depth perception for every task
    left, and there is no treatment you can improvise.

    Wear whatever eye protection you have: sunglasses, prescription glasses, goggles from
    a kit. If you have none, angle the core so flakes detach away from you, turn your face
    aside at the moment of impact, and work at arm's length rather than in your lap.

    Also: fresh flakes are sharper than a scalpel and you will not feel the cut
    immediately. Wrap the non-cutting end with bark or leather before use, keep the
    working area clear of flakes you are kneeling on, and gather the debris rather than
    leaving it where you walk barefoot or set down a hand.

### Pressure Flaking (Refining Edges)

1. Use a pointed antler tine, hardwood stick, or thick bone pressed against the tool edge.
2. Apply steady inward and downward pressure to pop small flakes off the edge.
3. Work both sides alternately to create a symmetrical, serrated cutting edge.

## Bone Tools

Bone from large game animals provides material for precision tools that stone cannot match.

### Awls

1. Split a long bone (deer leg bone works well) lengthwise by scoring a line with a sharp stone and striking along it.
2. Select a splinter approximately 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) long.
3. Grind the tip to a sharp point by rubbing it on a rough stone (sandstone or granite) at a 20-degree angle.
4. Use for punching holes in leather, bark, or woven material.

### Needles

1. Start with a thin bone splinter, 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) long.
2. Grind both the tip and the overall shape to a fine, smooth profile.
3. Drill or gouge an eye hole near the blunt end using a sharp stone flake or a thorny point rotated between your fingers.
4. Thread with sinew, plant fiber, or thin cordage (see [Cordage](cordage.md)).

### Scrapers

1. Split a large bone to expose a broad, flat section.
2. Grind one edge to a beveled scraping edge on rough stone.
3. Use for scraping hides, removing bark, and smoothing wooden tool handles.

## Wooden Tools

Wood is the most universally available tool material. Select hardwoods (oak, maple, hickory, mesquite) over softwoods when possible.

### Digging Sticks

1. Cut a straight hardwood branch 3-4 feet (90-120 cm) long, approximately 1.5 inches (4 cm) in diameter.
2. Sharpen one end to a blunt point using a knife or sharp stone.
3. Fire-harden the tip by holding it near hot coals (not in flame) and rotating slowly until the surface darkens and hardens. This takes 5-10 minutes.
4. Use for digging roots, grubs, latrine holes, and fire pits.

### Clubs

1. Select a dense hardwood limb 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) long with a natural thickening or knot at one end.
2. Trim and smooth the handle end for grip.
3. A heavy root ball or burl at the striking end adds mass. If none is available, lash a heavy stone to the end with cordage (see [Knots and Lashing](knots-and-lashing.md)).

### Spears

1. Cut a straight sapling or branch 6-8 feet (1.8-2.4 m) long.
2. Sharpen the tip with a knife or stone flake.
3. Fire-harden the point as described for digging sticks.
4. For a more effective weapon, split the tip 6 inches (15 cm) and wedge it open with a small stone to create a two-pronged fish spear.

### Fire Tongs

1. Split a green branch 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) long partway down the middle, or bend a flexible green stick into a U-shape.
2. Use to move hot coals, heated rocks, and cooking containers without burning your hands.

## Shell and Antler Tools

### Shell Tools

Large freshwater mussel shells and saltwater clam or oyster shells serve as:

- **Scrapers:** The natural edge scrapes hide, fish scales, and bark.
- **Digging tools:** Cup the shell in your palm for scooping soil.
- **Cutting edges:** Break a shell to expose a sharp edge. Fragile but functional for light cutting.

=== "Coastal Regions"
    Abalone, oyster, and large clam shells provide the most durable edges. Abalone shell is particularly hard.

=== "Inland/Freshwater"
    Freshwater mussels are thinner and more fragile. Use for scraping and scooping rather than cutting.

### Antler Tools

Shed antler or antler from harvested game is dense, tough, and shock-resistant.

- **Pressure flakers:** Use a tine tip for refining stone tool edges.
- **Wedges:** Cut a section of antler beam 4-6 inches (10-15 cm) long. Drive it with a hammer stone to split wood.
- **Handles:** Drill or gouge a socket in the antler base to mount a stone blade.

## Hafting Techniques

Hafting attaches a stone, bone, or shell head to a wooden handle. A hafted tool delivers far more force than a hand-held flake.

### Basic Split-Stick Hafting

1. Select a straight hardwood handle 12-18 inches (30-45 cm) long.
2. Split one end 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) deep.
3. Insert the stone or bone tool head into the split.
4. Lash tightly with wet sinew, rawhide, or strong cordage. Wrap in a criss-cross pattern above and below the tool head.
5. Apply pine pitch or tree resin over the lashing to seal and strengthen the joint.

!!! note
    Wet sinew and rawhide shrink as they dry, tightening the binding significantly. Apply them wet and let them dry in place for maximum holding power.

### Groove-and-Lash Hafting

1. Carve a groove around the handle 2-3 inches (5-7.5 cm) from the end.
2. Seat the tool head against the flat end of the handle.
3. Wrap cordage or sinew through the groove and over the tool head in a figure-eight pattern.
4. Seal with pitch.

## Tool Maintenance

- Resharpen stone edges by pressure flaking. Remove dull or crushed sections by popping off small flakes.
- Resharpen bone tools by grinding on rough stone.
- Re-lash hafted tools at the first sign of loosening. A flying tool head is dangerous.
- Store cutting tools in bark sheaths to protect edges and prevent injury.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    A fresh stone flake is as sharp as a scalpel and has no handle, no guard, and no
    predictable edge. Cut **away** from your body and never into your palm, keep your
    other hand behind the blade, and expect the edge to shear unexpectedly — stone
    fails without warning where steel bends. A deep cut in the field is an infection
    risk before it is anything else. See
    [Knife Use and Care](knife-use-and-care.md) for cutting technique.
- Keep bone tools dry when not in use. Prolonged moisture weakens bone.

## Common Mistakes

- **Knapping without eye protection.** Flakes travel toward the face at speed. This is the injury that ends the trip.
- Striking tool stone with another piece of the same material (use a harder hammer stone or softer antler for control).
- Using softwood for digging sticks and clubs (softwood breaks under load).
- Fire-hardening by placing wood directly in flame (this chars and weakens the wood — hold near coals, not in fire).
- Hafting with dry cordage (wet sinew and rawhide shrink-to-fit; dry cordage loosens over time).
- Ignoring edge protection (wrapping cutting edges prevents dulling and accidental cuts).
- Attempting to knap wet or frost-cracked stone (unpredictable fractures and flying shards).
