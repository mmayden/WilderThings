# Cordage
> How to make, test, and use rope and string from natural fibers, clothing, and paracord — the skill that unlocks snares, shelters, and fire.

## At a Glance
- The reverse-wrap two-ply technique works with any fiber and produces strong, reliable cordage.
- Dogbane, stinging nettle, and yucca are among the strongest natural cordage fibers.
- You can make functional cordage from your own clothing in minutes.
- Always test cordage before trusting it with load-bearing or life-safety applications.
- Paracord inner strands provide seven separate lines for fishing, snares, and sewing.

## Natural Fiber Sources by Region

### Temperate / Eastern North America

**Dogbane (Apocynum cannabinum)**
- Habitat: Open fields, roadsides, forest edges. Eastern and central North America.
- Identification: Opposite leaves, milky sap, red stems in autumn, pods that split to reveal silky seeds.
- Harvest: Best in late autumn after the stalks dry and turn brown. Snap the stalk, peel the outer bark in long strips.
- Fiber quality: Exceptional. One of the strongest North American plant fibers. Comparable to commercial hemp.

**Stinging Nettle (Urtica dioica)**
- Habitat: Moist, rich soil near streams and disturbed ground. Throughout North America, Europe, and Asia.
- Identification: Opposite, toothed leaves covered in stinging hairs. Square stem.
- Harvest: Use gloves or wrap your hands. Cut stalks, crush them flat, and peel the outer fiber from the woody core. Drying neutralizes the sting.
- Fiber quality: Excellent. Long, strong fibers. Historically used for cloth and fishing nets.

**Milkweed (Asclepias syriaca)**
- Habitat: Fields and roadsides. Eastern North America.
- Identification: Opposite leaves, milky sap, large seed pods.
- Harvest: Similar to dogbane — harvest dry stalks, peel outer bark.
- Fiber quality: Good. Not as strong as dogbane but widely available.

**Inner Bark of Basswood / Linden (Tilia americana)**
- Habitat: Deciduous forests. Eastern North America.
- Identification: Heart-shaped leaves, smooth gray bark on young trees, deeply furrowed on old trees.
- Harvest: Cut through outer bark to inner bark (bast). Peel in long strips. Soak in water for several days to separate fibers (retting). In an emergency, use strips directly without retting.
- Fiber quality: Good. Soft, flexible, readily available in large quantities.

**Inner Bark of Elm (Ulmus species)**
- Habitat: Deciduous forests, riparian areas. Eastern North America.
- Identification: Asymmetric leaf bases, rough bark.
- Harvest: Same as basswood — peel inner bark in strips.
- Fiber quality: Very good. Strong and flexible. Slippery elm bark is particularly pliable.

### Western North America

**Yucca (Yucca species)**
- Habitat: Arid and semi-arid regions. Southwestern US, Great Plains.
- Identification: Rosette of stiff, sword-shaped leaves with fibrous edges.
- Harvest: Cut a leaf, pound it with a rock to break the pulp, then scrape pulp away to reveal long, parallel fibers. Alternatively, soak leaves in water for several days until the pulp rots away.
- Fiber quality: Excellent. Strong, durable, and long. Used for thousands of years by indigenous peoples for sandals, nets, and rope.

**Inner Bark of Western Red Cedar (Thuja plicata)**
- Habitat: Pacific Northwest rainforests.
- Identification: Scale-like leaves, fibrous reddish bark.
- Harvest: Peel inner bark in long strips from dead standing trees or fallen logs.
- Fiber quality: Good. Soft and easy to work. Not as strong as yucca or dogbane but extremely abundant.

### Wetland / Riparian

**Cattail (Typha species)**
- Habitat: Marshes, pond edges, ditches. Worldwide.
- Identification: Tall, flat leaves with the distinctive brown seed head.
- Harvest: Use the long, flat leaves. Split them lengthwise for finer cordage.
- Fiber quality: Moderate. Functional for light lashing, but weaker than bast fibers. Weakens significantly when dry. Best used fresh or re-wetted.

## Reverse-Wrap Two-Ply Technique

This is the fundamental cordage-making method. Master this and you can make rope from any fiber source.

### Fiber Preparation
1. Gather a bundle of fibers approximately the thickness of a pencil.
2. Ensure fibers are long — 12 in (30 cm) minimum. Longer is better.
3. If fibers are stiff (yucca, dried bark), soak them in water until pliable. This may take 10 minutes to several hours depending on the material.
4. Rough up the fibers by twisting and rolling them between your hands to separate and soften them.

### Step by Step
1. Take a bundle of fibers and pinch the bundle at a point roughly one-third from one end. You now have a short leg and a long leg.
2. Twist the short leg away from you (clockwise if you are right-handed) between your right thumb and forefinger. Twist tightly until the fiber kinks at the pinch point.
3. Fold the bundle at the kink. You now have two plies hanging down, held together at the top by the kink. The short leg is now slightly shorter than the long leg. This offset is intentional — it ensures splices do not align.
4. Hold the kink between your left thumb and forefinger.
5. **The core motion:**
   - With your right hand, twist the front ply (nearest you) away from you (clockwise).
   - Wrap the twisted front ply toward you (counter-clockwise), crossing it over the back ply. The front ply is now the back ply, and vice versa.
   - Move your left-hand grip up to hold the newly formed twist.
   - Repeat: twist the new front ply away, wrap it toward you over the back ply.
6. Continue this rhythm: twist away, wrap toward. The opposing twist directions lock the plies together.
7. Maintain consistent, firm tension on both plies. Loose twisting produces weak cordage.

### Splicing (Adding New Fiber)
When one ply gets short (2-3 in / 5-8 cm remaining):
1. Lay a new bundle of fiber alongside the short ply, overlapping by at least 3 in (8 cm).
2. Twist the new fiber together with the short ply as if they are one strand.
3. Continue the reverse-wrap as normal.
4. Stagger splices — never splice both plies at the same point. This concentrates weakness.

### Finishing
- Tie an overhand knot at the end to prevent unraveling.
- Alternatively, tuck the tail ends back into the twist for 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm).

## Three-Ply Cordage

Three-ply is stronger than two-ply and creates rounder, more rope-like cordage. Use it when you need strength for [lashing](knots-and-lashing.md) heavy shelter components, crafting a bow drill string, or building snares for larger game.

### Method
1. Start with three bundles of fiber instead of two.
2. Anchor the top ends by tying them together or having a partner hold them.
3. Twist each strand individually in the same direction (clockwise).
4. Wrap the strands around each other in the opposite direction (counter-clockwise).
5. The principle is identical to two-ply: twist and counter-twist lock the structure.
6. Splice each strand independently, staggering the splice points.

## Sinew Preparation

Sinew (tendon) from game animals produces extremely strong cordage for sewing, bow strings, and binding.

### Sourcing
- **Leg tendons:** Found along the back of the lower legs of deer, elk, moose, and similar animals. The Achilles tendon is the largest and easiest to harvest.
- **Back strap sinew:** Runs along both sides of the spine. Longer but thinner fibers.

### Processing
1. Remove the tendon from the animal using a knife. Cut along both sides and peel it free (see [Knife Use and Care](knife-use-and-care.md)).
2. Remove all meat and fat. Scrape clean.
3. Dry the sinew completely. In dry conditions this takes 1-2 days. In humid conditions, dry near a fire (not directly over — heat makes sinew brittle).
4. Once dry, the sinew is hard and board-like. Pound it with a smooth rock to separate the individual fibers.
5. Pull fibers apart by hand into thin strands.
6. To use: moisten the fibers with saliva or water. Wet sinew is pliable and sticky. It shrinks as it dries, creating an incredibly tight bind.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Sinew must be kept dry until use. Prolonged moisture causes it to rot. Store dried sinew in a dry container or wrapped in dry bark.

## Root Cordage

Tree roots provide ready-made cordage that requires minimal processing.

### Best Sources
- **Spruce roots:** The gold standard. Long, flexible, strong. Found just below the soil surface radiating from the tree. Harvest by pulling gently and following the root.
- **Pine roots:** Similar to spruce but stiffer. Soak before use.
- **Willow roots:** Flexible and abundant near water.

### Processing
1. Harvest roots approximately pencil-thickness or thinner.
2. Split thick roots in half lengthwise using your knife or by starting a split and pulling apart.
3. Scrape off the outer bark.
4. Soak in water for 30 minutes to increase flexibility.
5. Use directly for lashing — roots do not need to be twisted into cordage. Their natural structure provides strength.

Spruce roots were historically used by indigenous peoples to sew birch bark canoes. They are waterproof, strong, and flexible enough to stitch through pre-drilled holes.

## Testing Cordage Strength

Never trust cordage for a critical application without testing.

### Pull Test
1. Wrap the cordage around both hands (protect your palms with cloth if the cordage is thin — it can cut skin under tension).
2. Pull steadily, increasing force. Note the load at which it breaks or begins to stretch and deform.
3. A good two-ply reverse-wrap cordage from quality fiber should hold 20-40 lb (9-18 kg) in a thickness of approximately 1/8 in (3 mm).

### Knot Test
1. Tie a simple overhand knot in the cordage and pull tight.
2. If it breaks at the knot, the fibers are too brittle or the twist is too loose.
3. Expect an overhand knot to cost roughly 40-50% of the strength — it is the worst of the common knots for this. Across knots generally the loss is 20-50%, so choose the strongest knot a task allows (see [Knots and Lashing](knots-and-lashing.md)).

### Bend Test
1. Bend the cordage sharply at a 90-degree angle.
2. If fibers crack or snap audibly, the cordage is too dry. Re-wet and re-twist.

### Load-Bearing Rule
For any critical application (supporting human weight, snare trigger under tension, bow drill string), build cordage to 4x the expected load. If you expect a 10 lb (4.5 kg) load, the cordage should hold 40 lb (18 kg) in testing.

## Cordage from Clothing

When natural fibers are unavailable or time is critical, your clothing is an immediate cordage source.

### Spiral Cutting (T-Shirt Yarn)
1. Lay a cotton T-shirt flat.
2. Cut off the hem at the bottom.
3. Starting at one side, cut a strip approximately 1 in (2.5 cm) wide, but stop 1 in (2.5 cm) from the opposite edge. Do not cut all the way through.
4. On the next pass, start from the opposite edge and cut parallel to the first, again stopping 1 in (2.5 cm) from the first edge.
5. Continue alternating, creating a continuous zigzag strip.
6. Stretch the strip — cotton T-shirt material curls into a round cord when stretched.
7. A single T-shirt yields approximately 30-50 ft (9-15 m) of usable cordage.

### Other Clothing Sources
- **Bootlaces:** Immediate cordage. Approximately 4 ft (1.2 m) each.
- **Drawstrings** from jackets, pants, or bags.
- **Seams:** Cut the seam thread from a jacket or pack. Thin but surprisingly strong.
- **Denim strips:** Cut jeans into 0.5 in (1.3 cm) strips. Twist two strips together for stronger cordage.
- **Sock yarn:** Unravel a wool or synthetic sock for fine thread (fishing line, sewing, snare triggers).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not sacrifice insulating clothing for cordage in cold conditions. Hypothermia kills faster than the problems cordage solves. Use non-essential clothing first (extra shirt, bandana, cotton underwear in warm weather).

## Paracord Inner Strands

Type III (550) paracord contains a woven sheath over 7 inner strands, each rated to approximately 35 lb (16 kg).

### Accessing Inner Strands
1. Cut the desired length of paracord.
2. Pull the sheath back from one end to expose the inner strands.
3. Pull each strand free individually.
4. The remaining sheath is still usable as flat webbing or low-strength cordage.

### Applications by Strand Count
| Strands | Approximate Strength | Use                                    |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 1       | 35 lb (16 kg)        | Fishing line, sewing, snare triggers   |
| 2-3     | 70-105 lb (32-48 kg) | Small game snares, gear repair         |
| 7 (all) | 245 lb (111 kg)      | Lashing, shelter guy lines             |
| Full cord | 550 lb (250 kg)   | Load-bearing, climbing assist (not rated for life safety) |

### Tip
Each inner strand can itself be unbraided into two thinner fibers. This gives you up to 14 micro-lines from a single piece of paracord — enough for multiple fishing lines or gill net construction.

## Applications

### Snares
- Use two-ply natural cordage or 2-3 paracord inner strands.
- Form a locking loop (simple overhand knot around the standing end) for a noose.
- Attach to a spring pole or deadweight anchor.
- Cordage for snares must be strong enough to resist the animal's initial thrashing.
- See [Knots and Lashing](knots-and-lashing.md) for the bowline and timber hitch used in snare anchoring.

### Lashing (Shelter Building)
- Use the thickest cordage you can produce, or full paracord.
- Square lashing and diagonal lashing require 10-15 ft (3-4.5 m) of cordage per joint.
- Pre-cut your lashing cordage to length before starting construction. Running out mid-lash wastes time and weakens the joint.
- See [Knots and Lashing](knots-and-lashing.md) for detailed lashing techniques.

### Fishing Line
- Single paracord inner strand or fine two-ply natural cordage.
- Attach an improvised hook (carved bone, thorn, bent safety pin) with a [clinch knot](knots-and-lashing.md) or simple overhand loop.
- For trotlines, run a heavier line across a stream and hang multiple baited hooks on lighter drop lines.

### Bow Drill String
- The bow drill string must withstand extreme friction and tension.
- Three-ply reverse-wrap cordage works best for this application.
- The string must not stretch excessively — overly elastic cordage slips on the spindle.
- Dogbane, nettle, and yucca fiber produce the best bow drill strings.
- Paracord (full, with sheath) works but generates less friction on the spindle. Rough up the section contacting the spindle with a knife.

### Traps and Triggers
- Deadfall trap trigger cords: use fine two-ply cordage, just strong enough to hold the deadfall but sensitive enough to release on contact.
- Toggle triggers: carve a wooden toggle (see [Knife Use and Care](knife-use-and-care.md)) and attach with a figure-eight knot.

### Sewing and Repair
- Single paracord inner strand or sinew for gear repair.
- Use a thorn, bone sliver, or carved hardwood needle.
- Punch holes with your knife tip before sewing — pushing a needle through pack fabric or leather without a pilot hole breaks needles.

## Common Mistakes
- Twisting both plies in the same direction as the wrap (cordage unravels immediately).
- Splicing both plies at the same point (creates a single point of failure).
- Using fibers that are too short (under 6 in / 15 cm) — the cordage will be mostly splice and little strength.
- Not testing cordage before critical use.
- Over-drying fibers to the point of brittleness.
- Using the wrong fiber for the job (cattail for load-bearing, for example).
- Sacrificing warm clothing for cordage in cold weather.
- Forgetting that natural cordage weakens significantly when wet (except sinew, which weakens when wet and strengthens when dried).
