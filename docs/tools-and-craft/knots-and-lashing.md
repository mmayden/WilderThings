# Knots and Lashing
> Eight essential survival knots and two lashing techniques — when to use each, how to tie each, and how to keep your rope alive.

## At a Glance
- Learn the bowline first — it is the single most useful rescue and utility knot.
- Every knot reduces rope strength by 20-50% — a figure-eight costs about 20-25%, a bowline about 30%, a plain overhand closer to 50%. Choose the strongest knot the task allows.
- A knot that cannot be untied when needed is the wrong knot.
- Square lashing and diagonal lashing together can build any shelter frame.
- Inspect rope and cordage before every load-bearing use.

---

## When to Use Each Knot

| Knot             | Use Case                                       | Strength Retention |
|------------------|-------------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| Bowline          | Rescue loop, anchor loop, non-slip fixed loop   | ~60%              |
| Clove hitch      | Quick attachment to pole or stake, starting lashings | ~60-65%       |
| Taut-line hitch  | Adjustable tension (guy lines, ridgelines)       | ~65%              |
| Trucker's hitch  | Mechanical advantage (tarp lines, load securing) | ~50-55%          |
| Figure-eight     | Stopper knot, end-of-rope safety                 | ~75%              |
| Square knot      | Joining two ropes of equal diameter               | ~45%              |
| Sheet bend       | Joining two ropes of different diameters          | ~55%              |
| Timber hitch     | Dragging logs, starting lashings on round objects | ~65%              |

## Bowline — Rescue Loop

Creates a fixed loop that does not slip or tighten under load. The most important knot in survival.

### When to Use
- Lowering or raising a person.
- Creating an anchor point around a tree.
- Making a fixed loop at the end of a line.
- Tying around your waist as an emergency harness.

### Step by Step
1. Hold the standing end (the long, loaded side) in your left hand and the working end (the free tail) in your right.
2. Form a small loop in the standing end approximately 12 in (30 cm) from the tail. The working end crosses over the standing end to form this loop.
3. Pass the working end up through the loop from behind (the "rabbit comes out of the hole").
4. Route the working end behind the standing end ("around the tree").
5. Pass the working end back down through the small loop ("back into the hole").
6. Tighten by pulling the working end and the loop simultaneously while holding the standing end.
7. Dress the knot: ensure all parts lie flat and parallel without crossing over each other.

**Security:** Under intermittent loading, a bowline can work loose. Add a stopper knot (figure-eight or overhand) in the tail, or tie the tail back to the loop with a half hitch.

### One-Handed Bowline (Self-Rescue)
1. Wrap the rope around your waist with the standing end on one side and the tail on the other.
2. With your free hand, grasp the standing end and twist your wrist to form the small loop.
3. Thread the tail up through the loop, around the standing end, and back down.
4. Tighten against your waist.

Practice this before you need it. See [Injured and Alone](../scenarios/injured-and-alone.md) for scenarios requiring one-handed knot work.

## Clove Hitch — Quick Attach

Wraps around a pole or stake and grips under load. Fast to tie and untie.

### When to Use
- Starting and finishing lashings.
- Quick attachment to a tree, pole, or stake.
- Hanging gear from a horizontal pole.
- Not for critical life-safety loads — it can slip on smooth or tapered surfaces.

### Step by Step
1. Pass the working end over the pole.
2. Cross the working end over the standing end, creating an X on the pole.
3. Pass the working end under the pole and tuck it under the X (between the cross and the first wrap).
4. Pull both ends to tighten. The two wraps should be parallel, with the working end exiting between them.

**Quick Method (for open-ended poles):**
1. Form two loops in the rope, both oriented the same direction (like two letter Ds facing the same way).
2. Stack the second loop on top of the first.
3. Slide both loops over the end of the pole and pull tight.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A clove hitch can slip on smooth poles under variable loading. Back it up with a half hitch if the load changes direction.

## Taut-Line Hitch — Adjustable Tension

An adjustable loop knot that slides to tighten but grips under load. Essential for tent and tarp guy lines.

### When to Use
- Guy lines on tarps and shelters.
- Adjustable ridgelines.
- Any line where you need to tension and re-tension without retying.

### Step by Step
1. Pass the working end around a stake or anchor point.
2. Bring the working end alongside the standing end (both running parallel).
3. Wrap the working end around the standing end twice, wrapping inside the loop (toward the anchor). These two wraps must be between the anchor and the knot.
4. Pass the working end over both wraps and make one more wrap around the standing end outside the loop (on the side away from the anchor).
5. Tighten by pulling the working end through and dressing the coils.
6. Slide the knot along the standing end to adjust tension. It grips when loaded in the direction of the standing end.

**Key Detail:** The two inner wraps provide friction. If the knot slips, add a third inner wrap.

---

## Trucker's Hitch — Mechanical Advantage

Creates a 3:1 (theoretical) mechanical advantage for tensioning lines. The most powerful tensioning system achievable with a single rope.

### When to Use
- Tightening a ridgeline for a tarp or shelter.
- Securing loads to a pack frame.
- Tying down gear on a raft or sled.
- Any application requiring extreme tension.

### Step by Step
1. Tie one end of the rope to Anchor Point A (use a bowline or clove hitch).
2. Midway along the rope, form a directional figure-eight loop (or a simple slip loop) to create a fixed point. This loop must be oriented so the loop faces Anchor Point B. The loop acts as a pulley.
3. Pass the working end around Anchor Point B (a tree, stake, or carabiner).
4. Thread the working end back through the midpoint loop.
5. Pull the working end to tension the line. You will feel the mechanical advantage — approximately 3:1 before friction losses.
6. Secure the tension with two half hitches around the standing end below the midpoint loop.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    The trucker's hitch generates significant force. On a tarp or shelter, it is easy to rip grommets or snap cordage. Apply tension gradually.

## Figure-Eight — Stopper Knot

A bulky knot at the end of a rope that prevents the rope from pulling through a hole, grommet, or device.

### When to Use
- Preventing rope from pulling through a tarp grommet.
- End-of-rope safety to stop a rappelling device.
- Base knot for the figure-eight on a bight (a fixed loop alternative to the bowline).

### Step by Step
1. Form a loop by passing the working end over the standing end.
2. Continue the working end under the standing end.
3. Pass the working end through the original loop from the top.
4. Pull tight. The knot resembles the numeral 8.

### Figure-Eight on a Bight (Fixed Loop Variant)
1. Double the rope to form a bight (a U-shaped fold).
2. Treat the doubled rope as a single strand.
3. Tie a figure-eight knot with the doubled strand.
4. The result is a fixed loop that is stronger and more secure than a bowline under ring-loading.

## Square Knot — Joining Equal Ropes

Joins two ropes of equal diameter. Simple and flat, making it useful for bandages and lashing ties.

### When to Use
- Joining two pieces of similar-diameter cordage.
- Tying bandages flat against the body.
- Finishing lashings.
- Not suitable for load-bearing connections between ropes under tension — it can capsize (invert and slip).

### Step by Step
1. Hold one end in each hand: left rope (L) and right rope (R).
2. Cross L over R and tuck L under R. Pull snug.
3. Now cross R over L and tuck R under L. Pull snug.
4. The result: two interlocking loops that lie flat. Both free ends exit on the same side of the knot.

**Verification:** "Right over left, then left over right." If both tails exit on the same side, it is a square knot. If tails exit on opposite sides, it is a granny knot (which slips under load). Retie.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A square knot is not safe for joining ropes under significant or dynamic load. Use a sheet bend or double fisherman's for critical joins.

## Sheet Bend — Joining Different Diameters

Joins two ropes of different diameters securely. Works where a square knot would slip.

### When to Use
- Joining a thin line to a thick one.
- Extending a rope with whatever cordage is available.
- Joining [natural cordage](cordage.md) of varying thickness.
- Attaching a line to a fixed loop or grommet.

### Step by Step
1. Form a bight (U-shape) in the thicker rope. Hold the bight in your left hand.
2. Pass the working end of the thinner rope up through the bight from behind.
3. Route the thin rope around the back of both legs of the bight.
4. Tuck the thin rope under itself (under the thin rope where it entered the bight) but over the bight. The thin rope's working end and standing end should exit on the same side of the bight.
5. Pull all four ends to tighten.

**Double Sheet Bend:** For very slippery or vastly different-diameter ropes, wrap the thin rope around the bight twice before tucking. This doubles the friction and prevents slipping.

## Timber Hitch — Dragging

Grips a cylindrical object (log, bundle) for dragging. Tightens under load, releases instantly when slack.

### When to Use
- Dragging logs for shelter construction or firewood.
- Starting lashings on round poles.
- Hoisting cylindrical objects.
- Bundling sticks for transport.

### Step by Step
1. Pass the working end around the log or bundle.
2. Bring the working end around the standing end.
3. Twist the working end around itself (around the part of the rope touching the log) at least three times. More turns = more grip.
4. Pull the standing end to tighten. The twists lock against the log under tension.
5. For dragging, add a half hitch around the log 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) ahead of the timber hitch to keep the log tracking straight.

## Square Lashing — Right-Angle Joints

Joins two poles at a 90-degree angle. The fundamental structural lashing for shelter building.

### When to Use
- Attaching cross-beams to uprights.
- Building shelter frames, tables, drying racks, and platforms.
- Any right-angle pole connection.

### Step by Step
1. Start with a clove hitch on the vertical pole, just below where the horizontal pole will sit.
2. Place the horizontal pole across the vertical pole.
3. **Wrapping turns:** Bring the rope over the horizontal pole, behind the vertical pole, under the horizontal pole (on the other side), and in front of the vertical pole. You have completed one wrapping turn.
4. Repeat for 3-4 wrapping turns. Keep wraps tight and parallel (no crossing). Pull hard on each turn.
5. **Frapping turns:** Pass the rope between the poles (around the wraps, perpendicular to them) to cinch the lashing tight. Complete 2-3 frapping turns, pulling as tightly as possible on each.
6. Finish with a clove hitch on the most convenient pole.

**Tip:** Alternate the direction of wrapping turns (over-under pattern) to create a woven pattern that grips better than parallel wraps.

### Strength Test
Push and pull on the joint from multiple angles before building on it. A proper square lashing should be rigid with minimal wobble. If it shifts, add more wrapping and frapping turns.

## Diagonal Lashing — Angled Joints

Joins two poles that cross at an angle other than 90 degrees (typically 45-60 degrees). Used for bracing.

### When to Use
- Adding diagonal braces to a square-lashed frame.
- Building an A-frame shelter.
- Any X-shaped cross where poles meet at non-right angles.

### Step by Step
1. Start with a timber hitch wrapped diagonally around both poles at their crossing point. Pull tight to draw the poles together.
2. **Wrapping turns:** Wrap 3-4 turns around one pair of opposite angles (the open space between the poles).
3. Shift direction: wrap 3-4 turns around the other pair of opposite angles (making an X pattern of rope at the crossing).
4. **Frapping turns:** Wrap 2-3 turns between the poles (around the wraps) to cinch everything tight.
5. Finish with a clove hitch.

### When to Use Each Lashing

| Joint Type                        | Lashing            |
|-----------------------------------|--------------------|
| Poles crossing at 90 degrees      | Square lashing     |
| Poles crossing at other angles    | Diagonal lashing   |
| Poles end-to-end (extending)      | Shear lashing      |
| Poles side-by-side (bundling)     | Round lashing      |

## Rope Care

### Inspection
Before any load-bearing use:
- Run the rope through your hands and feel for soft spots, abrasion damage, or lumps (core damage).
- Visually inspect for fraying, cuts, burns, or discoloration (chemical damage, UV degradation).
- Retire any rope with core damage visible through the sheath.

### Storage
- Coil rope when not in use to prevent tangles. Use a mountaineer's coil or butterfly coil.
- Keep rope off the ground when possible — ground contact introduces grit that abrades fibers internally.
- Dry wet rope before storing. Hang it in moving air, not in direct heat (heat damages nylon and dyneema).
- Store away from chemicals, solvents, battery acid, and UV exposure.

### Knot Impact on Rope Life
- Every knot creates a stress concentration. A figure-eight loop retains ~75% of rope strength; a square knot retains ~45%.
- Untie knots after use. Leaving a rope kinked and knotted permanently weakens the fibers at those points.
- If using [natural cordage](cordage.md), test it before every use — natural fibers degrade faster than synthetics, especially when wet and dried repeatedly.

### Field Repair
- Fuse cut ends of nylon or paracord with a flame to prevent unraveling.
- Whip the end of natural fiber rope by tightly wrapping thin cordage around the last 1 in (2.5 cm).
- If a rope is damaged midway, cut out the damaged section and rejoin with a double fisherman's knot or a sheet bend.

## Common Mistakes
- Using a square knot for load-bearing rope joins (it capsizes under asymmetric load).
- Tying a granny knot instead of a square knot (tails exit opposite sides = granny).
- Failing to dress knots (sloppy knots are weak knots).
- Over-tensioning with a trucker's hitch and ripping grommets or snapping cord.
- Using a clove hitch on a tapered or smooth pole without backing it up.
- Not leaving enough tail on the working end (minimum 6 in / 15 cm for safety).
- Storing rope in a tangled pile instead of coiling it.
- Failing to inspect rope before critical use.
