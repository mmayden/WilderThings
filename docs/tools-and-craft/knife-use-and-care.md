# Knife Use and Care
> The survival knife is your single most important tool — learn to choose, use, sharpen, and maintain it.

## At a Glance
- A fixed-blade, full-tang, drop-point knife between 4-6 in (10-15 cm) is the ideal survival blade.
- Batoning is safe and effective when done correctly; incorrect technique breaks knives and fingers.
- Sharpen in the field using river stones, ceramic, or leather stropping.
- A dull knife is more dangerous than a sharp one — it requires more force and slips unpredictably.
- Your knife is a tool, not a pry bar, screwdriver, or throwing weapon.

---

## Fixed Blade vs Folding for Survival

### Fixed Blade — The Clear Winner
- No moving parts to fail under stress.
- Full tang distributes force through the entire handle into the blade.
- Can be batoned (struck with a baton to split wood) without risk of the blade folding on your fingers.
- Easier to clean and maintain in field conditions.
- Faster to deploy in an emergency.

### When a Folder Is Acceptable
- It is the only knife you have. Any knife beats no knife.
- Use a locking folder (liner lock, frame lock, or back lock) — never a slip joint for survival tasks.
- Do not baton a folding knife. The lock mechanism will fail, and the blade will close on your hand.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    A folding knife that closes under force will sever tendons and arteries in your fingers. Never baton, pry, or twist with a folder.

---

## Blade Geometry

### Full Tang
The steel of the blade extends the full length and width of the handle. This is non-negotiable for a survival knife. Partial tang, rat-tail tang, and stick tang knives will snap at the handle junction under heavy use.

### Blade Shape: Drop Point
- The spine curves down to meet the edge at the tip, creating a strong, controllable point.
- Ideal for: carving, batoning, skinning, general cutting.
- Avoid tanto points (weak for carving), clip points (fragile tip), and recurves (difficult to sharpen in the field).

### Grind Types
| Grind         | Cross-Section         | Strengths                          | Weaknesses                       |
|---------------|-----------------------|------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Flat grind    | Wedge from spine to edge | Good slicer, easy to sharpen      | Less durable edge on hard use    |
| Convex grind  | Rounded taper to edge | Strongest edge geometry, excellent for batoning | Requires leather stropping to maintain |
| Scandi grind  | Flat bevel from midpoint to edge | Easiest to sharpen in the field, excellent for wood carving | Thicker behind the edge, poorer slicer |

**Recommendation:** A Scandi grind is the most practical survival grind because you can sharpen it on a flat stone with zero experience. Lay the bevel flat on the stone and stroke — no angle guessing required.

### Steel Selection
- **Carbon steel (1095, 1084, O1):** Easy to sharpen in the field, prone to rust without maintenance. Also works for traditional flint-and-steel, which stainless does not.
- **Stainless steel (440C, S30V, 154CM):** Rust resistant, harder to sharpen.
- For survival: carbon steel is generally preferred, mainly because it is far easier to bring back to an edge with whatever stone you find.

!!! note "Stainless knives strike a ferro rod perfectly well"
    A ferrocerium rod is *scraped*, not struck — sparks come from shaving off the rod
    itself, so what matters is a hard, square-cornered 90-degree spine, not the steel
    the knife is made from. A stainless blade with a sharp spine works fine; a carbon
    blade with a rounded spine does not.

    The distinction that does depend on steel type is **traditional flint and steel**,
    where the sparks are shaved from the *steel*. That needs high-carbon steel and will
    not work with stainless. Do not reject a stainless knife on fire-starting grounds.

## Batoning Technique

Batoning is the process of splitting wood by placing the knife edge on the end grain and striking the spine with a wooden baton (club).

### When to Baton
- Splitting rounds of firewood to expose dry inner wood for fire starting.
- Processing kindling from larger pieces.
- Splitting wood for shelter building.

### Step-by-Step
1. Select a piece of wood no wider than the length of your blade. The blade must protrude from both sides of the wood, or you risk trapping it.
2. Stand or kneel the wood upright on a stable, flat surface (a stump, a flat rock, or the ground).
3. Place the knife edge across the top center of the wood, aligned with the grain. Never baton across the grain.
4. Select a baton: a wrist-thick piece of hardwood, 12-18 in (30-45 cm) long.
5. Strike the spine of the knife near the tip with moderate, controlled blows. Do not swing wildly.
6. As the blade sinks in, continue striking the exposed portion of the spine to drive it through.
7. If the blade becomes stuck, place a second piece of wood on the exposed cutting edge below and baton it upward as a wedge.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Never strike the knife handle or the blade itself. Strike only the spine. Keep your holding hand below the wood, never above the blade.

### Do NOT Baton
- Folding knives (the lock will fail).
- Knives shorter than the diameter of the wood being split.
- Against knots in the wood (the blade will bind and potentially snap).
- On a knife with a partial tang.

## Feather Sticks

A feather stick is a piece of dry wood shaved with thin, curling strips still attached. It is one of the most reliable fire-starting preparations in wet conditions.

### Technique
1. Split a dry piece of softwood (pine, cedar, poplar) using batoning.
2. Hold the stick at roughly 45 degrees, braced against a log — or against your thigh with folded cloth or bark between. Cut **downward and away**, past the outside of your knee. Never draw the blade back toward your inner thigh.
3. Start each cut 2-3 in (5-8 cm) from the end.
4. Angle the blade at approximately 30 degrees to the wood surface.
5. Push the blade along the wood in a slow, controlled stroke. Do not dig deep — thin curls catch fire faster.
6. Stop each curl just before the end of the stick so it stays attached.
7. Rotate the stick and repeat until you have a cluster of 15-20 curls.
8. Make 3-5 feather sticks for a reliable fire start.

## Carving Techniques

### Chest Lever Grip
Lock your knife arm against your chest with the blade pointing away. Pull the workpiece toward you with your other hand. Your chest muscles control the cut. This is the most powerful and controlled carving grip.

### Thumb Push
Place the thumb of your non-knife hand on the spine of the blade near the handle. Push forward while guiding the cut with that thumb. Provides fine control for detail work.

### Knee Brace
Sit with the workpiece braced on your thigh or knee. Cut away from your body in controlled strokes. Never cut toward your femoral artery (inner thigh).

### Useful Carved Items
- Tent stakes and shelter pins.
- Pot hangers and drying racks.
- Toggle buttons for cordage connections.
- Trap triggers and deadfall components.
- Digging stick (fire-hardened tip).
- Improvised spear point (lash knife to shaft only as a last resort — losing your knife is catastrophic).

## Sharpening in the Field

### River Stones
1. Find a smooth, flat stone with a fine grain. Sandstone and slate work well. Test by running your thumbnail across it — a good sharpening stone feels slightly abrasive but not coarse enough to remove visible material from your nail.
2. Wet the stone with water.
3. For a Scandi grind: lay the bevel flat on the stone. You should feel the entire bevel make contact.
4. For other grinds: hold the blade at approximately 15-20 degrees to the stone.
5. Push the edge forward across the stone as if slicing a thin layer off the surface. Maintain consistent pressure and angle.
6. Alternate sides: 5 strokes on one side, 5 on the other. Repeat until a burr (a tiny fold of metal you can feel on the opposite side) forms along the entire edge.
7. Remove the burr with light alternating strokes, reducing pressure with each pass.
8. Test sharpness: a sharp knife catches on your thumbnail when lightly dragged across it.

### Ceramic (Bottom of a Coffee Mug, Ceramic Insulator)
Unglazed ceramic is roughly equivalent to a fine sharpening stone. Use the rough ring on the bottom of any ceramic mug, or the surface of a ceramic electrical insulator. Stroke the blade across it at the proper angle, alternating sides.

### Leather Stropping
Stropping polishes the edge and removes the fine burr left by sharpening.
1. Use any leather surface: a belt, a boot tongue, a sheath.
2. Draw the blade **spine-first** across the leather (opposite direction from cutting). Dragging edge-first will cut the leather.
3. Alternate sides for 20-30 strokes.
4. Strop after every sharpening session and periodically during use to maintain the edge.

## Maintenance

### Rust Prevention (Carbon Steel)
- Wipe the blade dry after every use.
- Coat with a thin layer of any available oil or fat: cooking oil, animal fat, lip balm, sunscreen, or even the natural oils from the sides of your nose or behind your ears.
- In humid conditions, check and re-oil daily.
- If surface rust appears, rub it off with fine sand or a river stone and re-oil immediately.

### Cleaning
- Wash with water after cutting food, sap-heavy wood, or animal materials.
- Scrub sticky residue (sap, pitch) with fine sand and water.
- Dry completely before storage.

### Handle Care
- Tighten handle bolts or pins if they loosen. A rattling handle means a compromised knife.
- If the handle is wood, oil it occasionally to prevent cracking (same oils as blade care).
- Wrap a damaged handle with paracord or [natural cordage](cordage.md) to restore grip.

### Sheath Care
- A leather sheath can trap moisture against the blade. Dry both knife and sheath after wet conditions.
- Store the knife outside the sheath in long-term camp to prevent corrosion.
- Kydex sheaths do not retain moisture and are preferable in wet climates.

## Safety Rules

1. **Cut away from your body.** Always. No exceptions.
2. **Maintain the "blood circle."** Extend your arm holding the knife fully. Rotate 360 degrees. No person or body part should be within that radius while cutting.
3. **Sheath or ground the knife when not in active use.** Never set a bare knife on a log, stump, or the ground where someone (including you) might place a hand on it.
4. **Pass a knife handle-first** with the blade flat along your forearm, edge facing away from both people.
5. **Never use a knife when your hands are wet, numb, or when you are exhausted.** Grip failures cause the majority of knife injuries.
6. **Keep it sharp.** Dull blades require excessive force and skip unpredictably.
7. **Secure your knife to your body** with a lanyard or belt loop. Dropping your knife in deep snow, water, or dense brush can be a survival-ending event.

## What NOT to Do with a Knife

- **Do not throw it.** You risk losing or damaging your single most important tool.
- **Do not pry with it.** Lateral force on a blade snaps tips and bends edges. Use a stick for prying.
- **Do not use it as a screwdriver.** The tip will chip or break.
- **Do not use it as a hammer.** Strike with the baton, not the knife handle.
- **Do not cut wire, metal, or stone.** These materials destroy the edge instantly.
- **Do not lend it to someone who does not know how to use it.** You are responsible for its condition.
- **Do not stab it into the ground.** Soil dulls the edge and introduces grit into the pivot of a folder.

## The Knife as the #1 Survival Tool

No other single tool offers the range of capabilities a knife provides:

- **Fire:** Process feather sticks, split kindling, scrape a ferro rod.
- **Shelter:** Cut cordage (or [make it](cordage.md)), carve stakes, harvest poles, strip bark.
- **Water:** Carve a container, cut a spigot for a birch tree, make a bark funnel.
- **Food:** Clean game, fillet fish, carve trap triggers and deadfalls, make fishing hooks from bone or thorns.
- **Signaling:** Carve ground-to-air symbols in snow or dirt, cut reflective material to shape.
- **Medical:** Cut bandages, shave splint materials, remove splinters (sterilize the blade first). Do **not** lance blisters — every medical guide here says to leave them intact, because the roof is a sterile dressing. See [First Aid Basics](../medical/first-aid-basics.md).
- **Self-defense:** Last resort against animals (see [Animal Attack](../scenarios/animal-attack.md)).
- **Tool making:** Carve a bow drill for fire, fashion a spear, make a digging stick.

If you can carry only one piece of gear into the wilderness, carry a quality fixed-blade knife.

## Common Mistakes
- Buying a cheap knife with a partial tang and discovering it at the worst possible moment.
- Batoning a folding knife.
- Using a knife with wet or numb hands.
- Cutting toward the body, especially toward the femoral artery.
- Storing a carbon steel blade in a wet leather sheath.
- Trying to sharpen without first understanding the blade's grind geometry.
- Applying too much pressure while sharpening (let the stone do the work).
- Throwing the knife for recreation and bending or losing it.
