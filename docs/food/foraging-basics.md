# Foraging Basics

> Know what to eat, when to eat it, and when foraging is not worth the calories.

## At a Glance

- Wild plants rarely provide enough calories to sustain you — treat them as supplements, not meals
- The Universal Edibility Test is a last resort, not a safety guarantee — it cannot detect water hemlock and several other lethal plants. It takes 24 hours; never compress it
- Avoid plants with milky sap, white/yellow berries, or umbrella-shaped flower clusters unless you have positive identification
- Learn 5-10 plants with absolute certainty rather than 50 with vague familiarity
- Foraging is most productive in spring and early summer; least productive in deep winter

## The Universal Edibility Test

!!! danger "DANGER: this test does not detect the plants most likely to kill you"

    The Universal Edibility Test comes from the U.S. Army survival manual and is
    widely reproduced, but it is a **last resort with real limits**, not a safety
    guarantee. Understand what it cannot do before you rely on it:

    - **It misses the deadliest plants.** Water hemlock — the most violently
      toxic plant in North America — smells like parsnip, does not reliably
      burn the lips or tongue, and produces no warning at the tasting stages.
      The final step has you eat 1/4 cup. A single mouthful of water hemlock
      root can kill an adult.
    - **Others that defeat it:** poison hemlock, death camas, foxglove,
      oleander, monkshood, and yew. Several taste mild or pleasant, and several
      act on the heart or nervous system hours after the test says "safe."
    - **Delayed-onset toxins pass unnoticed.** Oxalates and some alkaloids
      damage kidneys and liver over days with no immediate symptom.
    - **Never use it on mushrooms.** See [mushroom-identification.md](mushroom-identification.md).

    Weigh it against the payoff: 24 hours and considerable water for a plant
    part likely worth 15-30 calories per cup. **Positive identification of a few
    known species is always the better strategy.** Skip this test entirely if
    the plant has any warning trait from the list below — especially umbrella-
    shaped flower clusters, which is the family water hemlock and poison
    hemlock belong to.

Use this 8-step protocol only when you cannot positively identify a plant and starvation is a genuine threat. It requires approximately 24 hours and tests only ONE plant part at a time (leaf, stem, root, flower, fruit — each tested separately).

### Step 1 — Separate the Plant into Parts

Divide the plant into roots, stems, leaves, buds, flowers, and fruit/seeds. Test each part independently. A plant may have edible leaves and toxic roots.

### Step 2 — Smell Test

Crush the plant part and smell it. Reject anything with a strong or unpleasant odor (bitter almond scent = cyanide compounds). Allow 5 minutes of exposure.

### Step 3 — Contact Test (Skin)

Place the crushed plant part against the inside of your wrist or inner elbow. Hold it there for 15 minutes. Watch for rash, redness, burning, itching, or swelling. If any reaction occurs, discard that plant part.

### Step 4 — Wait

After the skin contact test, wait 8 hours. Monitor for delayed skin reactions, nausea, or any ill effects. Do not eat anything else during this period (water only) so you can attribute any reaction to the test plant.

### Step 5 — Lip Test

Touch a small portion of the prepared plant part to the corner of your lip. Hold for 3 minutes. Check for burning, tingling, or numbness. If irritation occurs, discard.

### Step 6 — Tongue Test

Place a small portion on your tongue. Hold for 15 minutes. Do not chew. Do not swallow. Spit it out. If burning, extreme bitterness, or numbness develops, discard and rinse your mouth.

### Step 7 — Chew and Spit

Chew a small amount and hold the chewed material in your mouth for 15 minutes. Do not swallow. Monitor for any adverse reaction. Spit out and rinse. Wait 8 hours. If no ill effects occur, proceed.

### Step 8 — Swallow Test

Eat a small portion — approximately 1/4 cup (60 ml). Wait 8 hours. If no nausea, cramps, vomiting, diarrhea, or other symptoms appear, the plant part is likely safe to eat in moderate quantities. Gradually increase portion size over subsequent meals.

| Step | Action | Duration |
|------|--------|----------|
| 1 | Separate parts | 5 min |
| 2 | Smell test | 5 min |

## Rules of Foraging

### Avoid These Characteristics (General Rules)

1. **White or yellow berries** — The majority are toxic. Exception: white mulberries are edible.
2. **Umbrella-shaped flower clusters (umbels)** — The carrot/parsley family (Apiaceae) contains some of the deadliest plants on the continent (poison hemlock, water hemlock). Exception: wild carrot (Queen Anne's lace), fennel, and others — but misidentification kills. Avoid unless you have expert-level identification skills.
3. **Milky or discolored sap** — Many plants with white, milky latex are toxic (spurge family, some euphorbias). Exceptions: dandelion, lettuce, and figs all produce milky sap and are edible.
4. **Three-leaf growth pattern** — "Leaves of three, let it be" applies to poison ivy/oak. Not a food toxicity rule, but a contact dermatitis warning.
5. **Almond or peach smell** when crushed — indicates cyanogenic glycosides (cyanide compounds).
6. **Shiny leaves** — often (not always) indicate toxic species.
7. **Beans or seeds inside pods** — many wild legumes are toxic without extensive preparation.

### Positive Indicators (Not Guarantees)

- Plants you observe animals eating (note: animals tolerate many toxins humans cannot)
- Aggregate berries (blackberry, raspberry pattern) — no known deadly aggregate berries
- Plants from known safe families (see below)

### The Exception Problem

Every avoidance rule has exceptions. The rules exist to keep you alive when you do not have positive identification. If you positively identify a plant, the rules become secondary to your identification.

## When to Forage vs. When Not to Waste Energy

### Forage When

- You are stationary (sheltering in place, awaiting rescue)
- Abundant, easily identifiable plants surround you
- You have adequate water (digestion requires water)
- Temperature and conditions allow safe movement
- Spring or early summer in temperate climates

### Do Not Waste Energy Foraging When

- You are actively traveling to safety and food is scarce
- Deep winter with snow cover — caloric expenditure exceeds return
- You have no water — eating plant material without water accelerates dehydration
- Unknown terrain with no recognizable species
- You are already in caloric debt and the available plants provide negligible energy

## Caloric Reality of Wild Plants

Most wild greens provide 15-30 calories per cup (240 ml). Compare this to your daily survival need of 1,500-2,500 calories. You would need to eat 50-160 cups of wild greens to meet daily caloric requirements — this is not feasible.

| Food Source | Calories per cup (240 ml) | Feasibility |
|-------------|--------------------------|-------------|
| Wild greens (average) | 15-30 | Supplement only |
| Berries (average) | 60-85 | Supplement |
| Nuts (acorns, processed) | 350-500 | Viable staple |
| Roots/tubers (cattail, burdock) | 60-120 | Good supplement |
| Pine inner bark (dried, ground) | 100-160 | Emergency staple |
| Seeds (collected) | 400-550 | Labor-intensive but calorie-dense |

**Bottom line:** Prioritize roots, nuts, seeds, and starchy plants over leafy greens. Greens provide essential vitamins and minerals but not survival calories. Combine foraging with trapping ([trapping-and-snares.md](trapping-and-snares.md)) and fishing ([fishing-improvised.md](fishing-improvised.md)) for adequate caloric intake.

## Tools for Foraging

Carry or improvise the following:

- **Digging stick** — hardened in fire, 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) long, sharpened point. Essential for roots and tubers.
- **Knife** — cuts stems, strips bark, processes plant material
- **Container** — bag, shirt, or improvised bark basket for collecting
- **Cordage** — bundling harvested plants for transport
- **Flat rock or board** — processing surface for crushing, grinding nuts and seeds

## Seasonal Availability (Northern Temperate)

| Season | Primary Sources | Notes |
|--------|----------------|-------|
| Early Spring | Young greens, shoots, bark cambium | Highest nutrient density of the year |
| Late Spring | Flowers, expanding leaves, early roots | Peak foraging season |
| Summer | Berries, fruits, mature leaves | Some greens become bitter/tough |
| Early Fall | Nuts, seeds, late berries, roots | Best caloric density (nuts) |
| Late Fall | Roots, bark, remaining seeds | Greens dying off |
| Winter | Bark cambium, pine needles (tea), stored nuts | Worst season — minimal options |

## Common Edible Plant Families

### Rose Family (Rosaceae)

Includes wild roses (hips), blackberries, raspberries, strawberries, hawthorn, crabapples. Generally recognizable by five-petaled flowers, alternate leaves, and thorny stems. No deadly members, though some seeds contain trace cyanide (apple seeds, cherry pits — avoid eating seeds in quantity).

See [edible-plants-temperate.md](edible-plants-temperate.md) for specific species.

### Mustard Family (Brassicaceae)

Includes wild mustard, shepherd's purse, garlic mustard, watercress, pennycress. Identified by four-petaled flowers arranged in a cross pattern (cruciform), peppery or mustard taste. No deadly members in this family. Excellent source of vitamins A and C.

### Legume Family (Fabaceae)

Includes clovers, vetches, wild peas. Identified by butterfly-shaped flowers and seed pods. Many wild legumes require cooking to neutralize toxins. Clovers are safe raw. Approach wild beans and peas with caution — some contain toxic lectins or alkaloids.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not confuse legume-family plants with similar-looking toxic species. Wild lupines (also Fabaceae) contain toxic alkaloids. Stick to species you positively identify.

## Common Mistakes

- **Testing multiple plant parts simultaneously** — invalidates the edibility test; you cannot determine which part caused a reaction
- **Assuming a plant is safe because an animal eats it** — deer eat plants that sicken humans; birds eat berries that kill mammals
- **Foraging near roads, industrial sites, or treated fields** — plants absorb heavy metals, pesticides, and pollutants
- **Ignoring preparation requirements** — acorns require leaching, some roots require cooking, pokeweed requires multiple boilings
- **Spending 2,000 calories to collect 200 calories of greens** — always calculate energy expenditure vs. return
- **Picking every plant in an area** — in a long-term situation, leave root stock and seeds to regenerate
