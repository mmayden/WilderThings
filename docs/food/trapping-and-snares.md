# Trapping and Snares

> Traps work while you sleep — invest time building them, then spend your energy on other survival tasks.

## At a Glance

- Trapping is more calorie-efficient than active hunting in most survival situations
- Set a minimum of 6-12 traps to have a reasonable chance of catching anything
- Check traps at least every 12 hours — animal suffering, predator theft, and spoilage are real problems
- Game trails, water sources, and funneling terrain are your highest-probability trap sites
- Know the legal framework: trapping is heavily regulated in non-survival contexts

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Trapping animals is illegal in most jurisdictions outside of genuine survival emergencies. Regulations cover trap types, species, seasons, and licensing. In a non-emergency context, obtain proper permits and follow all applicable laws.

## Game Trail Identification

Before building any trap, find where animals travel. A trap on a trail catches game; a trap in a random spot catches nothing.

### Signs of Active Game Trails

- **Tracks:** Clear prints in soft soil, mud, or snow. Note size and frequency — multiple overlapping prints indicate a well-used trail.
- **Scat (droppings):** Fresh droppings near a trail confirm recent and active use. Dry, crumbling droppings indicate old or abandoned routes.
- **Runs:** Narrow, worn paths through grass or underbrush, 4-8 in (10-20 cm) wide for rabbits, wider for deer.
- **Browse lines:** Chewed vegetation at consistent heights along a path.
- **Hair/fur:** Caught on branches, bark, or fencing along the route.
- **Funneling terrain:** Natural narrowing points — between rocks, along fence lines, through gaps in brush, between water and a hillside. Animals follow paths of least resistance.

### Best Trap Locations

- Trail bottlenecks (natural funnels)
- Near water sources (streams, ponds)
- Along fence lines or walls
- Entrances to burrows and dens
- Feeding areas (nut-producing trees, berry patches)
- Between bedding areas and food/water

## Simple Snare

The most fundamental survival trap. A wire or cordage loop positioned on a game trail that tightens around an animal as it passes through.

### Materials

- **Wire:** 20-24 gauge brass or stainless steel wire, 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) length. Ideal material.
- **Cordage alternative:** Paracord inner strands, bank line, or strong natural cordage (rawhide, sinew, braided plant fiber). Wire is far superior — cordage can be chewed through.
- **Anchor:** Sturdy stake, tree, or heavy drag (log/branch)

### Construction

1. Form a small loop at one end of the wire by twisting the wire around itself 4-5 times. This creates the locking eye.
2. Thread the free end through the small loop, creating an adjustable noose.
3. Size the noose opening:
   - Rabbit: 3-4 in (7.5-10 cm) diameter, bottom of loop 2-3 in (5-7.5 cm) off the ground
   - Squirrel: 2-2.5 in (5-6 cm) diameter
   - Larger game: scale accordingly
4. Anchor the free end to a solid stake driven deep into the ground, or tie to a tree/heavy log.
5. Position the noose across the game trail, supported by small twigs or natural features so it hangs in the path at head height for the target animal.
6. Add guide sticks on either side of the trail to funnel the animal through the snare loop.

### Placement Tips

- Set the snare in a slight narrowing of the trail.
- Use sticks and brush to create a subtle funnel directing animals through the loop. Do not create an obvious barrier — animals will divert.
- Minimize ground disturbance and human scent. Wear gloves if available. Rub the snare with local vegetation or soil.
- Wire maintains its shape in wind and rain; cordage may sag.

## Drag Snare

A snare attached to a drag (heavy branch or log) instead of a fixed anchor. The animal pulls the drag, which catches on vegetation and exhausts the animal without the snare breaking from the force of a panicked animal hitting a fixed point.

### Materials

Same as simple snare, plus a drag — a branch 3-5 ft (1-1.5 m) long with side branches that catch on brush.

### Construction

1. Build the snare loop as described above.
2. Instead of anchoring to a fixed point, tie the free end to the drag branch.
3. Position the drag near the trail but not directly in the path.
4. The animal enters the snare, pulls the drag, and the drag tangles in surrounding vegetation.

### When to Use

- When a fixed anchor is not available
- For larger animals that might break a fixed snare
- When you cannot drive stakes into frozen or rocky ground

## Spring Snare

Uses a bent sapling or branch under tension to lift the caught animal off the ground, keeping it away from scavengers and preventing escape.

### Materials

- Snare wire or cordage (noose)
- Flexible sapling or bent branch (spring pole), 5-8 ft (1.5-2.5 m) long
- Trigger mechanism (carved sticks)
- Cordage connecting spring pole to snare via trigger
- Anchor stake

### Trigger Mechanisms

#### Two-Pin Toggle Trigger

1. Carve two small sticks, each 2-3 in (5-7.5 cm) long, with a notch near one end.
2. Drive one stick (the base pin) into the ground at a slight angle.
3. Tie the spring pole line to the second stick (the toggle pin).
4. Hook the notches together so the tension of the spring pole holds them in place.
5. Attach the snare loop so that when an animal pulls on it, the toggle pin disengages from the base pin, releasing the spring pole.

#### L-Hook Trigger

1. Carve two sticks into L-shapes (or carve notches to create hook shapes).
2. One L-hook drives into the ground; the other attaches to the spring pole line.
3. The hooks interlock under tension. The snare loop attaches so that any lateral pull disengages the hooks.

### Construction Steps

1. Identify or plant a flexible sapling near the trail.
2. Bend the sapling down and test its spring — it must lift the target animal clear of the ground.
3. Set the trigger mechanism at ground level on the trail.
4. Connect the spring pole to the trigger.
5. Attach the snare noose to the trigger assembly so pulling the noose releases the trigger.
6. Position the noose across the trail at the target animal's head height.
7. Test the trigger by gently pulling the snare loop — it should release smoothly.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Spring snares are under tension. Keep your face and hands clear of the spring pole path during setup. A sapling snapping back can cause serious injury.

## Deadfall Traps

Use a heavy weight (flat rock or log) propped up by a trigger mechanism. When the trigger is disturbed, the weight falls and kills the animal.

### Figure-4 Deadfall

The most well-known deadfall trigger. Three sticks notched to interlock in a figure-4 pattern.

#### Materials

- Heavy flat rock, 5-15 lbs (2-7 kg) depending on target animal (heavier for larger animals)
- Three sticks: upright, diagonal, and horizontal (bait bar), each 6-10 in (15-25 cm) long
- Knife for carving notches
- Bait

#### Construction

1. **Upright stick (vertical post):** Flatten one end to rest on the ground. Carve a square notch near the top (the diagonal stick hooks here). Carve a second square notch midway (the horizontal bait bar hooks here).
2. **Diagonal stick (prop):** Carve a point at the top that rests against the underside of the rock. Carve a square notch at the bottom that interlocks with the top notch of the upright.
3. **Horizontal stick (bait bar):** Carve a point or notch at the end that engages the mid-point notch of the upright. The other end supports the rock edge. Bait goes on the free end.
4. **Assembly:**
   - Stand the upright on its flat base.
   - Hook the diagonal stick into the upright's top notch, angling upward to prop the rock.
   - Hook the bait bar into the upright's middle notch, extending under the rock.
   - Balance the rock on the diagonal stick (top) and bait bar (side).
   - The three sticks and rock should be in equilibrium. When an animal touches the bait bar, the system collapses.

#### Tips

- Practice carving and assembling the figure-4 without the rock until you can do it smoothly.
- The notches must be precise — too tight and the trap will not fire; too loose and wind triggers it.
- Position the rock so it falls cleanly on the kill zone (where the animal reaches for bait).
- Flat rocks work best. Round rocks roll off-target.

### Paiute Deadfall

Simpler and faster to build than a figure-4. Uses a toggle and cordage system.

#### Materials

- Heavy flat rock
- One upright stick, 6-8 in (15-20 cm)
- One toggle stick, 2-3 in (5-7 cm) — a small, thin stick
- Short length of cordage, 4-6 in (10-15 cm)
- Bait stick, 6-10 in (15-25 cm)

#### Construction

1. Tie one end of the cordage to the top of the upright stick.
2. Tie the other end to the center of the small toggle stick.
3. Stand the upright under the rock's edge, propping it up.
4. Lay the bait stick with one end under the rock and the other end extending out (bait on the free end).
5. Wrap the cordage once around the bait stick and hook the toggle against the rock underside. The friction of the cordage on the bait stick and the toggle against the rock hold everything in balance.
6. When the bait stick is disturbed, the toggle slips, the upright falls, and the rock drops.

## Fish Traps

### Funnel Trap (Basket Trap)

A woven enclosure with a funnel-shaped entrance that guides fish in but prevents them from finding the exit.

#### Materials

- Flexible branches or split wood strips (willow, hazel, bamboo)
- Cordage for binding
- Bait

#### Construction

1. Weave a cylindrical basket, 18-36 in (45-90 cm) long, 8-12 in (20-30 cm) diameter.
2. Construct a funnel (cone) from the same material, with the wide end matching the basket opening and the narrow end 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) diameter.
3. Insert the funnel into the basket opening with the narrow end pointing inward.
4. Secure the funnel to the basket with cordage.
5. Close the opposite end of the basket (or leave a small opening tied shut for bait access and fish removal).
6. Bait the trap with crushed insects, fish scraps, or bread-like material.
7. Place in a stream facing upstream, or in still water near shore.

See [fishing-improvised.md](fishing-improvised.md) for additional fish trap designs and fishing techniques.

### Weir

A V-shaped wall of rocks or stakes in a stream that funnels fish into a narrow collection point.

#### Construction

1. Build two walls of stacked rocks or driven stakes in a stream, forming a V shape pointing downstream.
2. The opening at the V's point should be narrow — 6-12 in (15-30 cm).
3. Place a basket trap or enclosure at the narrow point.
4. Fish swimming upstream enter the wide end of the V and are funneled into the collection point.
5. In tidal areas, build the weir to trap fish as the tide recedes.

## Trap Line Management

### How Many Traps

A single trap has a low probability of success on any given night. Increase your odds through numbers:

- **Minimum:** 6 traps across different locations
- **Recommended:** 12+ traps for reliable food production
- **Mix trap types:** Some snares on trails, some deadfalls near dens, a fish trap in the stream

### Checking Frequency

- Check every trap at least every 12 hours (twice daily — morning and evening)
- Reasons: animal suffering, meat spoilage in warm weather, predators stealing your catch, trapped animals chewing through cordage
- Plan a trap line route that is efficient to walk

### Trap Line Principles

- Spread traps across different animal habitats (forest edge, waterside, meadow)
- If a trap has not caught anything in 48 hours, relocate it
- Rebait deadfalls after rain (scent washes away)
- Repair and reset traps immediately after a catch
- Mark trap locations with subtle indicators so you find them on your route

## Bait Selection

| Target Animal | Effective Baits |
|--------------|----------------|
| Rabbit | No bait needed — place snares on runs. Rabbits follow habit trails. |
| Squirrel | Nuts, seeds, peanut butter |
| Raccoon | Fish scraps, sweet foods, shiny objects near bait |
| Opossum | Fish, fruit, carrion |
| Birds | Seeds, berries, insects |
| Fish | Insects, worms, fish scraps, crushed shellfish |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Bait also attracts predators. A baited deadfall may attract bears, coyotes, or other large animals. Be aware of the predator community in your area.

## Ethical and Legal Considerations

### In a Survival Emergency

Use whatever methods keep you alive. Survival overrides game regulations. After rescue, report what you caught and where. Most jurisdictions explicitly exempt genuine survival situations from trapping regulations.

### In All Other Contexts

- Trapping is regulated by state, provincial, and national wildlife agencies
- Permits and licenses are required in virtually all jurisdictions
- Legal trap types, target species, and seasons are strictly defined
- Many trap types described here (deadfalls, snares) are illegal for recreational trapping in many areas
- Body-gripping traps may be banned or restricted
- Check all local and national regulations before building or setting any trap

### Ethical Principles

- Check traps frequently to minimize suffering
- Kill trapped animals quickly if found alive (a sharp blow to the base of the skull)
- Do not set traps you cannot check regularly
- Release non-target animals unharmed when possible
- Do not waste — use all edible parts of caught animals

## Common Mistakes

- **Setting only 1-2 traps** — the probability of catching anything with a single trap on any given night is low. You need volume.
- **Poor placement** — a perfect trap in the wrong location catches nothing. Invest time finding active trails.
- **Over-engineering triggers** — a trigger that is too stable will not fire. A trigger that is too sensitive fires in the wind. Test before leaving.
- **Leaving human scent** — handle traps and triggers with gloves or rubbed with local vegetation and dirt.
- **Not checking traps** — unchecked traps mean spoiled meat, suffering animals, and wasted effort.
- **Building traps too close to camp** — animals avoid areas with heavy human activity. Set traps at least 100 yards (90 m) from your shelter.
- **Ignoring fish traps** — fish traps in productive water can be the most reliable passive food source. See [fishing-improvised.md](fishing-improvised.md).

| Trap Type | Target | Materials | Effectiveness |
|-----------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Simple snare | Rabbit, squirrel | Wire/cordage, stake | Moderate |
| Drag snare | Rabbit, larger game | Wire/cordage, drag log | Moderate |
| Spring snare | Rabbit, squirrel | Wire, sapling, trigger sticks | High |
| Figure-4 deadfall | Rodents, small mammals | Rock, 3 carved sticks | Moderate |
| Paiute deadfall | Rodents, small mammals | Rock, 2 sticks, cordage | Moderate |
| Funnel fish trap | Fish | Woven branches, cordage | High |
| Weir | Fish | Rocks or stakes | High |

!!! warning "CAUTION: rabbits carry disease that enters through your hands"
    Tularemia — rabbit fever — transmits through skin contact with infected tissue while
    skinning, not only through eating. Brucellosis and, in deer country, CWD behave the
    same way. Cooking the meat does not protect the hands that prepared it.

    Glove up or cover any cuts before handling a carcass, wash afterwards, and **discard
    any rabbit whose liver is covered in small white spots** — that is the sign of
    tularemia infection. Wash hands before eating regardless.
