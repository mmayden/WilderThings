# Cooking Without Gear

> Cook food safely using only natural materials and fire — no pots, pans, or manufactured tools required.

## At a Glance

- Rock boiling is the most versatile no-gear cooking method — heat stones and drop them into a water-filled container
- Never use rocks from riverbeds or wet environments for heating — they can explode when heated
- Earth ovens (pit cooking) handle large quantities and require minimal attention once sealed
- Green wood, bark, leaves, and clay all serve as cooking vessels when used correctly
- Internal food temperature must reach 165°F (74°C) to kill pathogens — when in doubt, cook longer

## Rock Boiling

Rock boiling lets you boil water in any container that holds liquid — bark vessels, animal stomachs, hide-lined pits, wooden bowls, or even tightly woven baskets.

**Method:**

1. Build a hot fire and place fist-sized rocks directly in the coals. Use dense, dry, non-porous rock — basalt and other solid igneous rock are best. Heat for 20-30 minutes until glowing.
2. Prepare a container with water and food.
3. Use two green-wood sticks as tongs (or a split stick) to lift hot rocks from the fire.
4. Brush off ash and drop rocks into the water.
5. The water reaches a boil within 2-3 rocks for a quart (liter) of water.
6. Replace rocks as they cool. Keep a rotation: rocks heating in the fire, rocks in the water, spent rocks drying to be reheated.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Heated rock can explode and throw fragments at face height. Two rules:

    - **Never use rock from streambeds, lakeshores, or wet ground.** Trapped moisture turns to steam and bursts the rock.
    - **Never use porous or layered rock** — sandstone, shale, slate, limestone, or pumice. These hold water inside even when the surface feels bone dry, and they split along their layers. Sandstone is porous enough to hold aquifers; do not put it in a fire.

    Use dense igneous rock (basalt is ideal) collected from high, dry ground. If a rock hisses, cracks, or pings as it heats, back away and let it burn out.

!!! tip
    A bark container will not burn as long as the water level stays above the flame line. Birch bark holds water naturally. Fold the edges up and pin with wooden skewers or thorns.

## Earth Oven (Pit Cooking)

Earth ovens cook large quantities slowly using retained heat. This method works for meat, fish, root vegetables, and shellfish.

**Method:**

1. Dig a pit 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) deep and wide enough for the food.
2. Line the bottom with dry rocks (same explosion warning applies — use dry, dense stones).
3. Build a large fire in the pit on top of the rocks. Burn for 1-2 hours until a deep bed of coals forms and rocks are superheated.
4. Rake out most of the coals, leaving the hot rocks.
5. Layer green leaves or wet grass over the rocks — this creates steam and prevents food from touching bare stone.
6. Place food on the leaf layer.
7. Cover food with another layer of green leaves.
8. Seal the pit with soil, packing it tight. Leave no gaps where steam can escape.
9. Cooking time: 2-4 hours for most foods. Large game joints may need 6-8 hours.
10. Dig up carefully and check that meat is cooked through.

=== "Root Vegetables"
    Wrap in large leaves. Place directly on the leaf layer over hot rocks. Cook 2-3 hours. Sweet potatoes, taro, yams, and tubers all work well.

=== "Whole Fish"
    Wrap in large leaves (banana, burdock, or corn husk). Place on the leaf layer. Cook 1-2 hours depending on size.

=== "Large Meat Joints"
    Wrap tightly in multiple layers of leaves. Place on the leaf layer and pack additional leaves around and over. Cook 4-8 hours.

!!! note
    Earth oven cooking frees you to do other tasks. Once sealed, it requires no tending. Plan your timing — check meat after the minimum cook time.

## Spit Roasting

The simplest open-fire cooking method. Best for small game, birds, and fish.

**Method:**

1. Sharpen a green-wood stick (hardwood preferred — avoid resinous pine or spruce).
2. Skewer the food lengthwise through the center.
3. Support the spit on forked sticks driven into the ground on each side of the fire, 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) above the coals.
4. Rotate regularly — every 5-10 minutes — for even cooking.
5. Cook until juices run clear (meat) or flesh flakes easily (fish).

!!! tip
    Flatten the spit where it passes through the food, or carve a notch. This prevents the food from spinning freely on the stick when you rotate it.

## Plank Cooking

Use a flat piece of green wood as a cooking surface.

**Method:**

1. Split a section of green hardwood (oak, maple, alder) into a plank roughly 1 inch (2.5 cm) thick.
2. Pin the fish or thin-sliced meat to the plank using small wooden pegs or thorns.
3. Prop the plank at an angle near the fire, food side facing the heat. Distance: 6-12 inches (15-30 cm) from flames.
4. Cook 15-30 minutes, adjusting distance to control heat. Rotate the plank 180 degrees halfway through.

Best for: fish fillets, thin meat strips. The wood adds a mild smoky flavor.

## Clay Baking

Clay seals in moisture and protects food from direct flame. No cleaning required — crack the clay shell open and eat.

**Method:**

1. Find clay soil (sticky, moldable when wet, holds shape). River banks and lake edges often have deposits.
2. Do not gut small game or birds. Leave scales on fish. The entrails and skin peel away with the clay.
3. Coat the food in a layer of clay 1/2-1 inch (1.5-2.5 cm) thick. Seal all gaps.
4. Place the clay-wrapped food directly in hot coals. Rake coals over the top.
5. Bake 1-2 hours for birds and small game, 30-45 minutes for fish.
6. Crack the hardened clay shell open. Skin, feathers, scales, and entrails pull away cleanly with the clay.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Ensure the clay layer has no cracks or thin spots. Steam must stay trapped inside. If clay cracks during cooking, patch with more wet clay or the food may dry out.

## Coal Roasting

Place food directly on or in hot coals. Simple, fast, and effective.

**Method:**

- **Root vegetables:** Push directly into hot coals. Cover with more coals. Cook 20-40 minutes. Brush off ash and peel the charred skin.
- **Meat:** Wrap in large green leaves (banana, burdock, dock, grape). Place the wrapped package in coals and cover. The leaves char on the outside but steam the food inside.
- **Eggs:** Poke a small hole in one end to prevent explosion. Nestle in warm ash (not directly in red-hot coals). Cook 10-15 minutes.

!!! tip
    Ash cakes: mix ground acorn flour, cattail pollen, or any wild grain with water to form a dough. Flatten into cakes 1/2 inch (1.5 cm) thick. Place directly on hot ash (not coals) and cook 5-10 minutes per side. Brush off ash before eating.

## Bamboo Cooking

In tropical and subtropical regions, green bamboo serves as a natural cooking pot.

**Method:**

1. Cut a section of green bamboo at least 3 inches (7.5 cm) in diameter. Cut just below a node on each end so one end is sealed by the natural membrane.
2. Fill through the open end with water, rice, meat, vegetables, or a combination.
3. Plug the open end with a wad of leaves.
4. Lean the bamboo at an angle over the fire, sealed end down, open end up and away from direct flame.
5. Alternatively, lay it horizontally on supports over coals.
6. Cook 20-40 minutes. The bamboo chars on the outside but the water inside prevents it from burning through.
7. Split the bamboo open to serve.

!!! note
    Each bamboo section is a single-use vessel. Cut multiple sections to cook several portions at once.

## Improvised Vessels

| Vessel | Material | Method | Best For |
|--------|----------|--------|----------|
| Bark container | Birch, elm bark | Fold and pin | Rock boiling |
| Green wood trough | Hollowed half-log | Carve with knife or coal-burn | Rock boiling |
| Animal stomach | Large game stomach | Suspend or place in pit | Boiling stew |
| Large shell | Abalone, turtle shell | Place over coals | Frying, heating |
| Flat rock | Any flat, dry stone | Heat directly in fire | Griddle for cakes, thin meats |

**Coal-burned bowl:** Place a hot coal on the surface of a green wood log section. Blow gently to keep it burning into the wood. Add new coals as needed. Scrape out charred wood with a stick or stone. Repeat until the depression is deep enough. This takes 1-2 hours but produces a durable rock-boiling vessel.

## Food Safety Temperatures

| Food | Minimum Internal Temp | Test Without Thermometer |
|------|----------------------|-------------------------|
| All wild game | 165°F (74°C) | Juices run clear, no pink inside |
| Fish | 145°F (63°C) | Flesh flakes easily, opaque throughout |
| Ground/shredded meat | 165°F (74°C) | No pink, firm texture |
| Root vegetables | 200°F (93°C) | Soft when pierced with a stick |
| Boiled water | 212°F (100°C) | Rolling boil for 1 minute (3 min above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m) |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Wild game carries higher parasite and bacteria loads than domestic meat. Always cook to 165°F (74°C). When in doubt, cook longer. Undercooked bear, boar, and wild cat meat can transmit trichinosis.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using wet or riverbed rocks** — trapped moisture causes rocks to fracture or explode when heated rapidly
- **Using resinous green wood for cooking implements** — pine, spruce, and fir sap makes food taste bitter and deposits creosote. Use hardwoods: oak, maple, alder, birch, willow.
- **Placing food too close to flame** — the outside chars while the inside stays raw. Cook over coals, not flames. Maintain 8-12 inches (20-30 cm) between food and heat source.
- **Not rotating spit-roasted food** — one side burns while the other is raw. Turn every 5-10 minutes.
- **Opening the earth oven too early** — heat escapes and cannot be recovered. Trust the timing and check once.
