# Field Butchering and Processing Game

> Properly bleed, gut, skin, and quarter wild game in the field to preserve meat safely and prevent spoilage.

## At a Glance

- Begin processing immediately after a kill — delay causes rapid spoilage, especially above 40°F (4°C)
- Remove the gut cavity contents within 30 minutes to prevent bacterial contamination of the meat
- Keep meat clean, cool, dry, and off the ground at every stage
- Wear gloves or wash hands thoroughly — tularemia, brucellosis, and CWD are real risks
- A single sharp knife is the only essential tool; everything else is helpful but optional

## Immediate Steps After a Kill

1. Confirm the animal is dead. Approach from behind. Touch the eye with a stick — no blink reflex means death.
2. Tag or mark the animal if required by local regulations.
3. Position the carcass on a slope with the head uphill if possible.
4. Begin bleeding within 5 minutes.

!!! note
    Legal requirements for tagging, reporting, and transporting game vary by jurisdiction. Know your local regulations before hunting. In a true survival emergency, sustaining life takes priority.

## Bleeding

1. Position the animal with the head downhill.
2. Cut the throat deeply across both carotid arteries and jugular veins — a single deep cut across the neck below the jaw.
3. Allow blood to drain for 5-10 minutes. Lift or prop the hindquarters if needed.
4. Save the blood if possible — it is nutritious and can be cooked into blood sausage or added to stews.

!!! tip
    Proper bleeding improves meat flavor and shelf life. Retained blood accelerates bacterial growth and gives meat a gamy, metallic taste.

## Gutting (Field Dressing)

### Large Game (Deer, Elk, Boar)

1. Place the animal on its back. Spread the hind legs.
2. Pinch the skin of the belly between the hind legs and make a small cut through the skin and abdominal wall. Do not puncture the intestines.
3. Insert two fingers in a V-shape into the cut, blade edge up between your fingers, and cut forward toward the ribcage. Your fingers guide the blade and push intestines away.
4. Cut around the anus (or genitals), freeing the lower intestine. Tie off the end with cordage to prevent contamination.
5. Cut the diaphragm (the thin muscle wall separating chest and abdominal cavity) along its attachment to the ribs.
6. Reach into the chest cavity and sever the windpipe and esophagus as high as you can.
7. Roll the animal on its side. The entire gut mass should slide out.
8. Wipe the cavity with dry grass or cloth. Do not wash with water unless you can dry the cavity immediately — moisture promotes bacteria.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If you puncture the stomach or intestines, the meat will be contaminated with bacteria. Cut carefully. If a puncture occurs, immediately wipe away all contamination and trim any affected meat.

**Save these organs (edible):** Heart, liver, kidneys. Check the liver — if it has white spots, cysts, or discoloration, discard it and proceed with caution on the rest of the animal.

### Small Game (Rabbits, Squirrels)

1. Pinch the skin on the back and cut a horizontal slit.
2. Insert fingers into the slit and pull the skin apart in opposite directions — toward the head and toward the tail. The skin peels off like a jacket.
3. Cut off the head, feet, and tail.
4. Make a small abdominal cut and remove the entrails. Save the heart, liver, and kidneys.
5. Rinse if clean water is available, or wipe dry.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Wear gloves when handling rabbits. Tularemia (rabbit fever) transmits through skin contact with infected tissue. White spots on the liver indicate infection — discard the animal.

## Skinning Large Game

=== "Flat Skinning (No Hanging)"
    1. Place the carcass on its back on a clean surface (bark, tarp, dry grass).
    2. Cut the skin along the inside of each leg from the hoof/foot to the centerline belly cut.
    3. Work the skin away from the muscle using your fist or a blunt tool. Pull rather than cut — less risk of puncturing the hide.
    4. Roll the carcass side to side, peeling the skin down. The skin becomes your clean work surface underneath.

=== "Hanging Method"
    1. Hang the carcass from a sturdy branch by the hind legs using rope or cord through the gambrel tendons (the thick tendon behind the ankle joint).
    2. Cut the skin around each hind leg at the knee joint.
    3. Cut down the inside of each hind leg to the vent.
    4. Peel the skin downward using body weight — grip the skin edge and pull steadily. Use a knife only where the skin resists.
    5. Continue peeling down over the front legs and head.

## Quartering

Quarter the carcass to make it portable and to cool the meat faster.

1. **Remove the front legs.** No bone joint connects them to the body — cut through the muscle between the shoulder blade and the ribcage. Lift the leg away.
2. **Remove the backstraps.** Cut along both sides of the spine from neck to pelvis, then peel the long muscle away from the ribs. These are the best cuts.
3. **Remove the hind legs.** Cut through the ball-and-socket hip joint. Flex the leg outward to find the joint, then cut through it.
4. **Remove rib meat.** Cut along the ribs close to the bone to salvage remaining meat.
5. **Neck meat.** Slice away from the vertebrae. Good for stew.

Hang quarters in shade with airflow. Protect from flies with cloth or a mesh bag if available.

## Processing Birds

1. Pluck or skin. Plucking preserves fat under the skin — dunk in hot (not boiling) water for 30-60 seconds to loosen feathers, then pull. Skinning is faster but loses the fat layer.
2. Cut around the vent. Reach in and pull out the entrails.
3. Save the heart, liver, and gizzard. Cut the gizzard open and peel out the inner lining and contents.
4. Remove the crop (food-storing pouch at the base of the throat).

## Processing Fish

1. Scale by scraping from tail to head with a knife blade or rough stone.
2. Cut from the vent to the gills. Remove entrails.
3. Remove the gills — they spoil fastest and give the meat a bitter taste.
4. For filleting: cut behind the gill plate down to the spine, then turn the blade and run it along the spine to the tail. Flip and repeat.

!!! tip
    Small fish (under 6 inches / 15 cm) can be eaten whole after gutting — bones soften when thoroughly cooked and provide calcium.

## Saving the Fat

On a lean wild animal the fat is a larger share of the survival value than the meat is,
and it is the part most often discarded. Muscle meat alone cannot sustain you — see
[Lean Meat Alone Will Not Sustain You](hunting-basics.md#lean-meat-alone-will-not-sustain-you)
for why.

1. **Marrow.** Crack the long bones — femur, humerus, tibia — with a rock or the back of
   an axe. Eat the marrow raw or add it to a broth. Boiling the cracked bones for an hour
   pulls out fat that cracking alone leaves behind.
2. **Cavity fat.** Peel the fat off the kidneys, the gut lining, and around the heart
   while gutting. Do not let it go out with the viscera.
3. **Subcutaneous fat.** On birds and on animals in good autumn condition there is a
   layer under the skin. Pluck birds instead of skinning them to keep it.
4. **Render and store.** Chop the fat small, heat it gently in a container with a little
   water until it liquefies, and pour off the clear fat. It keeps for weeks and travels
   better than meat.

!!! note
    Late-winter and early-spring animals may have almost no fat at all — their reserves
    are gone. Plan on plant calories to make up the difference in those seasons.

## Disease and Hygiene

| Disease | Source | Signs | Prevention |
|---------|--------|-------|------------|
| Tularemia | Rabbits, rodents | White spots on liver, lethargic animal | Wear gloves, cook to 165°F (74°C) |
| CWD | Deer, elk | Emaciated, drooling, no fear of humans | Avoid visibly sick animals, do not eat brain/spine |
| Trichinosis | Wild boar, bear | Not visible in meat | Cook to 165°F (74°C) throughout |
| Brucellosis | Wild boar, elk, bison | Swollen joints in animal | Wear gloves, cook thoroughly |

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Chronic Wasting Disease (CWD) is a fatal prion disease in deer and elk. Do not harvest animals that appear emaciated, excessively drooling, or unusually tame. Avoid contact with the brain, spinal cord, eyes, and lymph nodes. CWD cannot be destroyed by cooking.

**Basic hygiene rules:**

- Wash or sanitize hands before and after handling game
- Keep meat away from gut contents at all times
- Use separate cutting surfaces for gutting and meat processing if possible
- Cool meat to below 40°F (4°C) as fast as possible

## Tools

| Tool | Use | Substitute |
|------|-----|-----------|
| Fixed-blade knife (4-5 in / 10-12 cm) | All cutting tasks | Sharp stone flake |
| Small folding saw | Pelvis and rib cuts | Repeated knife scoring |
| Paracord or rope | Hanging, tying off | Vine, bark strips |
| Game bags (mesh) | Fly protection, airflow | Cloth, shirt |
| Latex/nitrile gloves | Disease prevention | Plastic bags over hands |

## Common Mistakes

- **Waiting too long to gut** — bacteria from the intestines migrate into the meat within hours, especially in warm weather
- **Puncturing the gut during field dressing** — use the two-finger guide technique and cut edge-up
- **Failing to cool the carcass** — prop the body cavity open with a stick, hang in shade, and allow airflow
- **Not wearing gloves** — tularemia and brucellosis transmit through cuts and abrasions during handling
- **Contaminating meat by placing it on the ground** — use the hide, bark, or leaves as a clean surface

## Quick Reference

| Step | Timing | Key Action |
|------|--------|-----------|
| Confirm kill | Immediately | Eye reflex test |
| Bleed | Within 5 min | Cut throat — both carotids |
| Gut | Within 30 min | Belly cut, remove organs |
| Skin | Within 1-2 hours | Pull, don't cut |
| Quarter | After skinning | Front legs, backstraps, hind legs, ribs |
| Cool | Immediately | Shade, airflow, off the ground |
