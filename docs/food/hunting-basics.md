# Hunting Basics

> Track, stalk, and harvest game animals using fundamental skills and improvised weapons when modern tools are unavailable.

## At a Glance

- Hunting burns significant calories — assess whether the expected return justifies the expenditure
- Tracking is the foundation: learn to read tracks, scat, trails, bedding areas, and feeding signs
- Wind direction and noise discipline determine whether you get close enough for a shot or strike
- Improvised weapons are limited in range and lethality — ambush and close-range encounters are your advantage
- Process kills quickly to prevent spoilage; field dress within 30 minutes in warm weather
- Save every scrap of fat and marrow — lean meat alone will not sustain you, and small game is almost all lean meat

!!! warning "DISCLAIMER"
    Hunting is regulated by law in every jurisdiction. Seasons, species, methods, and licensing requirements apply. In a non-emergency context, obtain all required permits and follow applicable regulations. In a genuine survival emergency, sustaining your life takes precedence — report any takes to authorities after rescue.

## Tracking Fundamentals

### Reading Tracks

**What to observe:**
- **Species:** Shape and size of the print. Cloven hooves = deer, elk, moose, pigs. Paw pads with claws = canines (dog, coyote, fox). Paw pads without visible claws = felines. Long hind feet = rabbits.
- **Direction of travel:** Toes point in the direction of movement. Deeper impression at the toe = the animal was moving quickly.
- **Age of track:** Sharp edges = fresh (hours). Crumbling edges, debris in the print = older (12+ hours). Tracks partially filled with water in mud = made before the water rose. Tracks with frost crystals in cold weather = made before the freeze (overnight or older).
- **Gait:** Walking tracks are evenly spaced. Running tracks are spaced further apart with deeper toe impressions and spray of substrate behind the print.

### Common Track Sizes

| Animal | Front Track Width | Notes |
|--------|------------------|-------|
| White-tailed deer | 2-3.5 in (5-9 cm) | Cloven hoof, heart-shaped |
| Rabbit (cottontail) | 1 in (2.5 cm) front, 3-4 in (7-10 cm) hind | Hind prints land ahead of front |
| Squirrel | 0.5-1 in (1-2.5 cm) | Four toes front, five toes hind |
| Turkey | 4-4.5 in (10-11 cm) | Three forward toes, distinctive |
| Raccoon | 2-3 in (5-7.5 cm) | Hand-like, five toes |
| Wild boar/feral pig | 2-3 in (5-7.5 cm) | Cloven, rounder than deer |

### Scat (Droppings)

Scat reveals diet, species, and recency.

- **Deer:** Piles of small, oval pellets (3/4 in / 2 cm). Soft/clumped when feeding on green browse. Hard, separated pellets on dry diet.
- **Rabbit:** Small, round, fibrous pellets. Often near burrow entrances.
- **Carnivore scat:** Twisted, tapered, often containing hair and bone fragments.
- **Bird (turkey, grouse):** J-shaped or cylindrical with a white uric acid cap.
- **Freshness:** Moist, dark, strong-smelling = fresh (hours). Dry, gray, crumbling, odorless = old (days to weeks).

### Trails and Runs

- **Deer trails:** 6-12 in (15-30 cm) wide, worn through vegetation, often connecting bedding areas (thick cover on high ground) to feeding areas (meadows, crop edges) and water.
- **Rabbit runs:** 4-6 in (10-15 cm) wide tunnels through grass or brush. Used repeatedly. Excellent locations for snares (see [trapping-and-snares.md](trapping-and-snares.md)).
- **Game highways:** Near water sources, multiple species use the same approach routes. Look for overlapping tracks of different species.

### Bedding Areas

- Deer bed in thick cover, usually on elevated ground with sight lines and escape routes.
- Look for flattened ovals in grass, leaves, or snow — 3-4 ft (1-1.2 m) long for deer.
- Droppings and hair near bedding confirm use.
- Do not hunt in bedding areas — you will push animals out permanently. Hunt between bedding and feeding/water instead.

### Feeding Signs

- **Browse lines:** Ragged, torn ends on twigs and branches (deer lack upper incisors and tear rather than cut cleanly).
- **Rooting:** Disturbed soil where pigs or bears have dug for roots, grubs, or tubers.
- **Scratching/rubbing:** Antler rubs on trees (deer). Bark stripped by bears. Scratched ground under nut trees (turkeys, squirrels).
- **Crop and mast damage:** Edge of agricultural fields, under oak or beech trees (acorn mast).

## Stalking Technique

### Wind Direction

Wind carries your scent. A deer can smell you from over 300 yards (275 m) downwind.

- **Always approach from downwind** — the wind blows from the animal toward you.
- Check wind direction constantly: wet a finger and feel for cooling, drop light debris and watch it drift, observe smoke direction.
- If the wind shifts, stop. Reposition or abort the stalk.
- Thermals: in the morning, cool air flows downhill (hunt above your target). In the afternoon, warm air rises uphill (hunt below).

### Noise Discipline

- **Step placement:** Place the ball of your foot first, feel for sticks and debris, then lower your heel. Avoid stepping on dry leaves, twigs, or gravel.
- **Speed:** Move one slow step at a time. Pause between steps. Natural background noise (wind, running water, other animals) provides cover — move when the wind gusts.
- **Clothing:** Avoid synthetic fabrics that swish and scrape. Wool and soft cotton are quieter.
- **Equipment:** Secure anything that rattles, clanks, or jingles. Tape or tie loose items.

### Movement Patterns

- Move in short segments: 3-5 steps, then stop and observe for 30-60 seconds.
- Use the terrain: stay behind ridgelines, use trees and brush for concealment.
- Avoid skylining yourself (appearing as a silhouette on a ridgeline or hilltop).
- Break your outline — humans are recognized by their upright, symmetrical shape.
- Approach from higher ground when possible — animals watch for threats at their level, not above.
- Best times to stalk: dawn and dusk (animals are active and feeding, light is low).

### The Final Approach

- Inside 50 yards (45 m), every movement matters. Animals at close range detect the smallest twitch.
- Move only when the animal's head is down (feeding, drinking).
- Freeze when the animal looks up — motion triggers flight response, but a stationary shape may not.
- Be in position before you need to be. Set up on a trail, a water approach, or a feeding area and let the animal come to you (ambush) rather than closing the final distance.

## Kill Zones by Animal Type

Effective shots or strikes must hit vital areas. With improvised weapons, range is limited — proximity and accuracy matter enormously.

### Deer / Large Ungulates

- **Heart/lung zone:** Behind the front shoulder, lower third of the body. This is the primary target. Heart sits low, lungs fill the chest cavity. A broadside shot into this area is the most lethal placement.
- **Size of kill zone:** Approximately 8-10 in (20-25 cm) diameter.
- **Avoid:** Head shots (small target, easy to wound), gut shots (slow death, meat contamination), leg shots (cripples without killing).

### Rabbit

- **Head/upper body:** Small target. A blunt impact weapon (throwing stick) to the head or body is effective.
- **Behavior advantage:** Rabbits freeze when startled and rely on camouflage before bolting. This freeze moment is your opportunity.

### Birds (Turkey, Grouse, Pheasant)

- **Head/neck:** For birds, target the head and neck — the body is protected by thick feathers that deflect weak projectiles.
- **On the ground:** Ground-dwelling birds are easier targets than birds in flight. Turkeys and grouse often walk rather than fly when disturbed.
- **At roost:** Birds on a roosting branch at dusk are stationary targets.

### Small Mammals (Squirrel, Raccoon)

- **Body shot:** Center mass. Small mammals do not present a large kill zone — aim for the torso.
- **Trapping is more efficient** for these species. See [trapping-and-snares.md](trapping-and-snares.md).

## Improvised Weapons

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Improvised weapons are far less effective than purpose-built hunting tools. Expect a high miss rate. Supplement with passive trapping and fishing methods.

### Throwing Stick (Rabbit Stick)

The simplest hunting weapon — a heavy, curved stick thrown sidearm at close-range targets.

**Construction:**
1. Select a hardwood stick or branch, slightly curved, 18-30 in (45-75 cm) long, 1-2 in (2.5-5 cm) diameter.
2. Weight should be concentrated toward one end (a natural club shape works well).
3. Smooth any knots that affect flight.

**Technique:**
- Throw sidearm with a flat trajectory, 2-4 ft (60-120 cm) off the ground.
- Effective range: 15-30 ft (5-10 m). Beyond this, accuracy drops rapidly.
- Best against: rabbits, ground birds, roosting birds at close range.
- Carry 2-3 throwing sticks — you get one throw before the animal flees.

### Bola

Two or three weights connected by cordage. Thrown at the legs or body of an animal to entangle it.

**Construction:**
1. Select 2-3 smooth, heavy stones, each 4-8 oz (110-225 g).
2. Wrap each stone in a pouch of fabric or rawhide and tie securely.
3. Connect the pouches with cordage 18-24 in (45-60 cm) long. For a three-stone bola, tie all three cords together at a central point.

**Technique:**
- Hold the center knot and whirl the bola overhead.
- Release toward the target at close range — 15-30 ft (5-10 m).
- The spinning weights spread and wrap around legs, wings, or neck.
- Best against: ground birds (turkeys, geese), medium mammals in the open.

### Sling

A pouch attached to two cords. Whirled overhead to launch a stone at high velocity.

**Construction:**
1. Cut a pouch from leather, sturdy fabric, or woven plant fiber — approximately 3 x 5 in (7.5 x 12.5 cm).
2. Punch holes at each end of the pouch.
3. Attach two cords, each 24-36 in (60-90 cm) long. One cord has a finger loop; the other has a knot or smooth end for release.

**Technique:**
1. Place a round, egg-sized stone (1.5-2 in / 4-5 cm) in the pouch.
2. Grip the loop on one cord with your index finger, hold the release cord between your thumb and forefinger.
3. Whirl overhead or beside you (vertical or horizontal).
4. Release the free cord at the right moment in the arc to send the stone at the target.
5. This requires extensive practice. Accuracy improves dramatically with repetition.

**Effective range:** 30-100 ft (10-30 m) with practice. Lethal to small-medium game. Can take rabbits, birds, and even stun or kill larger animals with well-placed head shots.

### Atlatl (Spear Thrower) — Concept

A lever arm that extends the length of the throwing arm, increasing spear velocity and range.

**Concept:**
1. Cut a straight branch 18-24 in (45-60 cm) long.
2. Carve or attach a hook/spur at one end that catches the butt of a dart (light spear).
3. Carve darts: straight shafts 4-6 ft (1.2-1.8 m) long with fire-hardened or stone/bone tips.
4. Place the dart butt against the spur, hold the atlatl behind the dart, and throw in an overhand motion — the atlatl acts as a lever.

**Advantages:** Doubles effective range and increases impact force compared to hand-thrown spears. Used by humans for tens of thousands of years before the bow.

**Limitations:** Requires significant practice. Darts must be straight and consistent. Not a weapon you build and use effectively on day one.

## Field Processing

Detailed butchering procedures are covered in
[Field Butchering](field-butchering.md). The essentials:

1. **Field dress immediately** — within 30 minutes, especially in warm weather. Gut bacteria multiply rapidly and taint meat.
2. **Open the body cavity** from sternum to pelvis. Remove all internal organs.
3. **Save the heart, liver, fat, and marrow** — the organs are the most nutrient-dense
   parts, and the fat and marrow are the only meaningful fat on a lean animal. See
   [Lean Meat Alone Will Not Sustain You](#lean-meat-alone-will-not-sustain-you).
4. **Drain blood** — hang or prop the carcass to drain. Blood attracts predators and accelerates spoilage.
5. **Cool the meat** — get air circulating inside the body cavity. In warm weather, shade the carcass and consider quartering for faster cooling.
6. **Protect from flies** — flies lay eggs within minutes. Cover the meat with cloth or place in a game bag. Smoke deters insects.

!!! warning "CAUTION: game carries disease that enters through your hands"
    Tularemia from rabbits and rodents, brucellosis from boar and elk, and CWD in deer
    and elk all transmit during handling, not only through eating. Glove your hands or
    cover cuts before field dressing, and discard a rabbit whose liver is covered in
    white spots. Full detail, including which animals not to harvest at all, is in
    [Field Butchering](field-butchering.md#disease-and-hygiene).

## Energy Expenditure vs. Caloric Return

This is the critical calculation in a survival situation. Hunting burns enormous calories — often for zero return.

### The Math

| Activity | Calories Burned per Hour | Success Probability | Expected Caloric Return |
|----------|------------------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Stalking deer (improvised weapon) | 200-400 | Very low (under 5%) | High if successful: 25,000-40,000 cal |
| Sitting in ambush | 80-120 | Low-Moderate (5-15%) | Variable |
| Rabbit hunting (throwing stick) | 150-300 | Low (10-20%) | Moderate: 500-800 cal per rabbit |
| Bird hunting (throwing stick/sling) | 150-250 | Low (5-15%) | Low-Moderate: 200-600 cal per bird |
| Setting snares (labor) | 150-250 (one-time) | Moderate (20-40% per trap line/night) | Moderate: 500-800 cal per rabbit |
| Fishing (active, improvised) | 100-200 | Moderate-High (30-60%) | Variable: 200-1,000+ cal per session |
| Fishing (passive, traps/nets) | 200-400 (one-time build) | Moderate-High (40-70% ongoing) | Ongoing: hundreds of calories daily |

### Lean Meat Alone Will Not Sustain You

The calorie counts above assume the calories are usable. Protein alone is not.

The liver disposes of the nitrogen left over from protein by converting it to urea,
and that conversion has a ceiling. Push past it and ammonia and amino acids build up
in the blood faster than they can be cleared. The result is a form of malnutrition
that happens *while you are eating*: diarrhoea, headache, weakness, low blood
pressure, and a hunger that eating more meat does not touch. Discomfort begins within
about a week of an all-lean-meat diet. Death follows in weeks.

This is called **rabbit starvation**, or *mal de caribou* — named for the animals that
cause it most often. It is not a curiosity. It killed Arctic explorers and voyageurs
who had plenty of meat.

**Why small game is the worst offender:**

| Food | Fat per 100 g | Calories from protein |
|------|--------------|----------------------|
| Wild rabbit | 2.3 g | ~78% |
| Squirrel, most small game | 2-4 g | 70-80% |
| Venison (lean cuts) | 1-3 g | 70-80% |
| Beef, moderately fatty | 15-20 g | ~35% |

Keep protein under roughly **35% of your calories**. Every animal in the table above
is far past that on muscle meat alone. A rabbit-only diet is not a thin diet — it is
an actively toxic one.

!!! danger "DANGER: a diet of lean meat and nothing else kills faster than eating nothing"
    You can lose weight steadily on fasting alone and stay clear-headed. A body running
    on nothing but lean protein deteriorates faster than one running on its own fat
    stores, because it must spend energy and water clearing the nitrogen. Given the
    choice between rabbit alone and rabbit plus anything fatty or starchy, the second
    is not an improvement — it is the difference between surviving and not.

**Fixing it — in order of what is usually available:**

1. **Bone marrow.** Crack long bones and eat the marrow. This is the densest fat on a
   lean animal and it is routinely thrown away.
2. **Body-cavity and organ fat.** Kidney fat, the fat lining the gut, fat around the
   heart. Render it and drink it or cook with it.
3. **Fat under the skin.** Pluck birds rather than skinning them — skinning takes the
   fat layer with it.
4. **Fish**, particularly oily species, and any fatty part of a larger animal.
5. **Carbohydrate:** acorns, nuts, tubers, cattail rhizome, inner bark. Starch spares
   protein just as fat does.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Wild animals are leanest in late winter and early spring, having burned their
    reserves through the cold. That is exactly when you are most likely to be
    depending on them. Assume the lean-season animal has almost no fat on it and plan
    to combine it with plant calories.

### When Hunting Is Worth It

- You have a high-probability ambush opportunity (game at close range, moving toward you)
- You are sheltered in place with time and energy to spare
- Large game is present (one deer feeds you for weeks)
- You have an effective weapon (even improvised) and have practiced with it
- Terrain allows close-range encounters (thick forest, narrow valleys)

### When Hunting Is Not Worth It

- You are calorie-depleted and physically weak — a failed hunt worsens your situation
- Only small game is available and terrain makes approach difficult
- You have no effective weapon and no materials to make one
- Open terrain prevents close-range stalking
- Better alternatives exist (fishing, trapping, foraging)

**General rule:** Passive methods (traps, nets, set lines) are almost always more calorie-efficient than active hunting. Set traps and fish first; hunt only when opportunity presents itself or when large game justifies the energy investment.

## Legal and Ethical Framework

### Survival Context

In a genuine survival emergency, preserving your life is the priority. Most legal systems recognize a necessity defense for taking game out of season or without a license when your life is at risk. After rescue:

- Report what you took, when, and where to the relevant wildlife agency
- Cooperate fully with any investigation
- Document your situation if possible (the survival context justifies the take)

### Non-Survival Context

- Obtain all required licenses and permits before hunting
- Follow all season, method, and bag limit regulations
- Use the most humane method available
- Track and retrieve all wounded animals — do not leave them to suffer
- Respect private property and obtain permission
- Follow all firearm/weapon safety regulations

### Ethical Principles (All Contexts)

- Take only what you need
- Use all edible parts of the animal
- Kill cleanly — target vital areas for quick, humane kills
- Do not hunt breeding animals with dependent young when avoidable
- Respect the animal — it is giving its life to sustain yours

## Common Mistakes

- **Hunting when you should be trapping** — active hunting burns calories with low success rates. Set traps first.
- **Ignoring wind direction** — this is the number one reason stalks fail. Check constantly.
- **Moving too fast** — patience is the stalker's primary weapon. Slow down by half, then slow down again.
- **Targeting too-large game with inadequate weapons** — an improvised spear against a deer is a very low-probability gamble. Focus on small game unless you have a clear advantage.
- **Not scouting first** — spend time observing before hunting. Learn the animals' patterns, routes, and timing.
- **Hunting in bedding areas** — you push animals out and they relocate. Hunt travel routes instead.
- **Failing to field dress quickly** — meat spoils within hours in warm weather. Process immediately.
- **Eating nothing but lean meat** — a rabbit-only diet is toxic, not merely
  insufficient. Save the marrow and fat, and pair meat with plant calories.
- **Field dressing bare-handed** — tularemia and brucellosis enter through cuts and
  abrasions on your hands, not through the meat you cook.
- **Underestimating the difficulty of the kill shot** — animals are fast, vital areas are small, and adrenaline ruins accuracy. Practice with your weapon before you depend on it.
