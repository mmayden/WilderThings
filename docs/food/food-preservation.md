# Food Preservation in Survival Situations

> Extend the shelf life of wild game, fish, and foraged food using smoking, drying, salting, and other field-expedient methods.

## At a Glance

- Meat spoils within hours above 40°F (4°C) — preservation must begin immediately
- Smoking and drying are the most reliable field methods with no special materials required
- Thin-cut meat dries fastest and preserves longest — aim for strips 1/4 inch (6 mm) thick or less
- Fat goes rancid quickly and cannot be preserved by drying — render it or use it immediately
- Watch for signs of spoilage: off smell, slimy texture, discoloration, mold (except on intentionally fermented foods)

## Smoking

Smoking preserves meat through dehydration, antimicrobial compounds in the smoke, and a protective surface coating. Two methods exist with very different purposes.

### Hot Smoking

Hot smoking cooks and partially preserves the meat simultaneously. Target 165-200°F (74-93°C) at the meat.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Meat held between 40°F and 140°F (4-60°C) is in the range where bacteria multiply fastest. Older instructions for hot smoking start at 125°F (52°C), which sits inside that range — meat can spend hours there growing bacteria while appearing to cook. Keep the smoker hot enough that the thickest part of the meat reaches at least 160°F (71°C), and get it there within a few hours rather than slowly.

**Setup:**

1. Build a small fire in a pit or contained area.
2. Let it burn down to coals. Add green wood or wood chips for smoke — hickory, oak, apple, or mesquite. Avoid resinous woods (pine, spruce, cedar) — they produce bitter, toxic creosote.
3. Build a rack of green sticks 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) above the coals.
4. Lay thin strips of meat across the rack.
5. Maintain steady smoke for 4-8 hours. Meat should be dark, dry on the surface, and cooked through.

**Shelf life:** 3-7 days without refrigeration. Longer in dry, cool conditions.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Resinous wood smoke (pine, spruce, fir) deposits creosote on meat, which is toxic and gives a harsh chemical taste. Use only hardwoods and fruitwoods.

### Cold Smoking

Cold smoking dehydrates and flavors without cooking. Temperature: below 90°F (32°C) at the meat.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Cold smoking does not cook the meat and does not by itself make it safe. The whole process happens at temperatures where bacteria grow. Traditionally, cold smoking is **always** paired with a salt cure done first — the cure, not the smoke, is what preserves the meat. Salt-cure before cold smoking, never cold smoke alone.

**Setup:**

1. Dig a fire pit and a separate smoking chamber connected by a trench or tunnel 6-10 ft (2-3 m) long.
2. Build the fire in the pit. The tunnel cools the smoke before it reaches the meat.
3. Cover the smoking chamber — a framework of sticks covered with bark, cloth, or a tarp works. Leave a small vent at the top.
4. Hang or rack meat strips inside the chamber.
5. Maintain smoke for 12-48 hours, adding fuel as needed.

**Shelf life (salt-cured first, then cold smoked):** 1-4 weeks depending on humidity and temperature; months with a heavy cure.

=== "Improvised Smokehouse (Tripod)"
    Lash three poles into a tripod. Wrap the sides with bark, large leaves, or a poncho, leaving the bottom open over the fire pit and the top slightly vented. Hang meat from cross-sticks inside.

=== "Improvised Smokehouse (Trench)"
    Dig a trench with a fire pit at one end and a covered chamber at the other. The trench acts as a natural cooling tunnel for cold smoking.

## Drying (Jerky)

Drying removes moisture that bacteria need to grow. The target is meat that bends without snapping (still slightly pliable) but shows no moisture when twisted.

**Method:**

1. Cut meat into strips no thicker than 1/4 inch (6 mm). Cut with the grain for chewy jerky; against the grain for brittle jerky.
2. Remove all visible fat — fat does not dry well and turns rancid.
3. Hang strips on a rack in direct sunlight and wind. Protect from flies with a thin cloth if available.
4. Dry for 1-3 days depending on humidity and temperature. Bring inside or cover at night to avoid reabsorbing moisture.
5. Alternatively, hang strips near (not over) a fire for faster drying — 6-12 hours.

**Shelf life:** 1-2 weeks in humid conditions. Several months in dry climates.

!!! tip
    Slice partially frozen meat for the thinnest, most even strips. If no freezing is possible, slice against a flat cutting surface and press the knife almost parallel to the surface.

## Salt Curing

Salt draws moisture from meat through osmosis and creates an environment hostile to bacteria.

**Dry cure:**

1. Cover all surfaces of the meat in a thick layer of salt — 1/2 inch (12 mm) minimum.
2. Stack pieces in a cool, shaded area with salt between each layer.
3. Allow 7-14 days, repacking with fresh salt if the existing salt becomes wet.
4. Rinse the surface and hang to dry.

**Brine cure:**

1. Dissolve salt in water until a fresh egg or small potato floats — roughly 1 cup salt per 1 quart (240 g per liter).
2. Submerge meat in the brine for 3-7 days.
3. Remove, rinse, and hang to air-dry.

!!! note
    Salt sources in the wild: seawater (boil down), salt licks, mineral deposits near springs, plant ash (contains potassium salts — less effective but usable). Seawater yields roughly 1 oz (28 g) of salt per quart (liter) when evaporated.

## Pemmican

Pemmican is the ultimate survival food — calorie-dense, shelf-stable, and nutritionally complete. It sustained indigenous peoples and fur traders for centuries.

**Ingredients:**

- Dried meat (jerky), ground to powder
- Rendered fat (tallow or lard)
- Optional: dried berries, dried crushed nuts

**Method:**

1. Grind dried jerky into powder or fine shreds. Use a rock on a flat surface if no tools are available.
2. Render fat: chop raw fat into small pieces, heat slowly over low fire until liquid. Strain out solid cracklings.
3. Mix powdered meat and melted fat in roughly equal proportions by volume.
4. Add crushed dried berries if available — roughly 10-15% of total volume.
5. Press into cakes or pack into intestine casing, bark containers, or hide pouches.
6. Allow to cool and solidify.

**Shelf life:** Months to years if kept dry and cool. The fat seals out moisture and air.

**Calories:** Approximately 130-150 per ounce (28 g). A fist-sized cake provides 600+ calories.

## Fat Rendering

Fat is the most calorie-dense food you can obtain. Render it for preservation and use.

1. Cut raw fat (from around kidneys, along the back, or under the skin) into small pieces — the smaller, the faster the render.
2. Heat slowly in any container over low fire. Do not boil — keep the temperature below the smoke point.
3. The fat liquefies; solid tissue (cracklings) sinks or floats.
4. Strain through cloth into a container.
5. Cool. Rendered fat solidifies into tallow (from ruminants) or lard (from non-ruminants).

**Shelf life:** 1-2 weeks unrefrigerated. Longer if stored in a cool cache and kept sealed.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Rancid fat smells sour or like paint. Do not eat rancid fat — it causes nausea, diarrhea, and can irritate the entire digestive tract. Smell-test fat before every use.

## Root Cellaring and Caching

Store fruits, roots, and meat in cool underground caches.

**Basic cache:**

1. Dig a hole 2-3 ft (60-90 cm) deep in shaded ground. Below ground temperature is cooler and more stable.
2. Line with bark, large leaves, or stones to keep food off bare soil.
3. Place food inside. Wrap meat in clean cloth or large leaves.
4. Cover with a thick layer of leaves or grass, then a layer of soil or bark.
5. Mark the location clearly.

**Temperature guide:** Below 2 ft (60 cm), ground temperature stays roughly 50-55°F (10-13°C) in summer, which slows spoilage significantly.

!!! tip
    Elevate meat caches out of reach of predators when possible — suspend from a branch or store on a high platform. Bear hangs (12 ft / 3.7 m up, 6 ft / 1.8 m from the trunk) protect against large animals — see [Bear Safety](../wildlife/bear-safety.md).

## Fermentation

Controlled fermentation preserves food through acid production by beneficial bacteria.

!!! danger "DANGER: do not ferment fish or meat in a sealed container"

    Nearly half of all foodborne botulism cases in the United States occur in
    Alaska, almost entirely from traditionally fermented fish and marine mammal
    products. Between 2000 and 2007 the rate there ran hundreds of times the
    national average.

    The risk rose specifically when people moved from porous, grass-lined ground
    pits to sealed plastic buckets, jars, and bags. Sealing the container is
    what creates the oxygen-free environment *Clostridium botulinum* needs —
    and roughly a third of preparers now use plastic containers.

    You cannot detect this by smell, taste, or appearance. **Botulinum toxin is
    odorless and tasteless**, and correctly fermented fish is supposed to smell
    powerfully strong — so the usual spoilage checks tell you nothing here.
    Botulism causes descending paralysis and respiratory failure, and the
    treatment is antitoxin plus possible mechanical ventilation. There is no
    field treatment.

    Do not attempt fish or meat fermentation in a survival situation. Dry it,
    smoke it, or salt-cure it instead — all three are covered above, all are
    more reliable, and none carry this risk.

**Vegetable fermentation** is a different proposition and is comparatively safe. Lactic acid bacteria rapidly drop the pH below 4.6, which is the threshold that prevents *C. botulinum* from producing toxin. Meat and fish have no such acid defence.

1. Pack wild greens, roots, or vegetables into a container with 2-3% salt by weight.
2. Submerge fully in liquid (the vegetables' own juice or added water). Anything above the brine line will mold.
3. Cover **loosely** — fermentation produces gas, which must escape. Do not seal airtight.
4. Keep in a cool place for 3-7 days.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Correct vegetable fermentation smells sharply acidic and tangy, and the brine turns cloudy. Discard it if it smells putrid or rotten rather than sour, if it turns slimy or ropy, or if it grows black, pink, or fuzzy mold. A thin white film on the surface is usually harmless yeast and can be skimmed off.

## Signs of Spoilage

| Sign | Meaning | Action |
|------|---------|--------|
| Sour or ammonia smell | Bacterial decomposition | Discard |
| Slimy or sticky surface | Bacterial growth | Discard |
| Green, black, or white mold | Fungal growth | Trim 1 inch (2.5 cm) beyond visible mold on dry-cured meat only. Discard all others. |
| Off-color (gray, green tint) | Oxidation or bacteria | Discard |
| Gas bubbles in sealed container | Anaerobic bacterial growth | Discard — possible botulism |

## Common Mistakes

- **Leaving fat on jerky** — fat does not dry and turns rancid, spoiling the entire batch
- **Smoking with resinous wood** — pine, spruce, and fir deposit toxic creosote on food
- **Insufficient drying time** — meat that feels dry on the surface may still be moist inside. Twist a strip — if moisture appears, continue drying.
- **Storing preserved food in humid, warm conditions** — heat and moisture reverse preservation. Cache in cool, dry, shaded locations.
- **Fermenting fish or meat in a sealed container** — this is how people get botulism. The toxin is odorless, so no smell test will catch it. Dry, smoke, or salt-cure instead.
- **Ignoring the smell test** — trust your nose for ordinary spoilage (it will not detect botulinum toxin). If food smells wrong, it is wrong. The caloric cost of discarding suspect food is always less than the cost of foodborne illness in a survival situation.

## Quick Reference

| Method | Time Required | Shelf Life | Materials Needed |
|--------|--------------|------------|-----------------|
| Hot smoking | 4-8 hours | 3-7 days | Fire, hardwood, rack |
| Cold smoking (after salt cure) | 12-48 hours | 1-4 weeks | Fire pit, tunnel, chamber, salt |
| Sun drying (jerky) | 1-3 days | 2 weeks-months | Rack, sun, wind |
| Salt curing | 7-14 days | Weeks-months | Salt |
| Pemmican | 2-3 hours (after drying) | Months-years | Dried meat, rendered fat |
| Fermentation (vegetables only) | 3-7 days | Weeks | Salt, loosely covered container |
| Root cellar cache | 1 hour to build | Extends life 2-4x | Digging tool |
