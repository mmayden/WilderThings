# Signaling for Rescue

> Make yourself big, bright, and unmistakable — rescuers cannot save what they cannot find.

## At a Glance

- **Three of anything means distress.** Three fires, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes. This is the universal pattern.
- **Signal mirrors are the single most effective non-electronic signaling device**, reaching roughly 10 mi / 16 km in ordinary conditions and up to 50 mi / 80 km in exceptionally clear air.
- **Electronic beacons (PLB/EPIRB) provide the highest probability of rescue.** Carry one and know how to activate it.
- **Contrast is everything.** Use colors, shapes, and movement that do not exist naturally in your environment.
- **Signal continuously and on schedule.** Rescuers search in patterns and time windows — your signal must be active when they pass overhead.

## Ground-to-Air Signals

### International Ground-to-Air Emergency Symbols

Construct these symbols at least 10 ft / 3 m tall per character, with proportions of 6:1 (length to width). Use materials that contrast sharply with the ground — dark rocks on snow, light fabric on dark earth, trampled paths in tall grass.

**Current ICAO standard (Annex 12).** These five are what aircrew are trained on worldwide — use them by preference:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **V** | Require assistance |
| **X** | Require medical assistance |
| **N** | No / negative |
| **Y** | Yes / affirmative |
| &#8594; (Arrow) | Proceeding in this direction |

**Legacy extended code.** These appear in older survival manuals and are still widely printed, but they are not part of the current ICAO set — a given aircrew may or may not know them. Use them only in addition to a **V** or **X**, never instead of one:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **F** | Need food and water |
| **I** | Require doctor — serious injury |
| **II** | Require medical supplies |
| **LL** | All is well |
| **II** | Need supplies |
| **LL** | All is well |

### SOS Layout

Spell SOS in letters large enough to read from 1,000 ft / 305 m altitude. Each letter should be at least 10 ft / 3 m high and 3 ft / 1 m wide. Use trenches, rocks, logs, or any high-contrast material. Maintain sharp, straight edges — nature does not produce straight lines, so searchers notice them immediately.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Signals that are too small, too low-contrast, or placed under tree canopy are invisible from the air. Always construct signals in the largest clearing available.

### Panel Signals

If you have brightly colored fabric, a tarp, an emergency blanket, or even a tent rainfly, spread it flat in an open area. International orange, red, and bright yellow are the most visible colors from the air. Anchor panels securely — a signal that blows away is worse than no signal, because you have lost the material.

Arrange panel signals in geometric shapes (triangle, cross, straight line) rather than random piles. Geometric shapes register as artificial and attract attention.

## Signal Mirrors

A signal mirror is the most underrated survival signaling tool. On a clear day, the flash from a 3 x 5 in / 7.5 x 12.5 cm glass mirror carries roughly 10 mi / 16 km, and up to 50 mi / 80 km when the air is exceptionally clear. Even a improvised reflector — a CD, phone screen, belt buckle, or aluminum wrapper — produces a visible flash at several miles.

### Aiming Technique (Sighting Method)

You cannot see your own flash hitting a distant target. Use this method:

1. Hold the mirror close to your face, just below one eye.
2. Extend your free hand to full arm's length toward the target (aircraft, ship, distant ridge).
3. Form a **peace sign (V)** with your index and middle fingers, placing the target between them.
4. Tilt the mirror until the reflected sun spot falls on your extended fingers.
5. Slowly sweep the flash across and through the V, bracketing the target.

The reflected beam spreads over distance, so even imprecise aiming produces a visible flash at range. Sweep continuously — a moving flash is far more noticeable than a static one.

### When No Target Is Visible

Flash the horizon in a slow, continuous sweep. Aircraft may be beyond visual range but within mirror-flash range. Sweep 360 degrees every few minutes during daylight hours. This "blind flashing" has resulted in documented rescues where the survivor never saw the aircraft that spotted them.

## Signal Fires

Fire is visible by day (smoke) and night (flame). It is one of the oldest and most reliable signaling methods.

### Three Fires in a Triangle

The international distress signal is **three fires arranged in an equilateral triangle**, spaced approximately 100 ft / 30 m apart. Prepare all three fire sites in advance with tinder, kindling, and fuel so you can ignite them rapidly when you hear or see a search aircraft.

If three fires are not feasible, build one large fire in the most open, visible location available.

### Smoke Generation

During daylight, **smoke is more visible than flame**. Maximize smoke production:

- **White smoke** (best against dark backgrounds like forests): Add green vegetation, fresh leaves, or damp moss to a hot fire.
- **Black smoke** (best against light backgrounds like snow, sand, or overcast sky): Add rubber, plastic, oil-soaked materials, or petroleum products if available.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Burning plastics and rubber produces toxic fumes. Stand upwind and avoid inhaling smoke directly.

Build the hottest base fire you can first, then add smoke-generating materials on top. A smoldering, weak fire produces thin, low smoke that dissipates quickly. A hot fire with green material piled on top produces a dense, rising column visible for miles.

### Platform Fires for Wet Ground

In swamps, snow, or saturated terrain, fire on the ground surface will extinguish itself. Build a platform:

1. Lay a base of green logs or thick branches in a grid pattern, 3-4 ft / 1-1.2 m square.
2. Stack a second layer perpendicular to the first.
3. Build your fire on top of the platform.

The platform insulates the fire from moisture and elevates it for better visibility. In deep snow, the platform also prevents the fire from melting downward and disappearing into a pit.

## Whistle Signals

Three short blasts on a whistle is the universal auditory distress signal. Repeat every few minutes when you believe searchers may be in range.

A whistle carries farther than the human voice (up to 1 mi / 1.6 km in open terrain vs. 600 ft / 183 m for shouting), requires far less energy, and does not cause throat damage from prolonged use.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not waste energy shouting. A whistle is louder, travels farther, and costs almost no calories. Carry one on your person at all times in the backcountry.

If you do not have a whistle, bang rocks together, strike a tree with a stick, or use any method that produces a sharp, repetitive sound in groups of three.

## Bright Clothing and Markers

Wear or display the brightest colors available. Prioritize:

1. **International orange** — the single most visible color against natural backgrounds
2. **Red**
3. **Bright yellow**

Hang brightly colored items from tree branches at the edge of clearings where they can catch wind and move. Movement attracts the eye far more effectively than static color.

Create trail markers if you move from your initial position: strips of fabric tied to branches, arrows made of rocks, or scratches on tree bark. Mark your direction of travel and the date if possible. Search teams follow these markers.

### Improvised Markers

- Tie clothing to a long pole and wave it when aircraft approach.
- Stomp large arrows or letters into snow, mud, or sand.
- Arrange rocks or logs in unnatural geometric patterns in clearings.

## Electronic Signaling

### Personal Locator Beacons (PLB)

A PLB transmits a 406 MHz distress signal to the COSPAS-SARSAT satellite system, which relays your GPS coordinates to rescue authorities. Activation-to-alert time is typically 5-15 minutes.

- Register your PLB before you need it (in the U.S.: beaconregistration.noaa.gov).
- Test it according to the manufacturer's schedule.
- Carry it on your person, not in your pack — if you are separated from your gear, the beacon goes with you.
- Activate it only in genuine emergencies. False activations waste SAR resources and may result in penalties.

### EPIRBs (Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacons)

EPIRBs function identically to PLBs but are designed for maritime use. Many models activate automatically upon immersion in water. If you are in a maritime survival situation, ensure the EPIRB is deployed, antenna is upright, and the unit is not submerged.

### Satellite Messengers

Devices such as the Garmin inReach, SPOT, and Zoleo provide two-way messaging and SOS capability via satellite networks. Advantages over PLBs:

- Two-way communication allows you to describe your situation and receive instructions.
- Tracking features let others monitor your position in real time.
- Non-emergency messaging reduces unnecessary SAR activations.

Carry charged batteries or a backup power source. Cold temperatures reduce battery life by 30-50% — keep the device inside your clothing in cold weather.

### Cell Phone Tips

Cell phones are unreliable in the backcountry but may work in some situations:

- **Text before you call.** SMS requires less signal strength and a shorter connection window than voice. A text may transmit where a call cannot connect.
- **Climb for signal.** Gain elevation. Move to ridgelines, hilltops, or open terrain. Even 100 ft / 30 m of elevation gain can make the difference.
- **Conserve battery.** Switch to airplane mode when not actively attempting to signal. Disable Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and background apps. Reduce screen brightness to minimum.
- **Dial emergency services (911 / 112).** Emergency calls can connect through any available carrier network, not just your own.
- **Leave a voicemail.** If the call connects briefly and drops, the voicemail may still record your message and location.
- **Try the emergency call even with zero bars.** On a recent iPhone, Pixel, or Galaxy the
  phone falls back to satellite by itself when no tower answers. It needs a clear view of
  the sky, so step into the open first — see
  [Satellite SOS Built Into Your Phone](gps-and-electronics.md#satellite-sos-built-into-your-phone).

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Do not plan around a cell phone as your primary signaling device. Satellite SOS has
    made phones far more useful than they were, but it depends on your model, your
    battery, and a clear sky. A PLB or satellite messenger has none of those dependencies
    and remains the right primary electronic signal on a serious trip.

## Improvised Reflectors and Flags

When purpose-built signaling tools are unavailable, improvise:

| Material | Use |
|---|---|
| CD/DVD | Signal mirror — use sighting technique |
| Aluminum foil / wrapper | Crumple loosely around a stick for a waving reflector |
| Glass or clear plastic | Angle toward the sun to produce a flash |
| Phone or watch screen | Use as an improvised mirror |
| Belt buckle, knife blade | Short-range reflector, effective to 1-3 mi / 1.6-5 km |
| Garbage bag (clear or white) | Spread flat as a ground panel or tie to a pole as a flag |
| Space blanket / emergency blanket | Extremely reflective; spread flat or hang vertically |

For flags, attach material to the longest available pole. Elevation and movement are key. A flag waving 15 ft / 4.5 m above ground level is visible at far greater distances than one at waist height.

## When to Signal vs. When to Self-Rescue

This decision can determine whether you live or die. Apply these criteria:

### Stay and Signal When:

- Someone knows your itinerary and expected return time.
- You are near your last known position or planned route.
- You are injured and movement risks worsening your condition.
- You have adequate shelter, water, and signaling resources.
- Terrain between you and help is unknown, hazardous, or impassable.

### Self-Rescue When:

- No one knows where you are or when to expect you.
- You are certain of the direction to help and the distance is manageable.
- Your current location is dangerous (rising water, fire, rockfall zone).
- You have been signaling for an extended period with no response and conditions are deteriorating.
- You have the physical ability, navigation tools, and supplies to travel safely.

!!! danger "WARNING"
    If you choose to self-rescue, leave clear markers at your departure point indicating your direction of travel, the date and time, your condition, and your intended destination. Search teams may arrive after you leave.

## Night Signaling

### Fire

Fire is the dominant night signal. A single bright fire is visible up to 20 mi / 32 km on flat terrain in clear conditions. Maintain three fires in a triangle pattern if possible. Keep fuel stacked and ready to build up the flames rapidly when you detect a potential rescuer.

### Flashlight SOS

Flash SOS in Morse code using any light source:

- **S:** Three short flashes (dot-dot-dot)
- **O:** Three long flashes (dash-dash-dash)
- **S:** Three short flashes (dot-dot-dot)

Pause, then repeat. "Short" is approximately 1 second on. "Long" is approximately 3 seconds on. Pause 3 seconds between letters and 7 seconds between repetitions.

Aim the flashlight toward likely search areas: trails, roads, ridgelines, or the direction of the nearest settlement.

### Chemical Lights (Chemlights / Glow Sticks)

Chemlights are visible at 1-2 mi / 1.6-3.2 km in darkness. Tie one to a length of cordage (3 ft / 1 m) and swing it in a circle overhead. The moving circle of light is highly visible and immediately identifiable as a human-made signal.

Attach chemlights to your shelter, your signal panel, or an elevated pole to mark your location passively through the night.

## Aviation Search Patterns

Understanding how search aircraft operate helps you position yourself for detection.

### Common Search Patterns

- **Parallel track (creeping line):** The aircraft flies back and forth in parallel lines across the search area. Position yourself in open terrain along the likely search corridor.
- **Expanding square:** The aircraft spirals outward from a central point (usually your last known position). Stay as close to your last known position as feasible.
- **Sector search:** The aircraft flies outward from a point along different compass headings. Open ground near the center of the search area is optimal.

### How to Be Visible

- **Stand in the open.** Never signal from under tree canopy. Move to clearings, riverbanks, ridgelines, or shorelines.
- **Create contrast.** Wear or display colors that oppose the background. Orange on green, dark on snow, light on dark rock.
- **Move.** Wave both arms overhead in a slow, deliberate motion. A moving figure is far more detectable than a stationary one.
- **Lie flat and spread out** if wearing high-contrast clothing on low-contrast ground — this maximizes your visible surface area from above.
- **Shadow matters.** At low sun angles, your shadow is longer than your body. Position yourself where your shadow falls on open, light-colored ground for additional contrast.

### Signaling an Aircraft Directly

When an aircraft approaches:

1. Face the aircraft.
2. Raise both arms overhead in a **Y shape** — this is the international "Yes, I need help" signal.
3. If you do not need help (e.g., you are signaling for someone else nearby), raise one arm and extend the other downward, forming a diagonal line — this means "No, do not land here."
4. Flash your signal mirror directly at the cockpit.
5. Deploy smoke if available — smoke gives the pilot wind direction for landing and visually marks your exact position.

Do not assume the aircraft has seen you on the first pass. Continue signaling through every pass until you receive a definitive acknowledgment (wing waggle, circle pattern, or radio contact).

## Common Mistakes

- **Building signals under tree cover.** Canopy blocks ground signals entirely from aerial view. Always signal from the largest clearing available.
- **Relying on a single signal type.** Combine methods: mirror + fire + ground symbol + whistle. Redundancy saves lives.
- **Signaling only when you hear an aircraft.** Prepare all signals in advance. By the time you hear an engine, you have seconds, not minutes.
- **Using low-contrast materials.** Gray rocks on gray soil are invisible from altitude. Prioritize contrast above all else.
- **Letting fires go out.** Maintain signal fires or keep ignition materials immediately at hand at all times. A dead fire signals nothing.
- **Forgetting to signal at night.** Many searches continue after dark with night-vision equipment or thermal imaging. Keep fire burning and light signals active.
- **Draining electronic device batteries.** Activate your PLB and then conserve all other battery power. Do not play with your phone.
