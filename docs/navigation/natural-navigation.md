# Natural Navigation

> Direction-finding without instruments using the sun, stars, moon, terrain, vegetation, and environmental patterns.

## At a Glance

- The shadow stick method gives a reliable east-west line anywhere the sun is visible
- Polaris gives true north within about 1-2 degrees; the Southern Cross gives south within about 3-5 degrees
- Moss on trees is not a reliable direction indicator — tree growth asymmetry caused by prevailing wind and sun exposure is
- No single natural method is accurate enough alone — combine at least two methods and cross-check
- Terrain association (rivers flow downhill, drainages merge) is the most practical way to find civilization

## Sun-Based Methods

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. At solar noon it is due south in the Northern Hemisphere and due north in the Southern Hemisphere. These facts power all sun-based navigation.

!!! note
    Near the equator (between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn), the sun passes directly overhead and may be north or south depending on the season. Sun methods are less reliable in the tropics.

### Shadow Stick — Equal-Length Method

This method takes 2-3 hours but gives a true east-west line.

1. Place a straight stick 3 feet (1 m) tall vertically in flat, open ground.
2. Mark the tip of the shadow with a stone or scratch.
3. Wait 2-3 hours. The shadow tip moves in an arc.
4. Mark the new shadow tip position.
5. Draw a straight line between the two marks. This line runs approximately east-west. The first mark is west, the second is east — shadow tips travel west to east in **both** hemispheres, because the sun always tracks east to west.
6. A perpendicular line gives you a north-south line. Which end is north does depend on hemisphere — in the Northern Hemisphere, north is the side away from the midday sun.

### Shadow Stick — Shortest Shadow Method

This method takes most of a day but is more accurate.

1. Set up the stick before midday.
2. Mark the shadow tip every 15-20 minutes from mid-morning through mid-afternoon.
3. The shortest shadow points true north in the Northern Hemisphere (true south in the Southern Hemisphere).
4. Connect the arc of shadow tips to get an accurate east-west line.

### Watch Method

=== "Northern Hemisphere"
    1. Hold an analog watch (or visualize a clock face using the current time) flat with the hour hand pointing at the sun.
    2. The midpoint between the hour hand and the 12 o'clock position is approximately south.
    3. Before 12:00, bisect the angle clockwise from the hour hand to 12. After 12:00, bisect counterclockwise.

=== "Southern Hemisphere"
    1. Point the 12 o'clock position at the sun.
    2. The midpoint between the 12 and the hour hand is approximately north.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    The watch method gives rough direction only — expect 15-20 degrees of error. It degrades further at high latitudes and near the equator. Use it as a quick check, not primary navigation.

If using a digital watch, draw a clock face on the ground with the current time and apply the same technique.

## Star Navigation

### Northern Hemisphere — Polaris (the North Star)

Polaris sits within 1 degree of true north. Find it with these methods:

**Using the Big Dipper (Ursa Major):**

1. Locate the Big Dipper — seven bright stars forming a ladle shape.
2. Find the two "pointer stars" at the outer edge of the dipper's bowl (Dubhe and Merak).
3. Extend a line from Merak through Dubhe approximately five times the distance between them.
4. That line hits Polaris, which is the last star in the handle of the Little Dipper (Ursa Minor).

**Using Cassiopeia:**

1. Locate Cassiopeia — five bright stars forming a W or M shape.
2. Polaris is roughly between the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia, at a similar distance from each.
3. Useful when the Big Dipper is below the horizon or obscured by trees.

### Southern Hemisphere — The Southern Cross

There is no bright star at the south celestial pole. Use the Southern Cross (Crux) to find due south.

1. Locate the Southern Cross — four bright stars forming a cross shape, with a fifth dimmer star offset.
2. Extend the long axis of the cross (from the top star through the bottom star) 4.5 times its length.
3. That point is approximately the south celestial pole.
4. Drop a vertical line from that point to the horizon. Where it meets the horizon is due south.

!!! tip
    Distinguish the Southern Cross from the False Cross (which is larger and dimmer) by looking for the two bright "pointer stars" (Alpha and Beta Centauri) to the left of the true Southern Cross.

## Moon Direction Indicators

The moon provides rough directional information:

- **Crescent moon:** Draw an imaginary line through the two tips (horns) of the crescent down to the horizon. In the Northern Hemisphere, this point is approximately south. In the Southern Hemisphere, it indicates approximately north.
- **Full moon:** Rises in the east at sunset, is due south (Northern Hemisphere) or due north (Southern Hemisphere) at midnight, and sets in the west at sunrise.
- **First quarter moon (right half lit, Northern Hemisphere):** Due south at sunset.
- **Last quarter moon (left half lit, Northern Hemisphere):** Due south at sunrise.

!!! note
    Moon methods give rough bearings only — within 20-30 degrees. Combine with other methods.

## Prevailing Wind Indicators

=== "North America"
    Prevailing winds blow from the west/southwest across most of the continental US and southern Canada. In the Arctic, expect winds from the north and northeast. Coastal areas have onshore-offshore wind cycles that override prevailing patterns.

=== "Europe"
    Westerly winds dominate from the Atlantic. In Scandinavia, expect northwest prevailing winds. The Mediterranean has complex local wind patterns (mistral, sirocco).

=== "Southern Hemisphere"
    Prevailing winds blow from the west/northwest across temperate zones (Roaring Forties). In Australia, the southeast trade winds dominate the northern half.

Wind-shaped trees (flagging) lean away from the prevailing wind. Snow cornices form on the leeward (downwind) side of ridges. Sand dunes migrate downwind. Use these as directional clues, not precise bearings.

## Vegetation Patterns

### The Moss Myth

Moss grows on all sides of trees. It prefers moisture and shade, not north-facing surfaces exclusively. In dense forest, moss distribution tells you nothing reliable about direction. Do not use moss for navigation.

### Tree Growth Asymmetry (Reliable)

In open areas with consistent wind or sun exposure, trees show directional growth patterns:

- **Sun-favored growth:** In the Northern Hemisphere, trees in open areas grow fuller and have more branches on the south-facing side (more sunlight). The reverse is true in the Southern Hemisphere.
- **Wind shaping:** Isolated trees in exposed areas lean away from the prevailing wind. Branches on the windward side are shorter and stunted.
- **Bark differences:** On some species (birch, beech), bark is thicker and more weathered on the windward side.

!!! warning "CAUTION"
    Vegetation indicators require open-grown, isolated trees. In dense forest, microclimates override directional patterns. Do not use vegetation indicators in thick canopy.

## Snow Melt Patterns

In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing slopes receive more direct sunlight. Snow melts faster on south-facing slopes and on the south sides of rocks, trees, and ridges. In the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes melt first.

Look for:

- Uneven snow line on ridges — the bare side faces the equator
- Snow melted away from the south side of tree trunks (Northern Hemisphere)
- South-facing rock faces cleared of snow first

These patterns are reliable in winter and spring in temperate latitudes above 35 degrees.

## Animal Behavior Indicators

Animal behavior gives general clues, not precise bearings.

- **Birds:** Many migratory species fly south in autumn and north in spring. Geese and other waterfowl follow flyways tied to major river systems.
- **Spiderwebs:** In open areas, spiders tend to build webs on the south side of trees and structures (Northern Hemisphere) for warmth. Unreliable in dense forest.
- **Ant mounds:** In some temperate regions, ant mounds tend to be on the south/southwest side of trees. The correlation is weak — use only as a supporting clue.

!!! note
    Animal indicators are the least reliable natural navigation method. Never use them as a primary method.

## River and Drainage Following

Rivers and streams flow downhill and merge into larger waterways. Larger waterways lead to civilization in most inhabited regions. This makes drainage following the most practical method for reaching help when lost.

1. Find any stream and follow it downstream.
2. Streams merge into larger rivers. Stay with the largest waterway.
3. In most inhabited areas, rivers lead to roads, bridges, towns, or other infrastructure within 5-30 miles (8-48 km).

!!! danger "WARNING"
    Do not follow rivers through canyons or gorges — the terrain may become impassable with cliff bands, waterfalls, and swift water. If the drainage narrows into a canyon, climb out and contour above it.

## Terrain Association

Terrain association uses the shape of the land to determine location and direction.

- **Ridgelines** are natural travel corridors with better visibility.
- **Valleys** contain water and easier footing but limit visibility.
- **Saddles** (low points between peaks) are natural pass points.
- **Drainages** form predictable patterns: small gullies merge into creeks, creeks into rivers.
- **Elevation changes** confirm direction of travel against known terrain.

Compare what you see around you with what the map shows. Match at least three terrain features to confirm your position.

## Cultural Indicators

In non-wilderness areas, human infrastructure reveals direction and proximity to civilization.

- **Satellite dishes:** In North America and Europe, most point roughly south (toward geostationary satellites on the equatorial arc).
- **Power lines:** Follow them to reach roads and towns. Higher voltage lines (taller towers, more wires) connect larger population centers.
- **Roads and trails:** Follow them downhill or toward wider/better-maintained surfaces.
- **Church architecture:** In traditional European churches, the altar faces east. The main entrance faces west.
- **Aircraft contrails:** Major flight routes follow known corridors. Frequent parallel contrails indicate a major air route, often between cities.

## Combining Methods for Accuracy

No single natural method is precise enough for reliable navigation. Use this approach:

1. Establish a rough direction with the best available method (shadow stick or stars).
2. Cross-check with a second independent method (terrain association, wind indicators, cultural features).
3. If the two methods disagree by more than 30 degrees, add a third method.
4. Favor the method with the strongest physical basis (shadow stick and stars over vegetation and animal behavior).
5. Reassess periodically — conditions change as you travel.

## Common Mistakes

- **Relying on moss for direction.** Moss grows where moisture exists, regardless of compass bearing. It is not a reliable indicator.
- **Using a single method without cross-checking.** Every natural method has error margins of 10-30 degrees. One method alone causes dangerous drift over distance.
- **Applying Northern Hemisphere rules in the Southern Hemisphere.** Sun, shadow, snow melt, and vegetation patterns reverse. Confirm which hemisphere you are in before interpreting indicators.
- **Following rivers blindly into canyons.** Always assess downstream terrain before committing to drainage following. Climb to a vantage point first if possible.
- **Trusting the watch method as precise navigation.** The watch method gives rough direction for orientation, not a bearing accurate enough to navigate to a specific point.
- **Ignoring cultural indicators.** A power line or road is a faster route to help than any wilderness navigation technique.
